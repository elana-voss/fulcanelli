# The Great Work in Plain Terms

## 1. What the book promises and what it withholds

Fulcanelli says the stylobate of Notre-Dame de Paris gives two things: the name of the matter and the sequence of the Work, «le nom lapidaire du sujet des Sages» (*the lapidary name of the subject of the Sages*) and «le travail de l'Elixir, depuis sa calcination première jusqu'à son ultime coction» (*the work of the Elixir, from its first calcination to its last coction*) [PARIS.2 ¶2]. He claims his science is exact and experimental: «La science que nous étudions est aussi positive, aussi réelle, aussi exacte que l'optique, la géométrie ou la mécanique ; ses résultats aussi tangibles que ceux de la chimie.» (*The science we study is as positive, as real, as exact as optics, geometry or mechanics; its results as tangible as those of chemistry.*) [PARIS.7 ¶29]. He also says, more than once, that he stops short of certain points by oath, and that where he cannot go on he prefers silence to a misleading hint: «nous avons préféré le silence aux allusions décevantes, le mutisme à l'abus de confiance» (*we have preferred silence to deceptive allusions, muteness to breach of trust*) [AMIENS ¶21]. He states that he has written no lie: «nous ne pensons pas qu'on nous reprochera jamais d'avoir écrit un seul mensonge» (*we do not think we shall ever be reproached with having written a single lie*) [AMIENS ¶21].

This page sets out, in the order of the Work as far as the text gives it, what the readings of the monuments yield as laboratory practice. Each point is marked **stated** (the text says it) or **interpreted** (my inference, with the passages it rests on). Quoted authors (Philalèthe, the Cosmopolite, Basile Valentin, Trévisan and others) are cited by Fulcanelli as witnesses; where he adopts, qualifies or rejects what they say, this is noted. Canseliet's prefaces are kept for the last section.

## 2. The matter: what it is called and how it is described

**Stated.** The black Virgins of the crypts represent the raw matter as it comes out of the mine: «C'est la matière première à l'état de minerai, telle qu'elle sort des gîtes métallifères, profondément enfouie sous la masse rocheuse.» (*It is the first matter in the state of ore, as it comes out of the metal-bearing deposits, deeply buried under the rocky mass.*) [INTRO.8 ¶4]. He quotes "the texts" for its look: «une substance noire, pesante, cassante, friable, qui a l'aspect d'une pierre et se peut broyer en menus morceaux à la façon d'une pierre» (*a black substance, heavy, brittle, friable, which has the look of a stone and can be crushed into small pieces like a stone*) [INTRO.8 ¶4].

**Stated.** It belongs to the mineral kingdom alone. He cites the commentator of the *Lumière sortant des Ténèbres*: «c'est donc dans le règne minéral seulement, où réside la semence métallique, que nous devons chercher le sujet propre à notre art» (*it is therefore in the mineral kingdom only, where the metallic seed resides, that we must seek the subject proper to our art*) [AMIENS ¶28], and the *Psautier d'Hermophile*: «Le Chaos métallique produit des mains de la nature contient en soy tous les métaux et n'est point métail.» (*The metallic Chaos produced by the hands of nature contains in itself all the metals and is not a metal.*) [AMIENS ¶25]. He calls this text "clear" [AMIENS ¶25]. Le Breton's definition is also cited: «un minéral connu des vrais Sçavans qui le cachent dans leurs écrits sous divers noms, lequel contient abondamment le fixe et le volatil» (*a mineral known to the true Learned, who hide it in their writings under various names, which contains abundantly the fixed and the volatile*) [AMIENS ¶31].

**Stated.** The names Fulcanelli gives the raw subject: «Quant au sujet grossier de l'Œuvre, les uns le nomment Magnesia lunarii ; d'autres, plus sincères, l'appellent Plomb des Sages, Saturnie végétable.» (*As for the gross subject of the Work, some name it Magnesia lunarii; others, more sincere, call it Lead of the Sages, vegetable Saturnia.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶23]. It is also *Fils de Saturne*, *Terre sainte*, and its celestial hieroglyph is the sign of the Ram, *Aries* [BOURGES.2 ¶23]. The names refer either to «sa propriété aimantine et attractive du soufre» (*its magnetic property attracting sulphur*) or to «sa qualité fusible, sa liquéfaction aisée» (*its fusible quality, its easy liquefaction*) [BOURGES.2 ¶23]. Elsewhere it is *Miroir de l'Art*: «la matière première, celle que l'artiste doit élire pour commencer l'Œuvre, est dénommée Miroir de l'Art» (*the first matter, the one the artist must choose to begin the Work, is named Mirror of the Art*) [PARIS.5 ¶4], and it is «si vulgaire et méprisé» (*so common and despised*) [PARIS.5 ¶4]. It is the *Chaos des Sages* «dans lequel tous les secrets cachés se trouvent en puissance» (*in which all the hidden secrets are found in potency*), a phrase he attributes to Philalèthe [BOURGES.2 ¶23].

**Stated.** The oak stands for the common name of the subject: «le chêne a toujours été pris, par les vieux auteurs, pour désigner le nom vulgaire du sujet initial, tel qu'on le rencontre dans la mine» (*the oak has always been taken, by the old authors, to designate the common name of the initial subject, as it is met with in the mine*) [BOURGES.2 ¶21]. The name itself is given only by a near-homophone: «Et c'est par un à-peu-près, dont l'équivalent répond au chêne, que les Philosophes nous renseignent sur cette matière.» (*And it is by an approximation, whose equivalent answers to the oak, that the Philosophers inform us about this matter.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶21]. He adds that he cannot say more without overstepping bounds [BOURGES.2 ¶21]. The chain he does give is galla–Gallia–gallus (gall, Gaul, cock), the cock being the attribute of Mercury; the oak also gives the *Kermès*, which in the *Gaye Science* means the same as *Hermès*, «les consonnes initiales étant permutantes» (*the initial consonants being interchangeable*), both meaning Mercury [BOURGES.2 ¶22]; and galle versus kermès: «tandis que la galle donne le nom de la matière mercurielle brute, le kermès (en arabe girmiz, qui teint en écarlate) caractérise la substance préparée» (*while the gall gives the name of the raw mercurial matter, the kermes (in Arabic girmiz, which dyes scarlet) characterises the prepared substance*) [BOURGES.2 ¶22]. He adds that Greek *gala* means milk and that the mercury is also called *Lait de Vierge* [BOURGES.2 ¶23]. He closes: «le vieux chêne hermétique sert de mère au mercure secret» (*the old hermetic oak serves as mother to the secret mercury*) [BOURGES.2 ¶23].

**Stated.** The reader is told the common name is within reach if he attends to the *galette des Rois* and to why the Egyptians deified the cat; the name is not written out [BOURGES.2 ¶23].

**Stated, from a blazon.** Reading Jacques Cœur's device *cuer* by the rules of cabala, Fulcanelli obtains «le nom vulgaire de la matière basique ouvrée (le fer)» (*the common name of the basic matter worked: iron*) [BOURGES.1 ¶4], and concludes that the *pierre au blanc* was made «par le fer “essencifié” et trois fois cuit» (*by iron "essencified" and three times cooked*) [BOURGES.1 ¶4]. In the Ariadne etymology, Provençal *aran*, *iran* is iron, and Greek Σιδηρος means both iron and magnet [INTRO.5 ¶5]. **Interpreted.** These two passages identify a worked iron with the basic matter of Jacques Cœur's work; the text does not say in so many words that iron is the subject of the Work in general, and the Lion vert passage names "arsenic and lead" as examples of the unripe state [PARIS.4 ¶17]. I record the readings without joining them.

**Stated.** The matter is marked as impure and contemptible. Through the ankh and Venus he reaches copper and sulphur: Κυπρος «est le même mot que Σουϕρος, soufre, lequel a la signification d'engrais, fiente, fumier, ordure» (*is the same word as Σουϕρος, sulphur, which has the meaning of manure, droppings, dung, filth*) [INTRO.4 ¶4]; he quotes «Blanchis le laiton et brûle tes livres» (*Whiten the laton and burn your books*) and the Cosmopolite's «Le sage trouvera notre pierre jusque dans le fumier» (*The wise man will find our stone even in the dunghill*) [INTRO.4 ¶4]. The rough stone was given «l'image du diable» (*the image of the devil*) [INTRO.4 ¶5]; the Lucifer figure of Notre-Dame was «destinée à représenter la matière initiale de l'Œuvre» (*meant to represent the initial matter of the Work*) [INTRO.4 ¶6]. Flamel is quoted: a stone «vile, abjecte et nullement prisée ; et si est couverte de fiens et excrémens» (*vile, abject and not at all prized; and yet it is covered with dung and excrement*) [AMIENS ¶30].

**Stated.** The Virgin-Mother is the primitive substance: «la Vierge-Mère, dépouillée de son voile symbolique, n'est autre chose que la personnification de la substance primitive» (*the Virgin-Mother, stripped of her symbolic veil, is nothing other than the personification of the primitive substance*) [PARIS.1 ¶5]. In the planetary metals «c'est la Lune, qui reçoit les rayons du Soleil et les conserve secrètement dans son sein» (*it is the Moon, which receives the rays of the Sun and keeps them secretly in her bosom*) [PARIS.1 ¶8]; Mary is the form, the Sun the vital spirit, Jesus «le feu corporifié» (*the fire made body*) [PARIS.1 ¶8].

**Stated.** The prepared matter is a leafy, friable stone. Sendivogius is quoted that the prepared mercury «offre l'aspect et la forme d'une masse pierreuse, friable et feuilletée» (*has the look and form of a stony mass, friable and laminated*), whence *terre feuillée* and the symbol of the book [BOURGES.2 ¶16]. A closed book sealed with seven bands marks the raw mineral and «sept opérations successives qui permettent de l’ouvrir» (*the seven successive operations that allow it to be opened*) [BOURGES.2 ¶16].

**Stated.** The *Nostoc* is the hieroglyph of the Magnesia because of «apparition soudaine, absorption d'eau et gonflement, coloration verte, consistance molle et gluante» (*sudden appearance, absorption of water and swelling, green colour, soft and sticky consistency*) [AMIENS ¶35], and its name, from the Greek for night, says that the subject «naît la nuit, a besoin de la nuit pour se développer et ne peut se travailler que la nuit» (*is born at night, needs the night to develop and can be worked only at night*) [AMIENS ¶36].

**Gap.** The common name of the subject is never written; the text says so itself [BOURGES.2 ¶21; AMIENS ¶21]. At Bourges it is given only as a graphic sign, a crown of oak leaves crossed by a sheaf, «correspondant, dans l'art spagyrique, au nom vulgaire du sujet» (*corresponding, in the spagyric art, to the common name of the subject*) [BOURGES.2 ¶27].

## 3. The star: the signature of the subject

**Stated.** The star is the sign of the right matter: «Le Corbeau est, en quelque sorte, le sceau canonique de l'Œuvre, comme l’étoile est la signature du sujet initial.» (*The Raven is, in a way, the canonical seal of the Work, as the star is the signature of the initial subject.*) [PARIS.3 ¶3]. It appears on the compost: «C’est une figure radiée, à six pointes (digamma), dite Etoile des Mages, qui rayonne à la surface du compost» (*It is a radiating figure with six points (digamma), called the Star of the Magi, which shines on the surface of the compost*) [INTRO.6 ¶13], and «Ce sceau magique révèle à l'artiste qu'il a suivi le bon chemin, et que la mixtion philosophale a été préparée canoniquement.» (*This magic seal reveals to the artist that he has followed the right road and that the philosophic mixture has been canonically prepared.*) [INTRO.6 ¶13]. Huginus à Barma is quoted for the matter on which it appears: «Prenez de la vraie terre, dit-il, bien imprégnée des rayons du soleil, de la lune et des autres astres.» (*Take of the true earth, he says, well impregnated with the rays of the sun, the moon and the other stars.*) [INTRO.7 ¶15]. Fulcanelli gathers the legends of the star and leaves the reader to sort «la vérité positive combinée à l'allégorie légendaire» (*the positive truth combined with the legendary allegory*) [INTRO.6 ¶15].

**Stated.** At Bourges the same sign is a net of lines: «La ceinture d'Offerus est piquée de lignes entre-croisées semblables à celles que présente la surface du dissolvant lorsqu'il a été canoniquement préparé.» (*Offerus's belt is pricked with crossed lines like those which the surface of the solvent shows when it has been canonically prepared.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. The old authors called it *Sceau d'Hermes*, *Sel des Sages* (for *Scel*), *Etoile des Mages*, *Etoile polaire* [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. The whole labour aims at it: «tout le travail de l'art consiste à évertuer ce mercure jusqu'à ce qu'il soit revêtu du signe indiqué» (*all the work of the art consists in exerting this mercury until it is clothed with the indicated sign*) [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. The pattern is clearer once gold has been put to dissolve in the mercury [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. At Amiens the master shows three disciples «l’astre hermétique» (*the hermetic star*), and Nicolas Rollin's rebus motto shows the sign of the Work, «l'unique, la seule étoile» (*the unique, the only star*) [AMIENS ¶40]. The six-pointed star is also the sign of the finished stone, «l'union des deux triangles du feu et de l'eau, ou du soufre et du mercure assemblés en un seul corps» (*the union of the two triangles of fire and water, or of sulphur and mercury joined in a single body*) [BOURGES.2 ¶28].

## 4. The conditions: night, season, place

**Stated.** Generation requires darkness: «l'absence totale de toute lumière solaire, même diffuse ou tamisée» (*the total absence of all solar light, even diffuse or filtered*) [AMIENS ¶37]. What the worker attempts is not a creation but a generation: «C'est donc une génération que vous désirez provoquer au sein de vos matériaux.» (*It is therefore a generation that you wish to provoke within your materials.*) [AMIENS ¶37]. The condensation of the universal spirit «ne s'accomplissait que la nuit, à la faveur des ténèbres, du ciel pur et de l'air calme» (*took place only at night, favoured by darkness, a clear sky and calm air*) [PARIS.6 ¶24]. The laboratory at the Hôtel Lallemant is a dark basement room «d'où la lumière solaire, ennemie de toute génération, doit être exclue» (*from which solar light, the enemy of all generation, must be excluded*) [BOURGES.2 ¶4], with a small chimney «qui suffirait à contenir la tour d'un athanor» (*which would suffice to contain the tower of an athanor*) [BOURGES.2 ¶3].

**Stated.** The season is spring. The zodiac of the north portal begins Aries, Taurus, Gemini: «Ce sont les mois printaniers indiquant le début du travail et le temps propice aux opérations.» (*These are the spring months indicating the beginning of the work and the time favourable to the operations.*) [PARIS.6 ¶20]. The dew is most active in spring, hence *Rosée de Mai* [PARIS.6 ¶24]. The steel is found in Aries, «lorsque le soleil parcourt le signe du Bélier» (*when the sun runs through the sign of the Ram*), in the *Clef du Cabinet* which he quotes [PARIS.4 ¶14]. The ram on the last medallion «témoigne qu'il a su choisir la saison favorable et la substance propre» (*testifies that he knew how to choose the favourable season and the proper substance*) [PARIS.5 ¶15].

## 5. The first preparation: opening the matter

**Stated.** The preparation is figured as the dressing of the corner-stone: «Quant à la taille de cette pierre angulaire, — nous entendons sa préparation» (*As for the cutting of this corner-stone, we mean its preparation*) [INTRO.4 ¶6], and as the three nails of the cross: «la croix porte l'empreinte des trois clous qui servirent à immoler le Christ-matière, image des trois purifications par le fer et par le feu» (*the cross bears the imprint of the three nails that served to immolate the Christ-matter, image of the three purifications by iron and by fire*) [INTRO.4 ¶3]. The plan of the church «nous révèle les qualités de la matière première, et sa préparation, par le signe de la Croix» (*reveals to us the qualities of the first matter, and its preparation, by the sign of the Cross*) [INTRO.4 ¶5]; the cross is the crucible, where the matter «meurt pour ressusciter ensuite, purifiée, spiritualisée, déjà transformée» (*dies to rise again afterwards, purified, spiritualised, already transformed*) [INTRO.4 ¶2].

**Stated.** The instruction at Bourges: «Ouvrez, c'est-à-dire décomposez cette matière, tâchez d’en isoler la portion pure, ou son âme métallique» (*Open, that is, decompose this matter, try to isolate its pure part, or its metallic soul*), and the product is «le Kermès, l'Hermès, le mercure teingeant qui porte en soi l'or mystique» (*the Kermes, the Hermes, the tinging mercury that carries the mystic gold in itself*) [BOURGES.2 ¶23]. The mercury only acquires its tinging power «à l'aide de préparations premières» (*by means of first preparations*) [BOURGES.2 ¶22]. These preparations are the *travaux d'Hercule*: the warrior of the twelfth medallion «vient d'accomplir le travail d'Hercule qu'est notre préparation» (*has just accomplished the labour of Hercules that is our preparation*) [PARIS.5 ¶15]; once they are done, the rest is the *ludus puerorum*, «c’est-à-dire à l’entretien du feu, ce qu’une femme filant quenouille peut facilement entreprendre et mener à bien» (*that is, keeping the fire, which a woman spinning at her distaff can easily undertake and carry through*) [AMIENS ¶6].

**Stated.** The Saint-Marcel socle shows the two starting matters and their change: the two besants are «les matières ou natures métalliques, — sujet et dissolvant, — avec lesquelles on doit commencer l'Œuvre» (*the matters or metallic natures, subject and solvent, with which the Work must be begun*) [PARIS.7 ¶18]; after the preliminary operations they become rosettes, and the calyx «indique que les racines métalliques ont été ouvertes et sont disposées à manifester leur principe séminal» (*indicates that the metallic roots have been opened and are disposed to manifest their seminal principle*) [PARIS.7 ¶18]. The union of these two natures gives «le Saturne des Sages, dénomination anagrammatique de natures» (*the Saturn of the Sages, an anagrammatic name for natures*) [PARIS.7 ¶22].

**Stated.** The dead metals must be made raw again. The dead trees of Amiens are the «planètes métalliques dont le feu, nous disent les Philosophes, a causé la mort, et que la fusion a rendues inertes, sans pouvoir végétatif» (*metallic planets whose death, the Philosophers tell us, was caused by fire, and which fusion has made inert, without vegetative power*) [AMIENS ¶20]; the masters recommend «les réincruder en leur fournissant, avec la forme fluide, l'agent propre qu'elles ont perdu dans la réduction métallurgique» (*to reincrudate them by giving them, with the fluid form, the proper agent they lost in metallurgical reduction*) [AMIENS ¶21]. *Réincruder* is glossed as «rendre cru, c'est-à-dire remettre dans un état antérieur à celui qui caractérise la maturité, rétrograder» (*to make raw, that is, to put back into a state prior to that which characterises maturity, to go backwards*) [BOURGES.1 n2]. He then asks «Mais où trouver cet agent ?» (*But where to find this agent?*) and answers that it is the great mystery he has scattered among the emblems [AMIENS ¶21].

## 6. The secret fire and the universal solvent

This is the point Fulcanelli calls the highest secret and refuses to deliver. What he does say:

**Stated.** Two fires are needed in the coction. The outer fire is the *feu de roue*: «Le feu soutenu, constant et égal que l'artiste entretient nuit et jour au cours de cette opération, est appelé, pour cette raison, feu de roue.» (*The sustained, constant and even fire that the artist keeps night and day during this operation is called, for this reason, fire of the wheel.*) [INTRO.6 ¶3]. Besides it «il faut, en plus, un second agent, dit feu secret ou philosophique» (*there is needed, in addition, a second agent, called secret or philosophic fire*), and «C'est ce dernier feu, excité par la chaleur vulgaire, qui fait tourner la roue» (*It is this latter fire, excited by common heat, which makes the wheel turn*) [INTRO.6 ¶3].

**Stated.** The secret fire looks like water. The philosophic calcination «demande l'aide d'un agent occulte, d'un feu secret, lequel, pour donner un aperçu de sa forme, ressemble plus à une eau qu'à une flamme» (*requires the help of an occult agent, a secret fire, which, to give an idea of its form, looks more like a water than a flame*) [PARIS.3 ¶20]. «Ce feu, ou cette eau ardente, est l'étincelle vitale communiquée par le Créateur à la matière inerte» (*This fire, or this burning water, is the vital spark communicated by the Creator to inert matter*) [PARIS.3 ¶20]. He says he was himself stopped by it: «nous fûmes arrêté nous-même par cette difficulté pendant plus de vingt ans» (*we were ourselves stopped by this difficulty for more than twenty years*) [PARIS.3 ¶20], and that he can do no more than point to Artephius, Pontanus and the *Epistola de Igne Philosophorum* [PARIS.3 ¶20]. Limojon is quoted: «Ce feu n'est pas actuellement chaud, mais il est un esprit igné introduit dans un sujet d'une même nature que la Pierre» (*This fire is not actually hot, but it is a fiery spirit introduced into a subject of the same nature as the Stone*) [PARIS.3 ¶22]. The anonymous manuscript says of the first putrefaction that it «se fait par notre feu secret, qui est le seul agent qui puisse ouvrir, sublimer et putréfier» (*is done by our secret fire, which is the only agent that can open, sublimate and putrefy*) [PARIS.3 ¶11].

**Stated.** The secret fire is enclosed in a water, and that water is the universal solvent: «ce feu secret enclos dans une eau, qui constitue le dissolvant universel» (*this secret fire enclosed in a water, which constitutes the universal solvent*) [PARIS.3 ¶23]. The matter used to prepare it is figured by the Ram, and Pernety is quoted: «Les Adeptes disent qu'ils tirent leur acier du ventre d'Ariès, et ils appellent aussi cet acier leur aimant.» (*The Adepts say they draw their steel from the belly of Aries, and they also call this steel their magnet.*) [PARIS.3 ¶23]. The Ariadne etymology gives the same: «αιρην est l'aimant, la vertu renfermée dans le corps que les Sages nomment leur magnésie» (*αιρην is the magnet, the virtue enclosed in the body that the Sages call their magnesia*) [INTRO.5 ¶5].

**Stated.** The solvent is made by fixing a spirit spread in the air into an attractive body. «C'est cet esprit, répandu à la surface du globe, que l'artiste subtil et ingénieux doit capter au fur et à mesure de sa matérialisation.» (*It is this spirit, spread over the surface of the globe, that the subtle and ingenious artist must capture as it materialises.*) [PARIS.4 ¶13]. For this «il est besoin d'un corps particulier servant de réceptacle, d'une terre attractive où il puisse trouver un principe susceptible de le recevoir et de le “corporifier”» (*a particular body is needed to serve as receptacle, an attractive earth where it can find a principle able to receive it and give it a body*) [PARIS.4 ¶13]. «C'est là cet aimant enfermé au ventre d'Ariès, qu'il faut prendre au moment de sa naissance, avec autant d'adresse que d'habileté.» (*This is that magnet shut in the belly of Aries, which must be taken at the moment of its birth, with as much address as skill.*) [PARIS.4 ¶13]. The Amiens quatrefoil shows the dew falling only on the magnesia, «une vertu attractive cachée dans ce corps» (*an attractive virtue hidden in this body*) [AMIENS ¶34].

**Stated.** The spirit is the May dew, and its extraction is the oath-bound secret. The Massacre of the Innocents shows «la condensation de l'Esprit universel, lequel forme, aussitôt matérialisé, le fameux Bain des astres où le soleil et la lune chimiques doivent se baigner, changer de nature et rajeunir» (*the condensation of the universal Spirit, which forms, as soon as materialised, the famous Bath of the stars where the chemical sun and moon must bathe, change nature and grow young*) [PARIS.6 ¶22]. The Sages named it *Rosée de Mai* [PARIS.6 ¶24]. It «s'extrait aisément d'un corps particulier, abject et méprisé, dont nous avons déjà décrit les caractéristiques, s'il n'était des bornes infranchissables» (*is easily extracted from a particular body, abject and despised, whose characteristics we have already described, were it not for impassable bounds*) [PARIS.6 ¶25]; «Nous touchons au plus haut secret de l’Œuvre et désirons tenir notre serment.» (*We touch the highest secret of the Work and wish to keep our oath.*) [PARIS.6 ¶25]. He identifies this with the *Verbum dimissum*, the lost Word [PARIS.6 ¶25; AMIENS ¶22].

**Stated.** The solvent's properties: it is a water that does not wet, and solid. From the fountain allegory, two waters combine and «De cette combinaison résulte une troisième eau qui ne mouille pas les mains, et que les Philosophes ont appelé tantôt Mercure, tantôt Soufre» (*From this combination results a third water that does not wet the hands, which the Philosophers have called now Mercury, now Sulphur*) [PARIS.2 ¶7]. «Le servus fugitivus dont nous avons besoin est une eau minérale et métallique, solide, cassante, ayant l'aspect d'une pierre et de liquéfaction très aisée.» (*The runaway servant we need is a mineral and metallic water, solid, brittle, looking like a stone and very easily liquefied.*) [PARIS.5 ¶7]. «C'est cette eau coagulée sous forme de masse pierreuse qui est l'Alkaest et le Dissolvant universel.» (*It is this water coagulated in the form of a stony mass that is the Alkahest and the universal Solvent.*) [PARIS.5 ¶7]. At Amiens: «Car l'eau dont nous avons besoin est sèche, ne mouille pas les mains et jaillit sous le choc de la verge d'Aaron.» (*For the water we need is dry, does not wet the hands, and springs forth under the blow of Aaron's rod.*) [AMIENS ¶15]. It penetrates all metals: «De quelle nature est ce puissant dissolvant capable de pénétrer tous les métaux, — l'or en particulier, — et d'accomplir, avec l'aide du corps dissous, le grand ouvrage en entier ?» (*Of what nature is this powerful solvent, able to penetrate all metals, gold in particular, and to accomplish, with the help of the dissolved body, the whole great work?*) [PARIS.2 ¶6].

**Stated.** It is not common quicksilver. The dissolution of vulgar mercury to make "our" mercury «nous paraît hasardeuse, sinon sophistique» (*seems to us hazardous, if not sophistical*) [PARIS.5 ¶7], and he declines to share Sabine Stuart de Chevalier's view, citing reasons «appuyées sur l'expérience» (*based on experience*) [PARIS.5 ¶7]. (**Interpreted.** The sentence as printed reads «de croire que le mercure vulgaire, dépourvu d'agent propre, pourrait devenir une eau utile à l'Œuvre» (*to believe that vulgar mercury, lacking its own agent, could become a water useful to the Work*) [PARIS.5 ¶7], without a negation; given that he disagrees with her, the sense must be that he does not believe it.) The oak's spring «n'ait aucune apparence du vif-argent vulgaire» (*has no appearance of common quicksilver*) [AMIENS ¶15].

**Stated.** The first agent for preparing the solvent is the Lion vert, the "young" state of the matter. «Le premier agent magnétique servant à préparer le dissolvant, — que certains ont dénommé Alkaest, — est appelé Lion vert» (*The first magnetic agent serving to prepare the solvent, which some have named Alkahest, is called Green Lion*), «C'est un fruit vert et acerbe, comparé au fruit rouge et mûr.» (*It is a green and sour fruit, compared with the red and ripe fruit.*), «C'est l'arsenic et le plomb à l'égard de l'argent et de l'or.» (*It is arsenic and lead with respect to silver and gold.*) [PARIS.4 ¶17]. Basile Valentin calls it *Vitriol vert*, for its hot, burning and saline nature; others *Emeraude des Philosophes*, *Rosée de mai*, *Herbe saturnienne*, *Pierre végétale* [PARIS.4 ¶17]. The Lion rouge is «la même matière, ou Lion vert, amené par certains procédés à cette qualité spéciale qui caractérise l'or hermétique» (*the same matter, or Green Lion, brought by certain processes to that special quality which characterises hermetic gold*) [PARIS.4 ¶18], with Basile's counsel «Dissous et nourris le vray Lion du sang du Lion vert, parquoy ils sont tous deux de même nature.» (*Dissolve and feed the true Lion with the blood of the green Lion, for they are both of the same nature.*) [PARIS.4 ¶18]. The dissolvent is also obtained by a combat: «Ce combat singulier des corps chimiques dont la combinaison procure le dissolvant secret (et le vase du composé)» (*This single combat of chemical bodies whose combination procures the secret solvent (and the vessel of the compound)*) is figured by Cadmus, Jason, Saint Michael and the rest [BOURGES.1 ¶9].

**Stated.** The mercury contains everything and is the only matter needed: «Il est important, avant d'entreprendre la pratique, de savoir que le mercure contient en soi tout ce qui est nécessaire au travail.» (*It is important, before undertaking the practice, to know that the mercury contains in itself all that is necessary for the work.*) [AMIENS ¶15]; «Dans notre Oeuvre, affirment les Philosophes, le Mercure seul suffit.» (*In our Work, the Philosophers affirm, Mercury alone suffices.*) [PARIS.3 ¶18]; «Celui qui sait faire l'Œuvre par le seul mercure a trouvé ce qu'il y a de plus parfait.» (*He who knows how to do the Work by mercury alone has found what is most perfect.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶28], given as what the most famous authors affirm.

**Interpreted.** Taken together, these passages describe one substance passing through states: a raw mineral (the oak, the gall, Saturn's child), opened by a first preparation, with the Lion vert as first agent, into a solid, fusible, dry "water" (the Alkahest) which has fixed in itself a spirit gathered from the air at night in spring (the May dew); on this water the star appears when it is rightly made. The text never says this in one place; it rests on [BOURGES.2 ¶21–23], [PARIS.4 ¶13–17], [PARIS.5 ¶7], [PARIS.6 ¶22–25] and [INTRO.6 ¶13].

**Gap.** How the spirit is fixed, from which "abject body" the dew is drawn, and what the "steel" is, are withheld on oath [PARIS.6 ¶25; AMIENS ¶21]. The Saint Thomas blazon is said to unveil «les secrets relatifs à l'extraction du mercure et à sa conjonction avec le soufre» (*the secrets relating to the extraction of the mercury and its conjunction with the sulphur*) [PARIS.8 ¶7]; the text describes the blazon and does not read it.

## 7. The vessel and the furnace

**Stated.** The furnace is the Athanor, «l'occulte four aux deux flammes, — potentielle et virtuelle, — que tous les disciples connaissent» (*the occult furnace with two flames, potential and virtual, that all disciples know*) [PARIS.2 ¶25]. It has «cendrier, tour et dôme» (*ash-pit, tower and dome*) [AMIENS ¶13]; the seventh relief shows its section and «l'appareillage interne destiné à supporter l'oeuf philosophique» (*the internal fittings meant to hold the philosophic egg*) [PARIS.4 ¶2]. The Amiens "apostate" is an alchemist at the furnace: «L'église est plutôt un athanor, et son clocher élevé en dépit des règles les plus élémentaires de l'architecture, le four secret renfermant l'oeuf philosophal.» (*The church is rather an athanor, and its steeple raised in defiance of the most elementary rules of architecture, the secret furnace holding the philosophic egg.*) [AMIENS ¶12]; the furnace has openings for watching the phases, the man points at the sack of coal, and his removed shoes show «jusqu'où doivent être poussés la prudence et le souci du silence dans cette besogne cachée» (*how far prudence and care for silence must be pushed in this hidden task*) [AMIENS ¶12].

**Stated.** Two vessels are distinguished: «le vase est doublement envisagé, et dans sa matière et dans sa forme, d'une part à l'état de vase de nature, de l'autre comme vase de l'art» (*the vessel is considered in two ways, in its matter and in its form, on one hand as vessel of nature, on the other as vessel of the art*) [BOURGES.2 ¶6]. The vessel of the art is the glass egg: «Celui-ci peut, à volonté, être sphérique ou ovoïde, pourvu qu'il soit en verre clair, transparent, sans soufflure.» (*It may, at will, be spherical or ovoid, provided it is of clear, transparent glass, without bubbles.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶6]; thick walls, some recommend *verre de Lorraine*, and «l'essentiel est qu'on puisse facilement le souder à la lampe d'émailleur» (*the essential thing is that it can easily be sealed at the enameller's lamp*) [BOURGES.2 ¶6]. The egg is also the compost in its vessel: «Par le terme d'œuf, les Sages entendent leur composé, disposé dans son vase propre, et prêt à subir les transformations que l'action du feu y provoquera.» (*By the term egg the Sages mean their compound, placed in its own vessel and ready to undergo the transformations that the action of fire will provoke in it.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶5], whose shell holds the rebis «formé de blanc et de rouge dans une proportion analogue à celle de l’oeuf des oiseaux» (*formed of white and red in a proportion like that of birds' eggs*) [BOURGES.2 ¶5]. The vessel of nature is the *Lion vert*: «Généralement, il est appelé œuf philosophique et Lion vert.» (*Generally it is called philosophic egg and Green Lion.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶5], with Ripley quoted that only one unclean body enters the magistery, commonly called Green Lion, «le milieu ou moyen pour joindre les teintures entre le soleil et la lune» (*the middle or means for joining the tinctures between the sun and the moon*) [BOURGES.2 ¶5].

**Stated.** The secret vessel is RER, and the artist must make it himself. «ces trois lettres contiennent un secret d'une importance capitale, qui se rapporte au vase de l'Œuvre» (*these three letters contain a secret of capital importance, relating to the vessel of the Work*) [BOURGES.2 ¶34]; «RER sert à cuire, à unir radicalement et indissolublement, à provoquer les transformations du compost RERE.» (*RER serves to cook, to unite radically and indissolubly, to provoke the transformations of the compost RERE.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶34]. Against Basile's "whoever has the matter will find a pot", he affirms «il sera impossible d'obtenir le moindre succès dans l'Œuvre si l'on a pas une connaissance parfaite de ce qu'est le Vase des Philosophes ni de quelle manière il faut le fabriquer» (*it will be impossible to obtain the least success in the Work without a perfect knowledge of what the Vessel of the Philosophers is and how it must be made*) [BOURGES.2 ¶34]; Pontanus restarted the work more than two hundred times before knowing it [BOURGES.2 ¶34]; «L'artiste doit faire lui-même son vaisseau ; c'est une maxime de l'art.» (*The artist must make his vessel himself; it is a maxim of the art.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶34]. RER means «une matière augmentée de la moitié d'une autre ou de la sienne propre» (*a matter increased by half of another or of its own*), «Notez qu'il ne s'agit point ici de proportions, mais d'une combinaison chimique indépendante des quantités relatives.» (*Note that this is not a matter of proportions, but of a chemical combination independent of the relative quantities.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶35]; the realgar example is explicitly a supposition, «supposons que la matière représentée par RE soit le réalgar ou sulfure naturel d'arsenic» (*let us suppose that the matter represented by RE is realgar or natural sulphide of arsenic*) [BOURGES.2 ¶35]. The reader is told «cherchez tout d'abord RER, c'est-à-dire le vaisseau» (*seek first of all RER, that is, the vessel*), that the Sibyl's philosopher is «C'est celui qui sait faire le verre.» (*He is the one who knows how to make glass.*), and that «L'industrie du potier vous serait plus instructive» (*The potter's craft would be more instructive to you*) [BOURGES.2 ¶36].

**Stated, quoted.** For the putrefaction of the water, the *Clef du Cabinet* prescribes an oak vessel: «le vaisseau doit être en bois de vieux chesne, tourné en rond en dedans, comme un demi-globe, dont les bords soient fort épais en quarré» (*the vessel must be of old oak wood, turned round inside like a half-globe, with very thick square edges*) [PARIS.4 ¶14]; the ninth relief shows a stave of oak as the material of the hermetic vessel [PARIS.4 ¶13]. Fulcanelli gives this text with the warning that it must be read «avec prudence» and that «l'esprit vivifie, mais la lettre tue» (*the spirit gives life, but the letter kills*) [PARIS.4 ¶15].

**Stated.** In the dry way the vessel is a crucible: «La cuisson de l'Œuvre, au lieu de nécessiter l’emploi d’un vase de verre, ne réclame que le secours d’un simple creuset.» (*The cooking of the Work, instead of requiring a glass vessel, needs only the help of a simple crucible.*) [PARIS.7 ¶7], and the maxim «un seul vaisseau, une seule matière, un seul fourneau» (*one vessel, one matter, one furnace*) could be explained by it [PARIS.7 ¶7].

**Interpreted.** The "vessel of nature", the Lion vert, the oak barrel and RER are all said to be the thing in which the matter is cooked, and the Lion vert is also the first agent of the solvent [PARIS.4 ¶17; BOURGES.2 ¶5]; the dissolvent combat gives «le vase du composé» [BOURGES.1 ¶9]. The narrowest reading the text supports is that the "vessel" of the Work is a matter, not only a glass: the glass egg holds it, and this inner vessel is what the artist must know how to make. The text does not say this outright.

## 8. The operations in their order

The text gives several counts: nine operations, «au cours des neufs opérations successives du labeur hermétique» (*in the course of the nine successive operations of the hermetic labour*) [PARIS.1 ¶3]; seven, «sept opérations successives qui permettent de l’ouvrir» [BOURGES.2 ¶16]; seven to nine "eagles" in Philalèthe [PARIS.4 ¶4]; three "works" after Geber [BOURGES.2 ¶32]; two Magisteries [BOURGES.1 ¶4]; and one single operation, «Procédé simple et complet qui ne comporte qu'une voie, n'exige qu'une matière, ne réclame qu'une opération.» (*Simple and complete process which has only one way, requires only one matter, demands only one operation.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶28]. The text does not reconcile these. It also warns that the raven «paraît à diverses reprises, et cette fréquence permet aux auteurs de jeter la confusion dans l'ordre des opérations» (*appears several times, and this frequency lets the authors throw confusion into the order of the operations*) [PARIS.3 ¶4], citing Le Breton's «il y a quatre putréfactions dans l'Œuvre philosophique» (*there are four putrefactions in the philosophic Work*) [PARIS.3 ¶5]. What follows is the order as far as the text fixes it. The text names a "first work" in which the conjunction falls [PARIS.6 ¶21] and a "second work" that opens with the coction of the Rebis [PARIS.5 ¶9]; **interpreted**, I group the reiterations and multiplication after these, a grouping the text does not name.

### 8a. Dissolution and the first putrefaction

**Stated.** The first putrefaction is distinct from the raven of the coction: the Chevalier Inconnu's text shows «deux opérations successives, dont la première se termine et la seconde commence après l'apparition de la couleur noire, ce qui n'est pas le cas de la coction» (*two successive operations, the first ending and the second beginning after the appearance of the black colour, which is not the case for the coction*) [PARIS.3 ¶9]. The signs of the first putrefaction, from the manuscript: «quelque acidité» (*some acidity*), «quelque odeur de sépulcre» (*some smell of the grave*), «yeux de poisson» (*fish eyes*), small bubbles on the surface, a froth, a long fermentation by the secret fire [PARIS.3 ¶11]. Every radical dissolution is a *Clef*: «On donne le nom de Clef à toute dissolution alchimique radicale (c’est-à-dire irréductible)» (*The name Key is given to any radical, that is irreducible, alchemical dissolution*) [PARIS.3 n6].

**Stated.** The solvent reduces the fixed body to its first state. The seventh medallion shows «la découverte de l'agent capable d'opérer la réduction du corps fixe, de le réincruder, selon l'expression reçue, en une forme analogue à celle de sa prime substance» (*the discovery of the agent able to effect the reduction of the fixed body, to reincrudate it, in the accepted expression, into a form like that of its first substance*) [PARIS.5 ¶11]; the old man is «notre Mercure» and the Palace «l'or vif, ou philosophique, or vil, méprisé de l'ignorant» (*the living or philosophic gold, vile gold, despised by the ignorant*) [PARIS.5 ¶11]. The solvent «permet la réincrudation de l'or naturel, son amollissement et le retour à son premier état sous la forme saline, friable et très fusible» (*allows the reincrudation of natural gold, its softening and its return to its first state in a saline, friable and very fusible form*) [BOURGES.1 ¶10]; «L'or perd sa couronne, — en perdant sa couleur, — durant un certain laps de temps» (*Gold loses its crown, in losing its colour, for a certain time*) [BOURGES.1 ¶10].

**Stated.** There are two solutions and they identify the way followed: «La dissolution de l'or alchimique par le Dissolvant Alkaest caractérise la première voie ; celle de l'or vulgaire par notre mercure indique la seconde. Par celle-ci on réalise le mercure animé.» (*The dissolution of alchemical gold by the Alkahest Solvent characterises the first way; that of vulgar gold by our mercury indicates the second. By the latter one makes the animated mercury.*) [PARIS.5 ¶14]. In the first «l'or ou roi est dissous peu à peu et sans violence» (*the gold or king is dissolved little by little and without violence*); in the second «le mercure hermétique semble attaquer le métal avec une vigueur caractéristique et qui ressemble assez aux effervescences chimiques» (*the hermetic mercury seems to attack the metal with a characteristic vigour rather like chemical effervescences*) [PARIS.5 ¶12]. The text ties the gentle dissolution to «l'animation du mercure» (*the animation of the mercury*) [PARIS.5 ¶12], while it says the animated mercury is made by the second way [PARIS.5 ¶14]; it does not reconcile the two. The quarrelling children show «l'action de l'eau pontique sur la matière grave» (*the action of the pontic water on the heavy matter*) [PARIS.5 ¶13]. Vulgar gold is made fit for the work only by «l'exaltation» and «la transfusion»; gold that has suffered neither seems excluded from the ancient way [PARIS.5 ¶6].

**Stated.** The dissolution works by repeated cohobation: «Cette allégorie a trait à l'extraction des parties fixes, centrales et pures, par les volatiles ou éthérées dans la Dissolution philosophique.» (*This allegory concerns the extraction of the fixed, central and pure parts by the volatile or ethereal ones in the philosophic Dissolution.*) [PARIS.5 ¶2]; one must «réitérer souvent l'affusion de l'eau sur la terre, de l'esprit sur le corps» (*often repeat the pouring of the water on the earth, of the spirit on the body*) until the horse carries its rider, and so «extraire le sel occulte du Lion rouge avec le secours de l'esprit du Lion vert» (*extract the occult salt of the Red Lion with the help of the spirit of the Green Lion*) [PARIS.5 ¶2].

**Stated.** Calcination in the philosophic sense is done by the secret fire, not by common fire [PARIS.3 ¶20]. The common, *spagyrique* account, which he calls a similitude the authors use for the general idea of the work [PARIS.3 ¶20], is that what resists is the fixed salt: «Ce lézard fabuleux ne désigne pas autre chose que le sel central, incombustible et fixe, qui garde sa nature jusque dans les cendres des métaux calcinés, et que les Anciens ont nommé Semence métallique.» (*This fabulous lizard designates nothing other than the central salt, incombustible and fixed, which keeps its nature even in the ashes of calcined metals, and which the Ancients named metallic Seed.*) [PARIS.3 ¶19]; the pure parts «peuvent s'extraire par lixiviation» (*can be extracted by lixiviation*) [PARIS.3 ¶19]. At Bourges the winged skull gives the same sequence: «Le corps, mortifié, tombe en cendre noire ayant l'aspect du poussier de charbon.» (*The body, mortified, falls into black ash looking like coal dust.*), then «la cendre, calcinée, abandonne ses impuretés grossières et adustibles ; il naît alors un sel pur, que la cuisson revêt de la puissance occulte du feu» (*the ash, calcined, gives up its gross and combustible impurities; a pure salt is then born, which the cooking clothes with the occult power of fire*) [BOURGES.2 ¶25].

### 8b. The eagles, sublimation and the first conjunction

**Stated.** The griffin is «l'hiéroglyphe de la première conjonction, laquelle ne s'opère que peu à peu, au fur et à mesure de ce labeur pénible et fastidieux que les Philosophes ont appelé leurs aigles» (*the hieroglyph of the first conjunction, which takes place only little by little, in the course of that painful and tedious labour the Philosophers called their eagles*) [PARIS.4 ¶3]. «La série d'opérations dont l'ensemble aboutit à l'union intime du soufre et du mercure porte aussi le nom de Sublimation.» (*The series of operations whose whole ends in the intimate union of sulphur and mercury also bears the name Sublimation.*) [PARIS.4 ¶3]. In it «le mercure exalté se dépouille de ses parties grossières et terrestres, de son humidité superflue, et s'empare d'une portion du corps fixe, qu'il dissout, absorbe et assimile» (*the exalted mercury strips itself of its gross and earthy parts, of its superfluous humidity, and seizes a portion of the fixed body, which it dissolves, absorbs and assimilates*) [PARIS.4 ¶3]. «Faire voler l'aigle, selon l'expression hermétique, c'est faire sortir la lumière du tombeau et la porter à la surface» (*To make the eagle fly, in the hermetic expression, is to bring the light out of the tomb and carry it to the surface*) [PARIS.4 ¶3]. Philalèthe is quoted: «il ne faut pas moins de sept aigles, et l'on devrait même en employer jusqu'à neuf» (*no fewer than seven eagles are needed, and one should even use up to nine*) [PARIS.4 ¶4].

**Stated.** The conjunction is a combat of fixed and volatile: «Le lion traduit la force terrestre et fixe, tandis que l'aigle exprime la force aérienne et volatile.» (*The lion expresses the earthy and fixed force, while the eagle expresses the aerial and volatile force.*), ending when «les antagonistes ne fassent plus qu'un même corps, de qualité moyenne et de substance homogène, le Mercure animé» (*the antagonists make but one body, of middle quality and homogeneous substance, the animated Mercury*) [PARIS.4 ¶11]. The caduceus is this product: «Le serpent indique la nature incisive et dissolvante du Mercure, qui absorbe avidement le soufre métallique et le retient si fort que la cohésion ne peut être ultérieurement vaincue.» (*The serpent indicates the incisive and dissolving nature of Mercury, which greedily absorbs the metallic sulphur and holds it so strongly that the cohesion can no longer be overcome.*) [PARIS.3 ¶18]; «Ce reptile est le type du Mercure dans son premier état, et la verge d'or, le soufre corporel qui lui est ajouté.» (*This reptile is the type of Mercury in its first state, and the golden rod the corporeal sulphur added to it.*) [PARIS.3 ¶18]. The result is the compound of the first order, «l’oeuf vitriolé qui n’exige plus qu’une cuisson graduée pour se transformer d’abord en soufre rouge, ensuite en Elixir, puis, au troisième période, en Médecine universelle» (*the vitriolated egg which needs only a graduated cooking to turn first into red sulphur, then into Elixir, then, in the third period, into universal Medicine*) [PARIS.3 ¶18].

**Stated.** Timing of the conjunction: Leo set in Cancer's place marks «la conjonction du ferment philosophique, — ou Lion, — avec le composé mercuriel, union qui se doit accomplir vers la fin du quatrième mois du premier Œuvre» (*the conjunction of the philosophic ferment, or Lion, with the mercurial compound, a union which must be accomplished towards the end of the fourth month of the first Work*) [PARIS.6 ¶21]. Taurus «c’est le second mois des opérations préparatoires dans le premier œuvre, et le premier régime du feu élémentaire dans le second» (*is the second month of the preparatory operations in the first work, and the first regime of the elementary fire in the second*) [PARIS.4 ¶21]. Bull and cow, sun and moon, sulphur and mercury are «les natures primitives contraires, avant leur conjonction, natures que l'Art extrait de mixtes imparfaits» (*the contrary primitive natures, before their conjunction, natures that the Art extracts from imperfect mixts*) [PARIS.4 ¶21].

**Stated.** Two other figures of the north portal belong here: the dog of Corascene «qu'il faut savoir séparer du compost à l'état de poudre noire» (*which one must know how to separate from the compost in the state of black powder*) and the Doves of Diana, under which «la spiritualisation et sublimation du mercure philosophal sont cachées» (*the spiritualisation and sublimation of the philosophic mercury are hidden*) [PARIS.6 ¶12]; the lamb is «emblème de l'édulcoration du principe arsenical de la Matière» (*emblem of the sweetening of the arsenical principle of the Matter*) [PARIS.6 ¶12]; the upside-down man is *solve et coagula*, «la conversion élémentaire en volatilisant le fixe et fixant le volatil» (*the elementary conversion by volatilising the fixed and fixing the volatile*) [PARIS.6 ¶12]: «Si le fixe tu sçays dissouldre» (*If thou canst dissolve the fixed*) [PARIS.6 ¶13], «Et le dissoult faire voler» (*and make the dissolved fly*) [PARIS.6 ¶14].

**Stated.** The animated mercury then carries the gold as a child: «le mercure, dès la dissolution effectuée, porte l'enfant, le Fils du Soleil, le Petit Roi (Roitelet), comme une mère véritable» (*the mercury, once the dissolution is done, carries the child, the Son of the Sun, the Little King (Wren), like a true mother*) [BOURGES.2 ¶14]; the *baigneur* in the cake is «l'or dans son bain» (*gold in its bath*) [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. Saint Christopher is «l'hiéroglyphe du soufre solaire (Jésus), ou de l'or naissant, élevé sur les ondes mercurielles et porté ensuite, par l'énergie propre de ce Mercure, au degré de puissance que possède l'Elixir» (*the hieroglyph of the solar sulphur (Jesus), or of the nascent gold, raised on the mercurial waves and then carried, by the Mercury's own energy, to the degree of power the Elixir has*) [INTRO.9 ¶15].

### 8c. The second solution and the extraction of the sulphur

**Stated.** The last medallion shows «une solution seconde, celle du Soufre, rouge ou blanc, par l'eau philosophique» (*a second solution, that of the Sulphur, red or white, by the philosophic water*) [PARIS.5 ¶15]; the compound is «plus céleste que terrestre» (*more celestial than earthly*) [PARIS.5 ¶15]. The Cosmopolite is quoted: Saturn «puisa dix parties de cette eau, et incontinent prit le fruit de l'arbre solaire et le mit dans cette eau» (*drew ten parts of this water, and at once took the fruit of the solar tree and put it in this water*) [PARIS.5 ¶15]. The cock-fox monster figures «L'extraction du Soufre rouge et incombustible» (*The extraction of the red and incombustible Sulphur*) [PARIS.4 ¶19]. The universal spirit is the sulphur: «l'Esprit universel, corporifié dans les minéraux sous le nom alchimique de Soufre, constitue le principe et l'agent efficace de toutes les teintures métalliques» (*the universal Spirit, embodied in minerals under the alchemical name of Sulphur, constitutes the principle and the effective agent of all metallic tinctures*), and it is obtained only by decomposing the body: the body must die if one is to extract «l'âme, vie métallique et Rosée céleste» (*the soul, metallic life and celestial Dew*) it held; «Les corps n'ont point d'action les uns sur les autres ; l'esprit, seul, est actif et agissant.» (*Bodies have no action on one another; the spirit alone is active and acting.*) [PARIS.6 ¶23].

**Stated.** The Saint-Marcel die gives the ignition that follows the union: «Les matières préparées et unies en un seul composé doivent subir la sublimation ou dernière purification ignée.» (*The prepared matters, united in a single compound, must undergo sublimation or the last fiery purification.*) [PARIS.7 ¶19]; «les principes purs, incombustibles, s'élèvent sous une forme très différente de celle qu'affectait le composé» (*the pure, incombustible principles rise in a form very different from that of the compound*), and this is «le sel des philosophes, le roi couronné de gloire, qui prend naissance dans le feu et doit se réjouir dans le mariage subséquent» (*the salt of the philosophers, the king crowned with glory, who is born in the fire and must rejoice in the subsequent marriage*) [PARIS.7 ¶19]. The residue then «est prêt, dès lors, à fournir le sel mercuriel, volatil et fusible, auquel les vieux auteurs ont souvent donné l'épithète de Dragon babylonien» (*is then ready to furnish the mercurial salt, volatile and fusible, to which the old authors often gave the epithet Babylonian Dragon*) [PARIS.7 ¶20].

**Stated.** At Amiens the cock and fox give the whole cycle on one matter. The cock is the volatile part extracted from the subject (the oak); it must take «l'état de fixité provisoire» (*the state of provisional fixity*) of the fox; the dry water, kept long at the fire, becomes fixed, «L'eau est devenue terre et le mercure soufre.» (*The water has become earth and the mercury sulphur.*) [AMIENS ¶15]. But «toute teinture sèche est inutile en sa siccité» (*every dry tincture is useless in its dryness*), so «il convient donc de redissoudre cette terre ou ce sel dans la même eau qui lui a donné naissance, ou, ce qui revient au même, dans son propre sang» (*one must therefore redissolve this earth or salt in the same water that gave it birth, or, which comes to the same, in its own blood*) [AMIENS ¶15]. A second like operation coagulates it again, this time in fusion: «Ainsi naîtra la première pierre, non absolument fixe ni absolument volatile, toutefois assez permanente au feu, très pénétrante et très fusible» (*Thus will be born the first stone, neither absolutely fixed nor absolutely volatile, yet fairly permanent in fire, very penetrating and very fusible*), properties to be increased by «une troisième réitération de la même technique» (*a third reiteration of the same technique*), so that «le coq aura chanté trois fois» (*the cock will have crowed three times*) [AMIENS ¶15].

### 8d. The coction of the Rebis (second work)

**Stated.** The Rebis is the double compound: «RE, une matière sèche, or philosophique ; RE, une matière humide, mercure philosophique ; RERE ou REBIS, une matière double, à la fois humide et sèche, amalgame d'or et de mercure philosophiques» (*RE, a dry matter, philosophic gold; RE, a moist matter, philosophic mercury; RERE or REBIS, a double matter, at once moist and dry, amalgam of philosophic gold and mercury*) [BOURGES.2 ¶33]. The philosophic mercury «résulte de l'absorption d'une certaine partie de soufre par une quantité déterminée de mercure» (*results from the absorption of a certain part of sulphur by a determined quantity of mercury*) [PARIS.5 ¶6].

**Stated.** The coction begins cold and slow. The shivering old man is «la première phase du second œuvre, alors que le Rebis hermétique, enfermé au centre de l'Athanor, souffre la dislocation de ses parties et tend à se mortifier» (*the first phase of the second work, when the hermetic Rebis, shut in the centre of the Athanor, suffers the dislocation of its parts and tends to mortify*) [PARIS.5 ¶9]; «C'est le début, actif et doux, du feu de roue symbolisé par le froid et par l'hiver» (*It is the active and gentle beginning of the fire of the wheel, symbolised by cold and winter*), and «C'est le règne de Saturne qui va paraître, emblème de la dissolution radicale, de la décomposition et de la couleur noire.» (*It is the reign of Saturn that is about to appear, emblem of radical dissolution, decomposition and the black colour.*) [PARIS.5 ¶9].

**Stated.** The raven of the coction is the first colour: «la couleur noire, première apparence de la décomposition consécutive à la mixtion parfaite des matières de l'Œuf» (*the black colour, first appearance of the decomposition following the perfect mixture of the matters of the Egg*), «la marque certaine du succès futur, le signe évident de l'exacte préparation du compost» (*the certain mark of future success, the evident sign of the exact preparation of the compost*) [PARIS.3 ¶3]. Trévisan is quoted at length: «lors est tout le compost tourné en manière de poix fondue, et est tout noircy comme charbon» (*then the whole compost is turned like molten pitch, and is all blackened like coal*), with a black cloud rising in the vessel [PARIS.3 ¶14]. Batsdorff gives the signs: «une noirceur très noire ou très profonde, une odeur puante, mauvaise et infecte» (*a very black or very deep blackness, a stinking, bad and foul odour*), which «n'est pas sensible à l'odorat, mais seulement à l'entendement» (*is not perceptible to the sense of smell, but only to the understanding*) [PARIS.3 ¶15]. Fulcanelli says the descriptions of the raven of the coction «englobent tous les caractères des autres opérations» (*include all the characters of the other operations*) [PARIS.3 ¶12].

**Stated.** The coction is one wheel turned twice: «la coction linéaire et continue exige la double rotation d'une même roue» (*the linear and continuous coction requires the double rotation of one wheel*) [AMIENS ¶7]. «La première roue correspond à la phase humide de l'opération, — dénommée élixation, — où le composé demeure fondu, jusqu'à la formation d'une pellicule légère» (*The first wheel corresponds to the moist phase of the operation, called elixation, where the compound remains molten, until a light skin forms*) which thickens; «La seconde période, caractérisée par la sécheresse, — ou assation, — commence alors» (*The second period, characterised by dryness, or assation, then begins*) and ends «lorsque le contenu de l’oeuf, calciné, apparaît granuleux ou pulvérulent, en forme de cristaux, de sablon ou de cendre» (*when the contents of the egg, calcined, appear granular or powdery, as crystals, sand or ash*) [AMIENS ¶7]. The commentator of the *Lumière sortant des Ténèbres* is quoted: the philosopher cooks «à une chaleur douce et solaire, et dans un seul vaisseau, une seule vapeur qui s'épaissit peu à peu» (*at a gentle and solar heat, and in a single vessel, a single vapour which thickens little by little*) [AMIENS ¶8]. The two wheels at Amiens are «deux révolutions qui doivent agir successivement sur le composé pour lui assurer un premier degré de perfection» (*two revolutions that must act successively on the compound to assure it a first degree of perfection*), or, as Pernety prefers, the two natures in the *conversion* [AMIENS ¶6].

**Stated.** The régime is the real thing, the colours a veil. Etteilla's painting says «Ne vous en rapportez point trop à la couleur» (*Do not rely too much on the colour*) [PARIS.3 ¶33]. «les Philosophes ne parlent clairement que lorsqu'ils veulent écarter les profanes de leur Table ronde» (*the Philosophers speak clearly only when they want to keep the profane away from their Round Table*); of the limpid descriptions of régimes with their colours, «vous en devez conclure que ces observations si bien décrites sont fausses et chimériques» (*you must conclude that these so well described observations are false and chimerical*) [PARIS.3 ¶34]. A régime is «Tout simplement la manière de faire végéter, d'entretenir et d'accroître la vie que votre pierre a reçue dès sa naissance.» (*Quite simply the way of making vegetate, maintaining and increasing the life your stone received at its birth.*) [PARIS.3 ¶35]; «le Régime de la pierre, c’est-à-dire sa coction, en contient plusieurs autres, entendez plusieurs répétitions d’une même manière d’opérer» (*the Régime of the stone, that is its coction, contains several others, understand several repetitions of one way of operating*) [PARIS.3 ¶35]. The stone is fed by stages: «Donnez d'abord du lait ; le régime carné, plus substantiel, viendra ensuite.» (*Give milk first; the meat diet, more substantial, will come afterwards.*), and «Et n'omettez pas, après chaque digestion, de séparer les excréments, car votre pierre pourrait en être infectée» (*And do not omit, after each digestion, to separate the excrements, for your stone could be infected by them*) [PARIS.3 ¶36]. He says he will not reveal what the Philosophers left in the shadow about the régimes [PARIS.3 ¶35].

### 8e. The three reiterations and multiplication

**Stated.** The Bourges credence prescribes three repetitions of one process: it is necessary «réitérer trois fois la calcination de ce corps pour réaliser les trois œuvres philosophiques, selon la doctrine de Geber» (*to reiterate three times the calcination of this body to realise the three philosophic works, according to Geber's doctrine*) [BOURGES.2 ¶32]. «La première opération conduit d'abord au Soufre, ou médecine du premier ordre ; la seconde opération, absolument semblable à la première, fournit l'Elixir, ou médecine du second ordre» (*The first operation leads first to the Sulphur, or medicine of the first order; the second operation, exactly like the first, furnishes the Elixir, or medicine of the second order*), and «la troisième opération, exécutée comme les deux premières, donne la Pierre philosophale, médecine du troisième ordre» (*the third operation, performed like the first two, gives the philosophers' Stone, medicine of the third order*) [BOURGES.2 ¶32]. The roasted pomegranate with "3 R" marks «la nécessité des trois réitérations du même procédé» (*the necessity of the three reiterations of the same process*) [BOURGES.2 ¶28], as do the three *cuers* of Jacques Cœur, «les trois réitérations indispensables à la perfection totale des deux Magistères» (*the three reiterations indispensable to the total perfection of the two Magisteries*) [BOURGES.1 ¶4].

**Stated.** The working instruction, the most explicit in the book: «Faites donc un vase, puis votre composé ; scellez avec soin de manière qu'aucun esprit ne puisse s'exhaler ; chauffez le tout selon l'art jusqu'à complète calcination.» (*So make a vessel, then your compound; seal with care so that no spirit can escape; heat the whole according to the art until complete calcination.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶36]. «Remettez la portion pure de la poudre obtenue dans votre composé, que vous scellerez dans le même vase. Réitérez pour la troisième fois» (*Put the pure portion of the powder obtained back into your compound, which you will seal in the same vessel. Repeat for the third time*) [BOURGES.2 ¶36].

**Stated.** Multiplication is done with the mercury, by further coctions: «La multiplication, en effet, ne peut se réaliser qu'à l'aide du mercure, qui joue le rôle de patient dans l'Œuvre, et par coctions ou fixations successives.» (*Multiplication, in fact, can only be realised with the help of the mercury, which plays the role of patient in the Work, and by successive coctions or fixations.*) [PARIS.7 ¶23]. The dragon covered with solar circles «ne peut acquérir cette puissance que par une série de cuissons ultérieures avec le Soufre ou Or philosophique, ce qui constitue les multiplications» (*can only acquire this power by a series of later cookings with the Sulphur or philosophic Gold, which constitutes the multiplications*) [PARIS.7 ¶27]; this reading is offered as "vraisemblable", not imposed [PARIS.7 ¶27–28].

**Interpreted.** The "three works" of Bourges and Amiens (sulphur, elixir, stone by three like calcinations) and the "three periods" of the caduceus (red sulphur, Elixir, universal Medicine by graduated cooking) [PARIS.3 ¶18] describe the same ascent; the text does not say whether the "nine operations" of the ladder are these three works counted with their inner steps, or a different count.

## 9. Colours, odours and other signs

**Stated.** Three colours in fixed order: «Ces couleurs, au nombre de trois, se développent selon l'ordre invariable qui va du noir au rouge en passant par le blanc.» (*These colours, three in number, develop in the invariable order that goes from black to red by way of white.*) [PARIS.3 ¶25]; many intermediate shades are superficial and transient, while the essential colours are «des colorations dans la masse qui se traduisent au dehors et résorbent toutes les autres» (*colourings in the mass which show outwardly and absorb all the others*) [PARIS.3 ¶25]. The three roses of a cathedral show the same «processus circulaire allant des ténèbres, — figurées par l'absence de lumière et la couleur noire, — à la perfection de la lumière rubiconde, en passant par la couleur blanche» (*circular process going from darkness, figured by the absence of light and the colour black, to the perfection of the ruddy light, passing through the colour white*) [INTRO.6 ¶2]. Paracelsus is quoted for four colours, black, white, yellow, red, black being «la racine et l'origine des autres couleurs» (*the root and origin of the other colours*) [PARIS.3 ¶30].

**Stated.** At the white the matter is clean: «Elle se présente alors sous l'aspect de granulations solides ou de corpuscules brillants, à reflets adamantins et d'une blancheur éclatante.» (*It then appears as solid granules or shining corpuscles, with adamantine reflections and a dazzling whiteness.*) [PARIS.3 ¶28]. At the red: «Obtenue sous forme de cristal ou poudre rouge, volatile et fusible, la pierre philosophale devient pénétrante et idoine à guérir les lépreux» (*Obtained in the form of a red crystal or powder, volatile and fusible, the philosophers' stone becomes penetrating and fit to heal the lepers*), the lepers being the base metals [PARIS.3 ¶29].

**Stated.** Odour: Tollius is quoted, «l'odeur puante, chez les Philosophes, dénote la fixation ; au contraire, l'odeur agréable marque la volatilité» (*the stinking odour, among the Philosophers, denotes fixation; on the contrary, the pleasant odour marks volatility*) [PARIS.3 ¶38]; and the same author mocks those who seek «votre blanche colombe, votre aigle jaune et votre faysan rouge» (*your white dove, your yellow eagle and your red pheasant*) in glass vessels, and the stone «dans une chose fixe» (*in a fixed thing*) [PARIS.3 ¶36].

**Stated.** Other colour notes: green belongs to the solvent and the young matter, the old man Saturn «était jadis peint en vert, tandis que l'intérieur visible du Palais offrait une coloration pourpre» (*was formerly painted green, while the visible interior of the Palace showed a purple colouring*) [PARIS.5 ¶11]; of the two fighting children in the rose window «l'un est vert, l'autre violet gris» (*one is green, the other violet-grey*) [PARIS.5 ¶18]; «D'après Aristote, le Mercure a pour couleur emblématique le gris ou le violet» (*According to Aristotle, Mercury has grey or violet for its emblematic colour*) [INTRO.9 ¶15]; for the black Virgins «on ne brûlait que des cierges de couleur verte» (*only green candles were burned*) [INTRO.8 ¶12]; blue corresponds to black [PARIS.3 ¶31]; the Virgin's blue is «couleur symbolique de l'astre des nuits» (*the symbolic colour of the star of the nights*) [AMIENS ¶39]; the lion's original colour, which would decide between its readings, is lost: «Le lion de pierre conserve son secret !» (*The stone lion keeps its secret!*) [PARIS.4 ¶19].

## 10. Weights, proportions, temperatures and times

**Stated.** On weights the authors are reticent; Fulcanelli collects: Basile's «bailler un cygne blanc à l'homme double igné» (*give a white swan to the double fiery man*), Huginus's balance with a ratio «d'un à deux» (*of one to two*), the Cosmopolite's «Le poids de l'eau, dit-il, doit estre pluriel, et celui de la terre feuillée blanche ou rouge doit estre singulier.» (*The weight of the water, he says, must be plural, and that of the white or red leafy earth singular.*), and the *Aphorismes Basiliens*, «laquelle consiste au poids du corps, qui doit égaler l'esprit et l'âme presque de sa moitié» (*which consists in the weight of the body, which must equal the spirit and the soul by almost its half*) [PARIS.5 ¶6]. His own position: «il est donc indispensable de connaître exactement les proportions réciproques des composants, si l'on opère par l'ancienne voie» (*it is therefore indispensable to know exactly the reciprocal proportions of the components, if one operates by the ancient way*); but «il est possible de substituer l'or vulgaire au soufre métallique» (*it is possible to substitute vulgar gold for the metallic sulphur*), in which case «l'excès de dissolvant pouvant toujours être séparé par distillation, le poids se trouve ramené à une simple appréciation de consistance» (*the excess of solvent always being separable by distillation, the weight is reduced to a simple judgement of consistency*) [PARIS.5 ¶6]. «La balance, on le voit, constitue un indice précieux pour la détermination de la voie ancienne, de laquelle l'or paraît devoir être exclu.» (*The balance, as we see, is a precious indication for determining the ancient way, from which gold seems to have to be excluded.*) [PARIS.5 ¶6]. The egg's white and red are in a proportion like a bird's egg [BOURGES.2 ¶5]; Saturn's "ten parts of water" to one fruit is quoted [PARIS.5 ¶15]. RER is not a proportion [BOURGES.2 ¶35].

**Stated.** Temperatures of the coction: «Albert Poisson donne la base de 50° avec augmentation progressive jusque vers 300° centigrades.» (*Albert Poisson gives a base of 50° with progressive increase to about 300° centigrade.*), while Philalèthe calls «le degré de chaleur qui pourra tenir du plomb (327°) ou de l'étain en fusion (232°), et même encore plus forte» (*the degree of heat that can keep lead (327°) or tin (232°) molten, and even stronger*) a «chaleur tempérée» (*temperate heat*) [AMIENS ¶8], and demands «faire bouillir nuit et jour sans cesse» (*to boil night and day without cease*), else it is a digestion, not a coction [AMIENS ¶9]. Fulcanelli does not choose between these. In the dry way «Au quatrième degré de feu, en opérant par la voie sèche, il devient nécessaire d'entretenir une température voisine de 1200°, indispensable aussi dans la projection.» (*At the fourth degree of fire, operating by the dry way, it becomes necessary to keep a temperature near 1200°, indispensable also in the projection.*) [AMIENS ¶12]. The fire of the wheel must be even: de Nuysement's verse is given, «Et pour donner partout une chaleur égalle» (*And to give everywhere an equal heat*) [INTRO.6 ¶6], «Car en montant trop haut le ciel tu brusleras» (*For in climbing too high thou wilt burn the heaven*) [INTRO.6 ¶8].

**Stated.** Times: the conjunction toward the end of the fourth month of the first work [PARIS.6 ¶21]; Taurus the second month of the preparations [PARIS.4 ¶21]; for the dry way, the Encyclopédie's «pendant quatre jours» (*for four days*) [PARIS.7 ¶4], the *Azoth*'s «cet Art peut estre appris en moins de douze heures, et de l'espace de huit jours mené à perfection» (*this Art can be learned in less than twelve hours, and brought to perfection in the space of eight days*) [PARIS.7 ¶5], Philalèthe's «par notre voie, il ne faut pas plus d'une semaine» (*by our way, no more than a week is needed*) [PARIS.7 ¶6], Lenglet's «l'Œuvre s’accomplit en huit jours, au lieu qu’il faut près de dix-huit mois pour la première voye» (*the Work is accomplished in eight days, whereas nearly eighteen months are needed for the first way*) [PARIS.7 ¶6], and Henckel's report of the artist Elias's four days (after Helvétius), with Henckel's question «si ce que les alchymistes appellent des grands mois ne seroit pas autant de jours» (*whether what the alchemists call great months might not be so many days*) [PARIS.7 ¶10]. The book's own figures elsewhere are months [PARIS.6 ¶21; PARIS.4 ¶21]; it does not say which way they belong to.

## 11. The dry way and the humid way

**Stated.** «Peu d'alchimistes consentent à admettre la possibilité de deux voies, l'une courte et facile, nommée voie sèche, l'autre plus longue et plus ingrate, dite voie humide.» (*Few alchemists consent to admit the possibility of two ways, one short and easy, named the dry way, the other longer and more thankless, called the humid way.*) [PARIS.7 ¶3]. Pernety denies it, Huginus affirms each master had his own process [PARIS.7 ¶3]. Fulcanelli affirms both: «Chimiquement, rien ne s'oppose à ce qu'une méthode, employant la voie humide, ne puisse être remplacée par une autre utilisant des réactions sèches pour aboutir au même résultat.» (*Chemically, nothing prevents a method using the humid way from being replaced by another using dry reactions to reach the same result.*) [PARIS.7 ¶4], and the Saint-Marcel pillar «décrit la pratique la plus courte de notre Science» (*describes the shortest practice of our Science*) [PARIS.7 ¶1]. The Encyclopédie's dry way: «cuire le Sel céleste, qui est le mercure des Philosophes, avec un corps métallique terrestre, dans un creuset et à feu nu, pendant quatre jours» (*cook the celestial Salt, which is the mercury of the Philosophers, with an earthly metallic body, in a crucible and over naked fire, for four days*) [PARIS.7 ¶4]. «Cette voie abrégée, mais couverte d'un voile épais, a été nommée par les Sages le Régime de Saturne.» (*This shortened way, but covered with a thick veil, was named by the Sages the Régime of Saturn.*) [PARIS.7 ¶7]; the crucible replaces the glass [PARIS.7 ¶7]; Trismosin is quoted, «Fais un feu dans ton verre, c’est-à-dire dans la terre qui le tient enfermé.» (*Make a fire in thy glass, that is, in the earth that holds it enclosed.*) [PARIS.7 ¶7]. Cyliani is quoted: «il ne faut que deux matières de même origine, l'une volatile, l'autre fixe ; qu'il y a deux voies, la voie sèche et la voie humide» (*only two matters of the same origin are needed, one volatile, the other fixed; that there are two ways, the dry way and the humid way*), and he follows the humid «par devoir» (*by duty*) though the dry «se fait avec une matière unique» (*is done with a single matter*) [PARIS.7 ¶9]. Henckel's guess is a method «à tenir longtemps les matières dans le plus grand degré de fluidité, ce qu'on obtiendroit par un feu violent, entretenu par l'action des soufflets» (*keeping the matters a long time in the greatest degree of fluidity, which would be obtained by a violent fire kept up by bellows*) [PARIS.7 ¶10]. Philalèthe's dry way is for the poor [PARIS.7 ¶6].

**Interpreted.** The "first way" and "second way" of the solution (Alkahest on alchemical gold, versus "our mercury" on vulgar gold, the latter making the animated mercury) [PARIS.5 ¶14], and the "ancient way" from which gold seems excluded [PARIS.5 ¶6], are not said to coincide with dry and humid; the text treats them as a different division (by what is dissolved and by what), and I leave them separate.

## 12. The result and its uses

**Stated.** Raw and finished are one substance at two degrees: «on appelle Toison d'or la matière préparée pour l'Œuvre, ainsi que le résultat final» (*the Golden Fleece is the name of the matter prepared for the Work, as well as of the final result*), and «Pierre des Philosophes et Pierre Philosophale sont donc deux choses semblables, en espèce et en origine, mais la première est crue, tandis que la seconde, qui en dérive, est parfaitement cuite et digérée.» (*Stone of the Philosophers and Philosophers' Stone are therefore two similar things, in kind and in origin, but the first is raw, while the second, which derives from it, is perfectly cooked and digested.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶20]. The stone is the *Absolu*: Cnossos's *Absolum* «est voisin d'Absolu, qui est le nom par lequel les alchimistes anciens désignaient la pierre philosophale» (*is close to Absolute, which is the name by which the ancient alchemists designated the philosophers' stone*) [INTRO.5 ¶7].

**Stated.** Its uses: transmutation, «guérir les lépreux» [PARIS.3 ¶29]; the Elixir or stone «assure à l'heureux possesseur de ce trésor le triple apanage du savoir, de la fortune et de la santé» (*assures the happy possessor of this treasure the triple appanage of knowledge, fortune and health*) [PARIS.5 ¶4]; the *pierre au blanc* is «la transmutation des métaux vils en argent» (*the transmutation of base metals into silver*) [BOURGES.1 ¶3]; the *Médecine universelle* is the third period of the compound [PARIS.3 ¶18]. Projection needs about 1200° [AMIENS ¶12]. The stone keeps its power by multiplications with the mercury [PARIS.7 ¶23, ¶27]. The iron coffer beside Saint Christopher is «le résultat» (*the result*) of the hidden matter he carries [INTRO.9 ¶16].

**Gap.** No projection is described, no quantity of metal tinged is given, and no medicinal use is described beyond the words *Elixir*, *Médecine universelle* and «santé» [PARIS.3 ¶18; PARIS.5 ¶4].

## 13. What is required of the worker

**Stated.** Patience first: the nine-rung ladder is the «hiéroglyphe de la patience» (*hieroglyph of patience*) [PARIS.1 ¶3], and Valois is quoted that patience is the ladder and humility the door [PARIS.1 ¶3]. Observation: of the alchemist of Notre-Dame, «Il ne médite pas, il observe.» (*He does not meditate, he observes.*) [PARIS.1 ¶14]; the Conclusion names observation, reasoning and meditation as the way to *Savoir* [CONCL ¶8]. The Conclusion lists the qualities: «La Science mystérieuse réclame beaucoup de justesse, d'exactitude, de perspicacité dans l'observation des faits, un esprit sain, logique et pondéré, une imagination vive sans exaltation, un cœur ardent et pur.» (*The mysterious Science demands much accuracy, exactness, perspicacity in observing facts, a sound, logical and balanced mind, a lively imagination without exaltation, an ardent and pure heart.*) [CONCL ¶7]; it demands that its aspirants ask Nature herself for «la pratique de ses travaux» (*the practice of its works*) [CONCL ¶7]; the disciple must study the classical texts «jusqu'à ce qu'il ait acquis assez de clairvoyance pour discerner les points obscurs du manuel opératoire» (*until he has acquired enough clear sight to discern the obscure points of the operative manual*) [CONCL ¶4]; «Nul ne peut prétendre à la possession du grand Secret, s'il n'accorde son existence au diapason des recherches entreprises.» (*No one can claim possession of the great Secret unless he tunes his existence to the pitch of the researches undertaken.*) [CONCL ¶5]. The four steps are *Savoir*, *Pouvoir*, *Oser*, *Se taire* [CONCL ¶9–16]; power comes from «L'imitation naïve des procédés naturels, l'habileté jointe à l'ingéniosité, les lumières d'une longue expérience» (*The naive imitation of natural processes, skill joined to ingenuity, the lights of long experience*) [CONCL ¶10]. The successful Adept «restera fidèle à son vœu de silence» (*will remain faithful to his vow of silence*) [CONCL ¶14]. Nature does not open to all: «La Nature n'ouvre pas à tous, indistinctement, la porte du sanctuaire.» (*Nature does not open the door of the sanctuary to all without distinction.*) [CONCL ¶3]. The secret of the fire «dépend du Père des Lumières» (*depends on the Father of Lights*) [PARIS.3 ¶20]; Isis alone «est la dispensatrice de la Révélation et de l'Initiation» (*is the dispenser of Revelation and Initiation*) [AMIENS ¶39]. Enthusiasm and faith are aids but must be subjected to reason and «au critérium de l'expérience» (*to the criterion of experience*) [PARIS.7 ¶29]. Thanks are owed to the Creator alone, and the reader is asked only to take for others the same pains [BOURGES.2 ¶36]. The work is done out of sunlight [AMIENS ¶37], with the prudence and silence the Amiens figure's removed shoes signify [AMIENS ¶12].

## 14. What the text does not say

- The common name of the subject: given only as a near-homophone of *chêne* and through *cuer*/*le fer* and the galette; never written out [BOURGES.2 ¶21, ¶23; BOURGES.1 ¶4].
- The nature and making of the secret fire; Fulcanelli names three books and stops [PARIS.3 ¶20].
- The body from which the May dew is drawn and how: oath [PARIS.6 ¶25].
- What RER is, beyond "a matter plus half of itself or another"; the realgar is a supposition [BOURGES.2 ¶34–35].
- The régimes of the coction, left as the Philosophers left them [PARIS.3 ¶35].
- The weights, "enveloped in similitudes" even in the sincerest authors [PARIS.5 ¶6].
- Which of the quoted temperatures and durations he endorses [AMIENS ¶8; PARIS.7 ¶4–10].
- The two biblical scenes above the Paris contreforts: «nous éviterons d'en parler, afin de ne point encourir le reproche d'une interprétation arbitraire» (*we shall avoid speaking of them, so as not to incur the reproach of an arbitrary interpretation*) [PARIS.2 ¶26].
- The meaning of the Saint Thomas blazon, described but not read [PARIS.8 ¶6–7].
- The meaning of the lion of the tenth relief, undecidable without its colour [PARIS.4 ¶19].
- After the night argument: «Nous pourrions dire beaucoup plus, mais nous estimons avoir assez parlé.» (*We could say much more, but we consider we have said enough.*) [AMIENS ¶39].

## 15. What Canseliet adds

Canseliet's three prefaces are a later layer in his own voice. What they add to the account of the Work, each labelled as his:

- **The key is a colour.** Canseliet says the key to the major arcanum is given by one of the book's plates, «Et cette clef consiste tout uniment en une couleur, manifestée à l'artisan dès le premier travail.» (*And this key consists simply in a colour, shown to the craftsman from the first work.*) [PREF1 ¶11]. He attributes the assurance to Fulcanelli (QUOTED: Fulcanelli, through Canseliet) and does not name the plate or the colour.
- **Fulcanelli had not finished the Work in 1922.** Canseliet states that when the book was written «Fulcanelli n'avait pas reçu Le Don de Dieu» (*Fulcanelli had not received the Gift of God*) [PREF2 ¶1], and prints a letter from an unnamed correspondent congratulating him on it later [PREF2 ¶6–16].
- **The end of the fire.** The letter gives one operative sign of completion: the fire goes out only when the work is done and «toute la masse tinctoriale imprègne le verre» (*the whole tinctorial mass impregnates the glass*), which becomes luminous like the sun [PREF2 ¶11] (QUOTED: the letter's author).
- **Mercury alone.** The sentence «Celui qui sait faire l'Oeuvre par le seul mercure a trouvé ce qu'il y a de plus parfait» (*He who knows how to do the Work by mercury alone has found what is most perfect*) is printed after the letter's signature, in the letter's type, with the gloss that this means receiving the light and accomplishing the Magistery [PREF2 ¶17] (voice UNCLEAR: the letter's author or Canseliet); the main text gives the same sentence as the authors' [BOURGES.2 ¶28].
- **The star is double.** From a notebook of Fulcanelli's talks: «Notre étoile est seule et pourtant elle est double.» (*Our star is single and yet it is double.*) [PREF2 ¶21] (QUOTED: Fulcanelli). Canseliet explains that the star is first seen in the *miroir de l'art* or mercury and afterwards in the *ciel chimique*, where it shines more discreetly [PREF2 ¶20, ¶26–27]; the one on the mercury is a reflection of the one that crowns the newborn [PREF2 ¶26].
- **Separation of light from darkness.** The artist must separate, in his small world, «à l'aide du feu secret et de l'esprit universel» (*with the help of the secret fire and the universal spirit*), the crystalline, luminous parts from the dense, dark ones [PREF2 ¶32]; this is the first of the *Grands Jours de Salomon* and shows what the philosophic earth and the heaven of the Sages are [PREF2 ¶32].
- **A recipe and a caution.** Canseliet quotes Philalèthe's four parts of fiery dragon to nine of magnet, melted by Vulcan, skimmed, purged three times by fire and salt [PREF2 ¶37] (QUOTED: Philalèthe). He states that the star is not a sign special to the Great Work: it appears in many lesser operations, always marking a partial or total transformation of the body it settles on [PREF2 ¶40]. He quotes Helvetius's silver star on lead under spirit of salt, and warns that the account lacks two essential data, the exact composition of the acid and the prior operations on the metal [PREF2 ¶41–42]; ordinary lead will never take the look of pumice under cold muriatic acid, so several preparations are needed to swell it and bring it to fermentation [PREF2 ¶42]. Lead, dead by reduction, revives when «patiemment nourri de substance ignée» (*patiently fed with fiery substance*) [PREF2 ¶43].
- **Solve et coagula and putrefaction.** The whole mode of operating is in «Solve et coagula ; dissous et coagule» (*Solve et coagula; dissolve and coagulate*) [PREF3 ¶12], and putrefaction is a law for every matter, the dark phase that opens operative alchemy [PREF3 ¶15]; the foul odour of the dark dissolution is followed by a sweet perfume, as Morien and Lulle say [PREF3 ¶22].
- **The two ways in Saint Marcel's legend.** The dragon's choice between the deserts and the sea is, for Canseliet, the allegory «dans laquelle se distinguent les deux voies sèche et humide» (*in which the dry and humid ways are distinguished*) [PREF3 ¶43].
- **Cabale, not kabbale.** He insists the book's phonetic cabala (from *caballus*) is not the Hebrew kabbalah [PREF2 ¶45]; this bears on method rather than on the Work.
