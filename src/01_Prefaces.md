# Prefaces by Eugène Canseliet

## PREF1 — The vanished Master and the key in a colour

Canseliet, signing as disciple, declines to analyse his Master's book and leaves its judgement to readers and to the Brothers of Heliopolis [PREF1 ¶1]. The Author has long been gone; Canseliet says he owes him everything, and that friends had hoped Fulcanelli would resolve the mysterious *Verbum dimissum* [PREF1 ¶2]. According to the philosophical Tradition as Canseliet states it, the spark of Revelation consumes the old man: name, family and country fall to dust, and a new personality is reborn from the ashes like the phoenix [PREF1 ¶3]. Fulcanelli disappeared when the fated hour struck and the Sign was fulfilled; his thought survives, shut in these pages as in a sanctuary [PREF1 ¶4; PREF1 ¶5].

Through him, Canseliet says, the Gothic cathedral gives up its secret: how the ancestors cut the *first stone* of its foundations, the stone on which Jesus built his Church and on which all truth, philosophy and religion rest [PREF1 ¶6]. Medieval monuments hold the same positive truth and scientific basis as the pyramids of Egypt, the temples of Greece, the Roman catacombs and the Byzantine basilicas [PREF1 ¶7; PREF1 ¶8]. Hermetists will find more: from the confrontation of the Book and the Building the Spirit is released and the letter dies; Fulcanelli made the first effort, the hermetists must make the last [PREF1 ¶9].

Canseliet then reveals, on the Author's assurance given more than ten years earlier and in obedience to his last wishes, that the key of the major arcanum is given without fiction by one of the figures of this book [PREF1 ¶11]. «Et cette clef consiste tout uniment en une couleur, manifestée à l’artisan dès le premier travail.» (*And this key consists quite simply in a colour, shown to the worker from the first labour.*) [PREF1 ¶11] No Philosopher, as far as he knows, has pointed out this essential point [PREF1 ¶11]. He thanks the painter Julien Champagne, to whom the Master entrusted the plates [PREF1 ¶12]. Signed E. Canseliet, F. C. H., October 1925 [PREF1 ¶13; PREF1 ¶14; PREF1 ¶15].

## PREF2 — The Master's anonymity, the letter, and the double star

Canseliet says the book was written in 1922, when Fulcanelli had not yet received the *Gift of God* but was so near the supreme Illumination that he chose to wait and to stay anonymous [PREF2 ¶1]. The Master willed absolutely that his real identity remain hidden behind the pseudonym required by Tradition, and Canseliet holds that no other name can ever replace it [PREF2 ¶2]. He presents this renunciation as an ethical discipline by which a new Adept aligns his destiny with his few predecessors, beacons along the road; like most ancient Adepts, Fulcanelli shed the *old man* and left only the trace of his name [PREF2 ¶3].

Oral teaching from master to disciple, Canseliet says, prevails over all other; Fulcanelli was initiated so, as Canseliet was by him, though Cyliani had already opened the door of the labyrinth to Canseliet when his booklet was reprinted in 1915 [PREF2 ¶4]. Canseliet corrects himself: Basile Valentin was his Master's *first* initiator, not his *true* one [PREF2 ¶5]. The true initiator was the unnamed addressee of a letter whose writer's signature had been scraped off; Fulcanelli kept it for years like a talisman, as written proof of that initiator's triumph, stained by the dust of his ever-burning furnace [PREF2 ¶5; PREF2 ¶6].

The letter (voice of its writer) congratulates his old friend on having received the *gift of God*, an arcanum so simple that reason alone cannot find it [PREF2 ¶8]. It says the fire goes out only when the work is complete and the tinctorial mass impregnates the glass, which, decantation after decantation, becomes saturated and luminous like the sun [PREF2 ¶11]. It speaks of the diadem of true royalty, and of the friend's blue mantle widened into the whole azure of the sky [PREF2 ¶12]. The writer's wife dreamed of a man wrapped in all the colours of the prism and raised to the sun [PREF2 ¶13]. The writer warns that one who has hailed the *morning star* may almost be said to lose sight and reason, fascinated by that false light and thrown into the abyss [PREF2 ¶13]. A line set after the signature says that whoever can make the Work by *mercury alone* has received the light and accomplished the Magistery [PREF2 ¶17].

Canseliet takes up that warning, which seems to contradict his own earlier study on the *Toison d'Or*: there he called the star the great sign of the Work, which seals the matter and shows the artist he has found the light of the wise, not of fools, and noted that it is first seen in the *mirror of art*, or mercury, before being found in the *chemical sky* [PREF2 ¶18; PREF2 ¶19; PREF2 ¶20]. He copies a note from Fulcanelli's talks: «Notre étoile est seule et pourtant elle est double.» (*Our star is single, and yet it is double.*) [PREF2 ¶21] One must tell its real imprint from its image; it shines more in daylight than at night [PREF2 ¶21]. He sets beside it Basile Valentin's two stars given to man to lead him to Wisdom, and asks whether a small alchemical painting at the Franciscan convent of Cimiez does not show these two stars, with the motto *Cum luce salutem*, glossed in the text as "with the light, salvation" [PREF2 ¶22; PREF2 ¶23; PREF2 ¶24]. Meditating these gives the key with which Cyliani opens the temple; failing that, one should reread Fulcanelli [PREF2 ¶25].

Canseliet's reading: the two stars are really one [PREF2 ¶26]. The star over the mystic Virgin, who is both *our mother* and the *hermetic sea*, announces conception but is only a reflection in the *mirror of Wisdom*; the Virgin is also called *stella matutina*, the *morning star*, so the morning star is this reflected image [PREF2 ¶26]. This visible but ungraspable star attests the other star, which crowned the divine Child at his birth, as Saint Chrysostom says the Magi's star came to rest on the Saviour's head [PREF2 ¶26]. That other is a nocturnal star shining dimly at the pole of the *hermetic sky*, the terrestrial sky of which Vinceslas Lavinius de Moravie and Jacobus Tollius speak [PREF2 ¶27; PREF2 ¶28; PREF2 ¶29; PREF2 ¶30]. Sky and earth, mixed in the original Chaos, do not differ in substance or essence, only in quality, quantity and virtue [PREF2 ¶31]. The chaotic, sterile alchemical earth contains the philosophical sky; the artist, with the secret fire and the universal spirit, must separate its pure, luminous parts from its dense, dark ones, drawing light from darkness, the work of the first of the *Great Days of Solomon* [PREF2 ¶32].

Philalèthe, in the *Entrée ouverte au Palais fermé du Roi*, calls the star's appearance the miracle of the world, the higher virtues joined in the lower, a sign the Sages saw in the East; follow it to the Cradle [PREF2 ¶33; PREF2 ¶34; PREF2 ¶35]. He then gives the manner of working: four parts of the fiery dragon hiding the magic steel, nine of the Magnet, joined by burning Vulcan; skim the foam, reject the crust, keep the kernel, purge three times by fire and salt, which is easy if Saturn has seen his image in the mirror of Mars [PREF2 ¶36; PREF2 ¶37]. The Almighty sets his royal seal on this Work [PREF2 ¶39].

The star, Canseliet adds, is not peculiar to the Great Work; it appears in many lesser processes, always marking a partial or total transformation of the body it rests on [PREF2 ¶40]. He translates Helvetius's *Vitulus Aureus*: a poor goldsmith of The Hague, Gril, poured a specially prepared spirit of salt on lead in a glass jar; after two weeks a silvery star floated on it, while the lead below turned ash-grey and spongy; when the liquid dried in the July heat the star settled on the lead, and on cupelling, one pound of lead gave twelve ounces of silver and from these two ounces of gold [PREF2 ¶41; PREF2 n2]. Canseliet warns that two data are missing, the exact composition of the acid and the prior operations on the metal: ordinary lead never becomes spongy in cold muriatic acid without preparations, dilation, purification and fermentation [PREF2 ¶42]. Blaise de Vigenère and Naxagoras dwelt on a long preliminary coction; common lead is dead because it suffered reduction (a great flame, says Basile Valentin, devours a small fire), but, Canseliet says, lead patiently fed with igneous substance revives into a living philosophical body [PREF2 ¶43].

He dwells on the star because Fulcanelli, at the very start of his text, dwells on the *Star*, the mineral *Theophany* that announces the secret buried in religious buildings [PREF2 ¶44]. This second edition follows the 1926 printing of 300 copies and adds three drawings by Julien Champagne and original notes by Fulcanelli, unchanged [PREF2 ¶44]. The book's merit, for Canseliet, is to have restored the phonetic *cabale*, which must not be confused with the transpositions and calculations of the Jewish *kabbale*: he derives *cabale* from Greek χαβάλλης or Latin *caballus*, horse, and *kabbale* from Hebrew *Kabbalah*, tradition; Fulcanelli recovered the lost key of the *Gaye Science*, the Language of the Gods or of the Birds [PREF2 ¶45]. Dated Savignies, August 1957, the preface closes with sayings of Nicolas Valois, Limonjon de Saint-Didier and Fulcanelli, the last urging us to dissect words, break their bark and free the spirit within [PREF2 ¶46; PREF2 ¶47; PREF2 ¶48; PREF2 ¶49; PREF2 ¶50; PREF2 ¶51; PREF2 ¶52].

## PREF3 — Putrefaction, and the statue of Saint Marcel

After an epigraph from François Villon's *Testament*, Canseliet welcomes Jean-Jacques Pauvert's reissue, now illustrated with photographs of the sculptures that Julien Champagne had drawn; Champagne knew Fulcanelli from 1905, ten years before Canseliet [PREF3 ¶1; PREF3 ¶8; PREF3 ¶10]. Alchemy, he says, is the search for and awakening of the Life asleep under the thick envelope of beings and the bark of things, a permanent purification to final perfection on both planes, matter and spirit [PREF3 ¶11]. Its method is the maxim *Solve et coagula*, "dissolve and coagulate": simple and linear, demanding sincerity, resolution, patience and imagination [PREF3 ¶12]. Citing the *Tourbe* through Baleus, the mother pities her child but the child is hard on her; Canseliet sets this family drama in the alchemical microcosm [PREF3 ¶13].

He attacks the Church, which possesses the Word that Freemasonry still seeks as the *lost word* (*verbum dimissum*) yet is abandoning it, upsetting the Latin mass [PREF3 ¶14]. Worse, it now accepts the cremation it once refused, despising the law of fertile putrefaction by which life continues in all matter under the appearance of death, the dark phase that opens operative alchemy's possibilities [PREF3 ¶15]. He cites Saint John on the grain of wheat that must die to bear fruit, and on Lazarus, whose body already smelled after four days [PREF3 ¶16; PREF3 ¶17; PREF3 ¶18]. He mocks the hygienic argument for cremation, already made a century earlier in the age of Comte and Littré [PREF3 ¶19]. Against it he sets the physicians Orfila and Devergie, who observed that a decomposing body's smell finally becomes almost aromatic, and compares this with Morien and Raymond Lulle, for whom the foul odour (*odor teter*) of the dark dissolution is followed by the sweetest perfume, proper to life and heat [PREF3 ¶20; PREF3 ¶21; PREF3 ¶22].

The rest of the preface defends Fulcanelli's correction of Louis-François Cambriel over the statue of Marcel, bishop of Paris, once on the trumeau of Notre-Dame's Saint Anne porch, now at the Musée de Cluny, replaced by a copy by Viollet-le-Duc and Lassus [PREF3 ¶23]. Cambriel had described the bishop with a finger at his lips, enjoining silence, and his sketch showed him bearded, with a mitre bearing four small crosses and a short crosier [PREF3 ¶24; PREF3 ¶25]. Fulcanelli, quoted, found the original beardless, its mitre plain, its crosier resting on the dragon's mouth, and the gesture of the *Mutus Liber* and Harpocrates pure invention [PREF3 ¶26]. «Saint Marcel est représenté bénissant, dans une attitude pleine de noblesse, le front incliné, l’avant-bras replié, la main au niveau de l’épaule, l’index et le médius levés.» (*Saint Marcel is shown blessing, in an attitude full of nobility, brow bowed, forearm bent, hand at shoulder height, index and middle finger raised.*) [PREF3 ¶26] This is section VII of the Paris chapter [PREF3 ¶27].

About three years later Grillot de Givry, in *Le Musée des Sorciers*, held that the crosier plunging into the dragon's mouth signifies that a celestial ray is needed to kindle the athanor's fire, and claimed that a sixteenth-century statue with a deliberately shortened crosier had stood on the portal until about 1860 [PREF3 ¶27; PREF3 ¶28]. Canseliet calls this third statue unknown to the archives; Grillot's photograph shows no episcopal crosier at all, only a thick bar without a volute, while Cambriel's own engraving shows a shortened but complete crosier [PREF3 ¶29; PREF3 ¶31; PREF3 ¶32]. Grillot, he says, aimed at Fulcanelli's correction without naming it, though the book had appeared in June 1926 [PREF3 ¶33]. Marcel Clavelle (Jean Reyor) in 1932, and recently Bernard Husson, revived the charge, Reyor arguing that Cambriel was deceived by an unfaithful copy, not a deceiver [PREF3 ¶34; PREF3 ¶35; PREF3 ¶36]. Canseliet suggests the photograph may show another place or another figure [PREF3 ¶37; PREF3 ¶38; PREF3 ¶39].

Many saints have a dragon, but Saint Marcel alone touches its head with his crosier [PREF3 ¶40]. Canseliet translates his legend from Gérard Dubois d'Orléans: a serpent fed on the corpse of a noble lady of ill repute; Marcel struck its head with his staff, threw his stole over it, led it in a circle before the people, and ordered it to the desert or the sea [PREF3 ¶41; PREF3 ¶42; PREF3 n1]. Canseliet sees here a hermetic allegory distinguishing the two ways, dry and humid, matching the 50th emblem of Michel Maier's *Atalanta Fugiens*, where a dragon enlaces a woman lying in her violated grave [PREF3 ¶43]. Against the sixteenth-century replacement he cites Esprit Gobineau de Montluisant, who on 20 May 1640 saw on the middle pillar a bishop putting his crosier into the mouth of a dragon rising from wavy water, in which a king's head with triple crown seems to drown and re-emerge [PREF3 ¶45; PREF3 ¶46]. Reyor then moved the statue to Louis XIV's reign, and Husson called the date a misprint corrected to the seventeenth century in the caption, a correction Canseliet says the caption does not contain [PREF3 ¶47]. Canseliet closes by calling Fulcanelli's reproach of Cambriel well founded and the controversy settled; Savignies, July 1964 [PREF3 ¶48; PREF3 ¶49; PREF3 ¶50; PREF3 ¶51].
