# Bourges

## BOURGES.1 — The Jacques-Cœur palace

Besides its cathedral, Bourges has two remarkable buildings: the Jacques-Cœur palace and the Hôtel Lallemant [BOURGES.1 ¶1]. The palace was once «un véritable musée d’emblèmes hermétiques» (*a true museum of hermetic emblems*) [BOURGES.1 ¶2]. Vandalism and its successive uses have ruined the interior, and only the façade is still in its first state [BOURGES.1 ¶2].

Jacques Cœur, *grand argentier* of Charles VII, who built the palace in the fifteenth century, had the reputation of a proven Adept [BOURGES.1 ¶3]. David de Planis-Campy credits him with «le don précieux de la pierre au blanc» (*the precious gift of the stone at the white*), which Fulcanelli glosses as the transmutation of base metals into silver; this may be the source of the title *argentier* [BOURGES.1 ¶3]. Fulcanelli reads the motto «A vaillans cuers riens impossible» (*To valiant hearts nothing impossible*) by cabalistic rules [BOURGES.1 ¶4]. Read as *cuer* in the spelling of the period, it yields three things: the universal Spirit (*rayon de lumière*, "ray of light"); the common name of the basic matter worked (*le fer*, "iron"); and the three reiterations needed to perfect the two Magisteries (*les trois cuers*) [BOURGES.1 ¶4]. He concludes that Jacques Cœur practised alchemy, or at least watched the white stone being made «par le fer “essencifié” et trois fois cuit» (*by "essencified" iron cooked three times*) [BOURGES.1 ¶4].

The scallop shell and the heart always appear together on the palace [BOURGES.1 ¶5]. They may be a rebus on the owner's name, but the scallop was also the pilgrims' badge of Saint-Jacques [BOURGES.1 ¶5]. Fulcanelli says the *Mérelle de Compostelle* (pl. XXXIX) «sert, dans la symbolique secrète, à désigner le principe Mercure» (*serves, in the secret symbolism, to designate the principle Mercury*), which is also called the *Voyageur* (Traveller) or *Pèlerin* (Pilgrim) [BOURGES.1 ¶5]. A footnote adds that Mercury is the Philosophers' «eau benoîte» (*blessed water*), and that large shells were once used to hold holy water [BOURGES.1 n1]. Everyone who undertakes the work and seeks the star (*compos stella*) wears the shell mystically [BOURGES.1 ¶5]. Flamel, in his *Figures Hiéroglyphiques*, describes the same allegorical journey to ask «Monsieur Jacques de Galice» for help [BOURGES.1 ¶5]. Every alchemist must make this journey, half by land and half by sea: «Pèlerins d’abord, pilotes ensuite.» (*Pilgrims first, pilots afterwards.*) [BOURGES.1 ¶5]

The restored chapel keeps nothing of the old symbolism except a ceiling of angels and a sculpted Annunciation [BOURGES.1 ¶6]. The most curious piece is a corbel in the *Trésor* room, said to show Tristan and Yseult (pl. XL) [BOURGES.1 ¶7]. Fulcanelli accepts the subject. He holds that the Round Table romances are traditional hermetic legends, renewed from the Greek fables, that pass on ancient science under the veil of fiction [BOURGES.1 ¶7]. At the centre, a hollow cubic casket sits at the foot of a leafy tree that hides King Marc's crowned head; Tristan and Yseult stand on either side, gazing at the hollowed stone between them [BOURGES.1 ¶8].

For Fulcanelli, the Tristan myth repeats that of Theseus: Tristan kills the *Morhout* as Theseus kills the Minotaur [BOURGES.1 ¶9]. It is «l’hiéroglyphe de fabrication du Lion vert» (*the hieroglyph of the making of the Green Lion*), whence Tristan's name *Léonois*; Basile Valentin teaches the same thing as the fight between the eagle and the dragon [BOURGES.1 ¶9]. This fight is «Ce combat singulier des corps chimiques dont la combinaison procure le dissolvant secret (et le vase du composé)» (*This single combat of the chemical bodies whose combination yields the secret solvent (and the vessel of the compound)*) [BOURGES.1 ¶9]. Fulcanelli lists its other forms: Cadmos, Apollo and Python, Jason, Horus and Typhon, Hercules and the Hydra, Perseus, saints Michael, George and Marcel, the fox and the cock of the Paris medallions, Cyliani's alchemist and dragon, and the red and green serpents [BOURGES.1 ¶9].

The solvent allows the *réincrudation* of natural gold: its softening and its return to its first state, in a saline, friable and very fusible form [BOURGES.1 ¶10]. A footnote defines the term as «rendre cru, c'est-à-dire remettre dans un état antérieur à celui qui caractérise la maturité, rétrograder» (*to make raw, that is, to put back into a state earlier than the one that marks maturity, to go backwards*) [BOURGES.1 n2]. This is the rejuvenation of the king, personified here by Tristan, King Marc's nephew; chemically, uncle and nephew are one and the same thing [BOURGES.1 ¶10]. Gold loses its crown when it loses its colour, and later gains a second one «infiniment plus noble que la première» (*infinitely nobler than the first*), as Limojon de Saint-Didier says [BOURGES.1 ¶10]. Hence the old king stays hidden in the tree that grows out of the stone [BOURGES.1 ¶10]. The queen is wife to both the old man and the young hero, which keeps «la tradition hermétique qui fait du roi, de la reine et de l’amant la triade minérale du Grand Œuvre» (*the hermetic tradition that makes the king, the queen and the lover the mineral triad of the Great Work*) [BOURGES.1 ¶10]. The tree behind Tristan is so loaded with huge fruit that Fulcanelli would compare the forest of *Mort-Roi* to the Garden of the Hesperides [BOURGES.1 ¶10].

## BOURGES.2 — The Hôtel Lallemant

### The "kitchen" and the vessel

The Hôtel Lallemant is perfectly preserved, with no restoration or damage to its symbolic decoration [BOURGES.2 ¶1]. Because it is built on a slope, a vaulted stairway leads up to the inner courtyard [BOURGES.2 ¶2]. The guide shows a low, dark basement room as the "kitchen", pointing to a corbel of a clerk holding a pestle [BOURGES.2 ¶3]. Fulcanelli doubts this: the fireplace is too small to cook in but would hold the tower of an athanor [BOURGES.2 ¶3]. Such a room suits alchemical work, «d’où la lumière solaire, ennemie de toute génération, doit être exclue» (*from which sunlight, the enemy of all generation, must be kept out*) [BOURGES.2 ¶4]. No mortar is shown, and the "pestle" is really «un matras à long col» (*a long-necked flask*); its hollow, whistle-cut end proves that it is a vessel (pl. XLI) [BOURGES.2 ¶4].

This secret vessel bears names chosen to mislead the profane, usually *œuf philosophique* (philosophic egg) and *Lion vert* (Green Lion) [BOURGES.2 ¶5]. The text states that by *œuf* the Sages mean their compound placed in its own vessel; it holds the philosophic *rebis*, made of white and red as a bird's egg is [BOURGES.2 ¶5]. No text has ever explained *Lion vert* [BOURGES.2 ¶5]. Batsdorff calls the cooking vessel the Green Lion without giving a reason [BOURGES.2 ¶5]. The Cosmopolite says that in the Work «il y a ce seul Lion verd qui ferme et ouvre les sept sceaux indissolubles des sept esprits métalliques» (*there is this one Green Lion which shuts and opens the seven indissoluble seals of the seven metallic spirits*) [BOURGES.2 ¶5]. G. Aurach's manuscript shows a glass flask half full of a green liquor, whose name gives its colour; this is Basile Valentin's *vitriol* [BOURGES.2 ¶5]. The third figure of the *Toison d’Or* shows the same thing [BOURGES.2 ¶5]. Fulcanelli judges that Ripley comes closest to the truth: «Il n’entre qu’un seul corps immonde dans notre magistère ; les Philosophes l’appellent communément Lion vert. C’est le milieu ou moyen pour joindre les teintures entre le soleil et la lune.» (*Only one unclean body enters our magistery; the Philosophers commonly call it Green Lion. It is the medium or means for joining the tinctures between the sun and the moon.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶5]

So the vessel is considered in two ways: by its matter, as the *vase de nature* (vessel of nature), and by its form, as the *vase de l’art* (vessel of art) [BOURGES.2 ¶6]. The citations above concern its nature [BOURGES.2 ¶6]. As to form, the egg may be spherical or oval, made of clear glass without bubbles, thick enough to resist internal pressure, and with a neck that can easily be sealed at the enameller's lamp [BOURGES.2 ¶6]. Some authors recommend *verre de Lorraine*, which a footnote describes as moulded glass with thick, even walls [BOURGES.2 ¶6; BOURGES.2 n2]. The laboratory and the vessel, where the Adept works and where nature acts, are the two certainties that make the house a philosophical dwelling [BOURGES.2 ¶7].

### Saint Christopher

In the loggia, a painted stone relief shows Saint Christopher setting the Child Jesus down on the bank of the torrent, while a hermit with a lantern comes out of his hut at night (pl. XLII) [BOURGES.2 ¶8]. Fulcanelli sees an analogy between the giant who carries Christ and «la matière qui porte l’or (Χρυσοφορος)» (*the matter that carries gold*), which plays the same role in the Work [BOURGES.2 ¶9]. He transcribes the legend as Amédée de Ponthieu tells it after Jacques de Voragine, italicising the words that bear on the work, its conditions and its materials [BOURGES.2 ¶9].

In the legend, the dull-witted giant Offerus *travels* to serve the greatest king on earth [BOURGES.2 ¶10]. He leaves a king who fears the devil to serve the devil, a black chief leading horsemen dressed in red [BOURGES.2 ¶11]. When the devil fears a wayside cross, *Offerus passes the cross alone* and seeks Christ [BOURGES.2 ¶11]. A hermit tells him that Christ is served by prayer, fasting and *vigils*, and then sets him to carry travellers across a *torrent* that *comes down from a high mountain* [BOURGES.2 ¶11]. *One night* a child calls him *three times*; in mid-stream the waters rise and the child grows heavier [BOURGES.2 ¶12]. Offerus says «Il me semble que je porte le monde.» (*It seems to me that I am carrying the world.*), and the child answers that he carries «celui qui a fait le monde» (*him who made the world*) [BOURGES.2 ¶12]. Christ baptises him Christophe, and from then on he travels the earth teaching the word [BOURGES.2 ¶12].

The artist, guided by the scholar of hermetism who commissioned the work, added a wide belt at the giant's abdomen, and this belt gives the figure its true esoteric meaning [BOURGES.2 ¶13]. A footnote says that, according to documents in the house's archives, Jean Lallemant belonged to the alchemical Fraternity of the *Chevaliers de la Table ronde* [BOURGES.2 n4]. Fulcanelli says this is not taught, but he explains it because «un livre qui n’apprend rien est inutile et vain» (*a book that teaches nothing is useless and vain*) [BOURGES.2 ¶13].

### The Sign and the Epiphany cake

The belt is marked with criss-crossed lines like those on the surface of the solvent when it has been *canoniquement* (canonically) prepared [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. This is the *Signe* (Sign), which all the Philosophers take as the outward mark of the purity of their mercurial substance [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. Fulcanelli repeats that «tout le travail de l’art consiste à évertuer ce mercure jusqu’à ce qu’il soit revêtu du signe indiqué» (*the whole work of the art consists in energising this mercury until it is clothed with the said sign*) [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. The old authors called this sign «Sceau d’Hermes, Sel des Sages (Sel mis pour Scel)» (*Seal of Hermes, Salt of the Sages (Salt put for Seal)*), the mark and signature of the Almighty, the Star of the Magi, and the Pole Star [BOURGES.2 ¶14].

The pattern shows more clearly when gold is dissolved in mercury to bring it back to its first state as *or enfant* (child gold) [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. That is why mercury, the *loyal serviteur* (loyal servant), is called the Fountain of Youth [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. After the dissolution, mercury «porte l’enfant, le Fils du Soleil, le Petit Roi (Roitelet), comme une mère véritable, puisqu’en effet l’or renaît dans son sein» (*carries the child, the Son of the Sun, the Little King (Kinglet), like a true mother, since gold is indeed reborn in its womb*) [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. Fulcanelli cites Hermes' *Table d’Emeraude*, with a gloss on the wind inside the quotation: «Le vent, — qui est le mercure ailé et volatil, — l’a porté dans son ventre» (*The wind — which is the winged and volatile mercury — carried it in its belly*) [BOURGES.2 ¶14].

Fulcanelli finds the secret version of this truth in the *Gâteau des Rois* shared at Epiphany, the feast of the Child shown to the Magi, whom a star guided [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. The *galette* is "signed" like the matter itself and contains the little figure called the *baigneur* (bather) [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. That figure is the Child carried by Offerus; it is «l’or dans son bain, le baigneur» (*gold in its bath, the bather*), and it is also the bean, the clog, the cradle, the cross, and the fish «qui nage dans notre mer philosophique» (*that swims in our philosophic sea*), in the Cosmopolite's phrase [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. Christ was sometimes shown with a fish's tail, for example on a capital at Saint-Brisson-sur-Loire [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. Fulcanelli states: «Le poisson est l’hiéroglyphe de la pierre des Philosophes dans son premier état, parce que la pierre, comme le poisson, naît dans l’eau et vit dans l’eau» (*The fish is the hieroglyph of the Philosophers' stone in its first state, because the stone, like the fish, is born in water and lives in water*) [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. Allegories say to catch it with a net, an image of the criss-cross meshes on the Epiphany cakes; Pfau's 1702 alchemical stove shows an angler [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. A footnote adds that *avoir de la galette* (to have cake) means to be rich, and that whoever finds the bean will be doubly king, by science and by fortune [BOURGES.2 n7].

At a friend's house, Fulcanelli saw a cake marked with an oak instead of lozenges, with a porcelain fish in place of the baigneur: «ce poisson était une sole (lat. Sol, solis, le soleil)» (*this fish was a sole (Lat. Sol, solis, the sun)*) [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. He says the Cosmopolite's fish *Echinéis* is the *oursin* (sea urchin, *echinus*), the *ourson* (bear cub), the *petite ourse* (Little Bear), the constellation of the pole star [BOURGES.2 ¶14]. This is why Limojon de Saint-Didier tells seekers to steer «par la vue de l’étoile du nord» (*by the sight of the north star*) [BOURGES.2 ¶14].

Whoever finds this *poisson royal* (royal fish) in the cake is made king [BOURGES.2 ¶15]. The dolphin was a "royal fish", and the king's eldest son bore the title *Dauphin*; the fishermen of the *Mutus Liber* try to catch a dolphin, and dolphins appear on the Hôtel Lallemant's corner turret, on a pillar capital and on the chapel credence [BOURGES.2 ¶15]. Fulcanelli holds that the Greek *Ichtus* of the Catacombs has the same origin [BOURGES.2 ¶15]. A Catacomb painting reproduced by Martigny shows a fish carrying a basket of loaves; the basket, woven of crossed strands, is the same hieroglyph as the cake; he also points to the *cista* of Bacchus, in which, Fr. Noel says, «était renfermé ce qu’il y avait de plus mystérieux» (*was shut up what was most mysterious*) [BOURGES.2 ¶15].

The pastry is *feuilletée* (flaky, "leaved"), with the baigneur set in it like a bookmark [BOURGES.2 ¶16]. Sendivogius says that prepared mercury is a stony, friable, leafy mass: «Si vous l’observez bien, dit-il, vous remarquerez qu’elle est toute feuilletée.» (*If you observe it well, he says, you will notice that it is all in leaves.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶16] Its crystalline plates lie like the leaves of a book; hence the names *terre feuillée* and *livre aux feuillets* [BOURGES.2 ¶16]. The first matter is therefore shown as a book, open when it has been worked and closed when it has only been taken from the mine [BOURGES.2 ¶16]. A book sealed with seven bands marks the seven successive operations that open it, each one breaking a seal; this is the Great Book of Nature, easy to read if one can find it and open it [BOURGES.2 ¶16].

### The chapel and the Golden Fleece

The chapel's ornament is entirely profane and taken from hermetic science: a painted relief of the Golden Fleece, a coffered ceiling of hieroglyphic figures, and a credence holding an alchemical riddle [BOURGES.2 ¶17; BOURGES.2 ¶18]. Fulcanelli leaves open whether it was a chapel, a study or an oratory: «Chapelle, studio ou oratoire ?» (*Chapel, study or oratory?*) [BOURGES.2 ¶18]. The relief shows an oak forest in a ring of rocks, animals, reeds, and the ram's fleece on a rock guarded by a dragon; Jason, once carved at the foot of an oak, has broken off (pl. XLIII) [BOURGES.2 ¶19].

For Fulcanelli the fable is a complete riddle of the hermetic work [BOURGES.2 ¶20]. The text states: «on appelle Toison d’or la matière préparée pour l’Œuvre, ainsi que le résultat final» (*the prepared matter for the Work, as well as the final result, is called Golden Fleece*) [BOURGES.2 ¶20]. The two differ only in purity, fixity and maturity: the Stone of the Philosophers is raw, while the Philosophers' Stone that comes from it is fully cooked and digested [BOURGES.2 ¶20]. The task is not impossible, for «quiconque connaît le dragon sait aussi comment le vaincre» (*whoever knows the dragon also knows how to overcome it*); the real difficulty is interpreting the symbols, and Fulcanelli urges disciples to work at this material and tangible point [BOURGES.2 ¶20].

Here the oak and the ram are «une même chose sous deux aspects différents» (*one same thing under two different aspects*) [BOURGES.2 ¶21]. The old authors used the oak to «désigner le nom vulgaire du sujet initial, tel qu’on le rencontre dans la mine» (*designate the common name of the initial subject, as it is found in the mine*), by a near-pun whose equivalent answers to *chêne* [BOURGES.2 ¶21]. Fulcanelli says he cannot speak more plainly without overstepping certain limits, and dismisses the "doctors of kabbalah" [BOURGES.2 ¶21].

The oak bears galls (*galla*); bringing together «galla, Gallia, gallus, nous obtenons galle, Gaule, coq» (*galla, Gallia, gallus, we obtain gall, Gaul, cock*) [BOURGES.2 ¶22]. The cock is the emblem of Gaul and, as Jacob Tollius says, the attribute of Mercury [BOURGES.2 ¶22]. The oak also gives *Kermès*, «qui a, dans la Gaye Science, la même signification que Hermès, les consonnes initiales étant permutantes» (*which has, in the Gay Science, the same meaning as Hermes, the initial consonants being interchangeable*); both terms mean Mercury [BOURGES.2 ¶22]. The *galle* gives the name of the raw mercurial matter, while the *kermès* (Arabic *girmiz*, "which dyes scarlet") marks the prepared substance [BOURGES.2 ¶22]. The text states that «le mercure des Philosophes, c'est-à-dire leur matière préparée, doit posséder la vertu de teindre» (*the mercury of the Philosophers, that is, their prepared matter, must have the power to tinge*), which it gains only through first preparations [BOURGES.2 ¶22].

The raw subject is called *Magnesia lunarii*, *Plomb des Sages* (Lead of the Sages), *Saturnie végétable*, and *Fils* or *Enfant de Saturne* (Son or Child of Saturn) by Philalèthe, Basile Valentin and the Cosmopolite [BOURGES.2 ¶23]. These names refer now to its magnet-like attraction for sulphur, now to its ready fusibility [BOURGES.2 ¶23]. All call it the *Terre sainte* (Holy Land), and its celestial hieroglyph is the sign of the Ram (*Aries*) [BOURGES.2 ¶23]. *Gala* is Greek for milk, and mercury is also called *Lait de Vierge* (Virgin's Milk) [BOURGES.2 ¶23]. Fulcanelli says that whoever heeds what he said of the Epiphany cake, and knows why the Egyptians deified the cat, will know the subject's common name; he does not state the name [BOURGES.2 ¶23]. «Ouvrez, c'est-à-dire décomposez cette matière» (*Open, that is, break down this matter*), isolate its pure part or "metallic soul", and you will have the tinging mercury that carries the mystic gold, as Christopher carries Jesus and the ram its fleece [BOURGES.2 ¶23]. So the Fleece hangs on the oak like the gall and the kermes, and «le vieux chêne hermétique sert de mère au mercure secret» (*the old hermetic oak serves as mother to the secret mercury*) [BOURGES.2 ¶23].

### The chapel ceiling

The ceiling's coffers rest midway on two square pillars set against the walls [BOURGES.2 ¶24]. A footnote compares two other initiatic ceilings, at Dampierre-sur-Boutonne and at Plessis-Bourré [BOURGES.2 n12]. The right capital shows a winged human skull on oak leaves (pl. XLIV): «Traduction expressive d’une génération nouvelle, issue de cette putréfaction, consécutive à la mort, qui survient aux mixtes lorsqu’ils ont perdu leur âme vitale et volatile.» (*Expressive rendering of a new generation, issued from that putrefaction, following death, which befalls compounds when they have lost their vital and volatile soul.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶25] The dark blue or black colour of the dead body belongs to the «Corbeau, hiéroglyphe du caput mortuum de l’Œuvre» (*Raven, hieroglyph of the caput mortuum of the Work*), the first sign of dissolution and of the coming *soufre* (sulphur) [BOURGES.2 ¶25]. The wings teach that the loss of the volatile, watery part breaks the body's cohesion; the body falls into black ash, which inner fire calcines until a pure *sel* (salt) is born [BOURGES.2 ¶25].

The left capital shows a vase flanked by two dolphins, with a flower like a heraldic lily; these refer to the solvent, or common mercury, the principle opposite to sulphur [BOURGES.2 ¶26]. At the base of both pillars, an oak-leaf crown crossed by a bundle of the same foliage is the graphic sign of the subject's common name [BOURGES.2 ¶27]. Crown and capital together make the full symbol of the first matter: the globe held by God, Jesus and certain monarchs [BOURGES.2 ¶27].

Among the coffers (pl. XLV), Fulcanelli singles out several [BOURGES.2 ¶28]:
- An armillary sphere on a fiery ground stands for sulphur and its extraction from the first matter [BOURGES.2 ¶28].
- A beehive, a child urinating into his clog, and a child kneeling by flat ingots with an open book and a dead serpent [BOURGES.2 ¶28]. On the top ingot is Solomon's starred seal: «En bas, le mercure ; en haut, l’Absolu.» (*Below, mercury; above, the Absolute.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶28] It is one way, one matter, one operation, and the most famous authors say: «Celui qui sait faire l’Œuvre par le seul mercure a trouvé ce qu’il y a de plus parfait.» (*He who knows how to do the Work by mercury alone has found what is most perfect.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶28] The two triangles of fire and water, sulphur and mercury joined in one body, make the six-pointed star, «hiéroglyphe de l’Œuvre par excellence et de la Pierre Philosophale réalisée» (*hieroglyph of the Work par excellence and of the Philosophers' Stone achieved*) [BOURGES.2 ¶28].
- A flaming forearm seizing chestnuts, and the same arm holding a torch out of a rock [BOURGES.2 ¶28].
- Amalthea's horn with a hen or partridge perched on it; Fulcanelli says a black hen or a red partridge would have the same meaning [BOURGES.2 ¶28].
- A vase falling from a lion's mouth, «c’est une version originale du solve et coagula de Notre-Dame de Paris» (*it is an original version of the solve et coagula of Notre-Dame de Paris*) [BOURGES.2 ¶28].
- A child breaking a rosary over his knee [BOURGES.2 ¶28].
- The *mérelle* with a bound mass on it; the coffer repeats fifteen times the graphic sign that identifies its content, and the same sign appears large in a furnace [BOURGES.2 ¶28].
- The child, who seems to play the artist, standing in the shell and throwing small shells [BOURGES.2 ¶28].
- An open book burning; a haloed dove, emblem of the Spirit; a fiery raven pecking a skull, for death and putrefaction; and an angel spinning the world like a top, as in the Jesuits' *Typus Mundi* [BOURGES.2 ¶28].
- Philosophic calcination, shown as a pomegranate in fire in a goldsmith's vase, marked 3 R for the *trois réitérations* (three reiterations) of the same process [BOURGES.2 ¶28].
- The *ludus puerorum* of Trismosin's *Toison d’or*: a child on a hobby horse [BOURGES.2 ¶28].

### The credence: RERE, RER

The small sixteenth-century credence by the window holds a riddle that no visitor had solved, according to the guide; Fulcanelli says this is because no one grasped the aim of the decoration or read the Golden Fleece relief in its true sense (pl. XLVI) [BOURGES.2 ¶30]. Its pilasters carry emblems of the *mercure philosophal* (philosophic mercury): the *mérelle*, or holy-water shell, with wings and Neptune's trident, always signifying the watery, volatile principle [BOURGES.2 ¶30]. Its pediment is a shell with two dolphins tied together, and three flaming pomegranates [BOURGES.2 ¶30].

The riddle is two terms, RERE and RER, each repeated three times in the niche [BOURGES.2 ¶31]. The repetition signals three repetitions of one technique [BOURGES.2 ¶32]. The three pomegranates confirm this: they stand for the «feu corporifié dans ce sel rouge qu’est le Soufre philosophal» (*fire made bodily in that red salt which is the philosophic Sulphur*), whose calcination must be repeated three times to perform Geber's three works [BOURGES.2 ¶32]. The first gives the Sulphur (first-order medicine); the second, identical, gives the Elixir, which differs from the Sulphur only in quality; the third gives the Philosophers' Stone, which has all their virtues multiplied [BOURGES.2 ¶32]. For how this is done, Fulcanelli points to the ceiling relief of the pomegranate roasting in a vessel [BOURGES.2 ¶32].

Fulcanelli's decoding of RERE: RE, the Latin ablative of *res*, means the thing, considered as matter; RE plus RE is "two things in one", so RERE equals RE BIS, the REBIS that means the Philosophers' compound, ready to be transformed by fire [BOURGES.2 ¶33]. «RE, une matière sèche, or philosophique ; RE, une matière humide, mercure philosophique» (*RE, a dry matter, philosophic gold; RE, a wet matter, philosophic mercury*); RERE is their amalgam, with a double hidden property in exact balance [BOURGES.2 ¶33].

Fulcanelli says he may not fully unveil RER, but that it holds a secret of the first importance about the *vase de l’Œuvre* (vessel of the Work): RER serves to cook and unite the compound RERE and to bring about its changes [BOURGES.2 ¶34]. He warns against trusting Basile Valentin's *Douze Clefs* or taking literally the words «celui qui a la matière trouvera bien un pot pour la cuire» (*whoever has the matter will surely find a pot to cook it in*); he affirms that without perfect knowledge of the Philosophers' Vessel and how to make it, no success is possible [BOURGES.2 ¶34]. Pontanus says he failed more than two hundred times before he knew this vessel [BOURGES.2 ¶34]. «L’artiste doit faire lui-même son vaisseau ; c’est une maxime de l’art.» (*The artist must make his vessel himself; it is a maxim of the art.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶34] Nothing should be undertaken before full light on this eggshell, which the medieval masters called *secretum secretorum* [BOURGES.2 ¶34].

Since RE is a thing, R, half of RE, is half a thing; RER is a matter increased by half of another matter or of itself, as a chemical combination, not a proportion [BOURGES.2 ¶35]. As an example only, Fulcanelli supposes RE to be *réalgar* (natural arsenic sulphide); RER would then be realgar increased by its sulphur or by its arsenic, each taken as half of it [BOURGES.2 ¶35].

His advice is to seek RER, the vessel, first; RERE will then be easy to know [BOURGES.2 ¶36]. Asked what a Philosopher is, the Sibyl answered: «C’est celui qui sait faire le verre.» (*It is he who knows how to make glass.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶36] The potter's craft teaches more than glassmaking: one of Piccolpassi's plates shows «une colombe dont les pattes sont attachées à une pierre» (*a dove whose feet are tied to a stone*) [BOURGES.2 ¶36]. Following Tollius, the magistery lies in something volatile, which needs a vessel to keep it from escaping [BOURGES.2 ¶36]. Fulcanelli's procedure: make a vessel, then the compound; seal it so that no spirit escapes; heat until complete calcination; put the pure part of the powder back into the compound and seal it in the same vessel; repeat a third time [BOURGES.2 ¶36]. He asks no thanks, only that the reader take the same trouble for others [BOURGES.2 ¶36].

### Jean Lallemant

The author of these emblems left no known book; like the great medieval Adepts, he preferred to entrust his science to stone rather than vellum [BOURGES.2 ¶37]. Fulcanelli names him: Jean Lallemant, alchemist and knight of the Round Table, worthy of a place beside Geber and Roger Bacon [BOURGES.2 ¶38]. He is equal in knowledge to Basile Valentin and Flamel and superior to them in two qualities: «la modestie et la sincérité» (*modesty and sincerity*) [BOURGES.2 ¶38].
