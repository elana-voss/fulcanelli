# Reading the Monuments in Plain Language

This page says, in plain modern English, how Fulcanelli's book *Le Mystère des Cathédrales* reads the Gothic cathedrals and a few later buildings: what a reader needs before starting, and the reading itself, step by step. It is drawn from the French text alone. The book's special words are left out, except a few French words that sound alike, shown as the book's own examples because the reading turns on them. The passages behind each point are given on the page *Reading the Monuments in Plain Terms*. The book never sets out its method as one list, and Fulcanelli says that he holds back part of what his readings yield; the steps below are this page's arrangement of the book's main line, and some say where the book places a moment differently. Points the book leaves open are said to be open. The prefaces by Canseliet to the first, second and third editions are a later layer and are kept in their own section near the end.

## What the reading is

**What is read.** The Gothic cathedrals, Notre-Dame de Paris above all, then Amiens; two houses at Bourges; and a later stone cross at Hendaye. Fulcanelli treats a cathedral as a picture book in stone whose letters are its carvings. Such stone books, he holds, are clearer than written ones, because they carry a single, absolute meaning, free of the subtleties and double meanings of literature. A cathedral is for him not only a work to the glory of Christianity but a vast gathering of the people's ideas and faith, whose structure and ornament give off a more secular, almost pagan spirit. Most captivating of all, for him, are the symbols of the old alchemists, which the humble medieval sculptors, he says, rendered with skill.

**What it reads with.** Throughout, the book reads with two means: a way of hearing words, in which words that sound alike carry a hidden sense whatever their spelling; and close, exact looking at what is carved, with the old alchemical writers at hand.

**What it is for.** To recover the meaning hidden under the stone. At Notre-Dame de Paris, Fulcanelli says, the base of the façade gives, in stone, the name of the material the old masters worked on, and lets one follow their work step by step, from its first burning to its last cooking. He holds this science to be as positive, real and exact as optics, geometry or mechanics.

## What you need before you begin

### Understanding

1. **You take the building as a book with two sides.** At the main door of Notre-Dame de Paris a seated woman stands for alchemy, holding two books, one closed for the hidden teaching and one open for the outward teaching; Fulcanelli calls her the title of the chapter of the Gothic church, read as a silent book. The reading looks for the meaning hidden under the stone.

2. **You accept that nothing is chance, words included.** Fulcanelli grants that his readings will be taken for mere plays on words, and agrees; what matters is that they guide faith toward a certainty and keep it from wandering in imagination. Here below, he says, there is no chance and no coincidence; all is foreseen and ordered. One would find marvels if one knew how to cut words open, break their shell and free the spirit they hold.

3. **You know that the builders spoke a secret language.** It starts from a sound-pair: *gothique* heard in *argotique*, "in slang". Gothic art is heard as the art of slang; and since slang, as the dictionaries define it, is a language for those who want to share their thoughts without being understood by the people around them, it is, the book says, a spoken secret language. All who were in the secret spoke slang, among them the medieval freemasons who built the cathedrals. Slang is one branch of an older mother tongue, the language of philosophers and diplomats, which gave the key to a double knowledge, sacred and profane.

### Knowledge

4. **You know the old alchemical writers and how they write.** Fulcanelli reads the figures with their books beside him, quoting them to help the seeker find his road. He reminds the student that, as his books say in several places, these writers speak clearly only when they want to keep outsiders away: their perfectly clear descriptions of the coloured stages of the work are to be judged false, and their books are shut with seals that must be broken one by one. The book's last pages say the student will profit from the book if he does not despise the old masters' works and studies the classic texts closely, until he can make out the obscure points of their working method.

5. **You know that much has been lost.** The cathedrals were once gilded and painted throughout; on Notre-Dame the painted coat has disappeared, save for traces of gilding, and the colours of its porch figures survive in the glass of a round window. Notre-Dame has been mutilated by revolutions and vandals, and its restorers did not always respect what was spared; yet enough remains to make a visit worthwhile. Time wears and blurs the stone, but the meaning remains; a restorer's new carving can cut the meaning off.

### Character

6. **You observe exactly and keep a sound mind.** The book's last pages ask of anyone who takes up its science much accuracy, exactness and sharpness in observing facts, a sound, logical and balanced mind, a lively imagination without excess, and an ardent, pure heart.

7. **You think for yourself.** The same pages ask for complete indifference to theories accepted without checking on the strength of books or of their authors' fame, and for thinking more with one's own brain than with other people's.

8. **You keep enthusiasm under reason.** In the research of the science, he says, enthusiasm and private faith are precious aids, but they must be kept under logic and reasoning and put to the test of experience; one must see rightly and speak well, not a word unweighed.

9. **You have the sharp sight to gather what is scattered.** Fulcanelli says he broke up a great mystery of the work and scattered it at random among the emblems, so that only a sharp-sighted seeker can know its qualities and identify it. Nature, the book's last pages say, does not open the door of the sanctuary to everyone.

## How a monument is read, step by step

The book states one order firmly: on the Paris façade it always takes the figures in their order, from the outside toward the doors, as a worshipper entering the church would meet them, to keep some method in its study; and it says that this base of the façade lets one follow the work from its first burning to its last cooking. The book never lists the steps of reading as such; the steps below arrange its main line at Paris, Amiens and Bourges, and some say where it places a moment differently.

### Step 1: Hear the names

Listen to the words: the name of the style, of the church, of the figures. In the book the name of the style is heard first, in its introduction; other names are heard where they are met, often in the middle of a figure's reading.

- **How it is done.** Hear a word as it sounds, not as it is spelled: a law of sound, the book says, governs this hidden speech in every language, whatever the spelling. The book takes the people's names seriously: truth, it says, comes out of the mouth of the people, who kept the name "Gothic art" against the Academy, and the people are the faithful keepers of spoken tradition.
- **What happens.** *Christophe* is heard as *Chrysophe*: the giant saint who carries the Christ child is read as the one who carries gold. A devil figure at Notre-Dame was called by the people *Maistre Pierre du Coignet*, "Master Peter of the Corner", a name that puzzled the scholars; the book reads it as *la maîtresse pierre du coignet*, "the master stone of the corner": the figure stands for the raw starting material of the work, given, as it often was, the image of the devil.
- **Cautions.** Fulcanelli does not set out the rules of this hearing. At Hendaye he says that some authors, Grasset d'Orcet above all, gave them clearly enough, and only sketches the way: read the Latin as written but in French, let the vowels change, take the new words the sounds give, and restore their spelling, order and sense. He sets his hearing apart from that of self-styled masters of the same art, Jewish or Christian, whose combinations, he says, lead to nothing solid, positive or scientific.

### Step 2: Read the parts of the building

Read the plan, the facing, the windows, the floor and the crypt. The book does this in its introduction, before it reads the Paris façade.

- **The plan.** The cross of the plan is read as the sign of the crucible, the work's melting pot; through it the plan is said to reveal the qualities of the starting material and its preparation.
- **The facing.** Those who enter from the west walk toward the rising sun: they leave darkness and go toward the light.
- **The windows.** Of the three great round windows, the north one is never sunlit, the south one blazes at noon, the west one glows at sunset: so the façades show the colours of the work in a circle, from black through white to red. The central window, called the wheel in the Middle Ages, stands for the action of fire and its length.
- **The floor.** The maze stands for the whole work, with its two great difficulties: the way to the centre and the way out.
- **The crypt.** The black statues of the Virgin kept underground stand for the primitive earth the worker must choose to start from, an ore which, the texts say, is black, heavy and brittle; it is fitting, the book says, that the human image of this mineral should share its colour and live underground.
- **Caution.** The pagan fight once shown at the centre of the Chartres maze is taken as proof that pagan subjects entered Christian imagery, and so of a hidden sense drawn from myth, but the book denies any link between these mazes and those of ancient Greece and Egypt.

### Step 3: Choose what to read, and set the rest aside

Find the part of the building given to the science. At the Paris porch the book prefers to keep to figures whose secular character is beyond dispute; elsewhere it also reads sacred subjects, such as saint Christopher and the coffin in a scene from the life of Christ on the Virgin's door.

- **How it is done.** At Paris the base of the façade, under the three porches, is given over entirely to the science. At Bourges one small room has ornament that is entirely secular and all taken from the science: not one religious scene.
- **What is set aside.** Above two reliefs at the main Paris door stand two biblical scenes. Fulcanelli grants that a hidden sense lies there under sacred appearances, yet he avoids them, so as not to be blamed for an arbitrary reading. A statue of a bishop-saint on the Paris south door has, in his view, no secret meaning; only the block under it and the base below carry one.
- **Caution.** He faults an earlier writer, Cambriel, for extending without proof the meaning of that block to the statue.

### Step 4: Take the figures in their order

Follow the building's own order.

- **How it is done.** On the Paris façade, go from the outside toward the doors. The figures stand in two rows on each side of the porch: twelve seated figures above, each holding a disc with an emblem, and twelve medallions below. The reading begins with the upper row, on the left.
- **Caution.** The book states this order for Paris, and says that the central porch at Amiens reproduces almost faithfully both the Paris figures and their order; for the other buildings it gives no such rule.

### Step 5: Look at what each figure holds and how it is made

What a figure holds, and exactly how it is carved, guide the reading.

- **How it is done.** Start from the attribute. The first figure of the upper row at Paris holds a raven, sign of the colour black; the woman holding it stands for rotting. A small detail can settle a scene: at Bourges a starred seal carved in the half-shadow of the mouldings decides the sense of a small relief.
- **The carvers' habits.** The medieval carvers marked supernatural things with a band of clouds, and in every Gothic scene of a vision the one who sees faces it. A figure at Amiens beside two wheels, read by several scholars as a prophet asleep in a vision, has no clouds, has open eyes and does not face the wheels: he is therefore, the book says, not asleep, and seems to keep watch, and the wheels veil something well known.
- **Exactness.** The old carvers were exact and careful translators: they would not have left out the dogs had a scene of a cock and a fox been the well-known fable, and the book cannot believe that one would have carved a pestle without its mortar. At Bourges the so-called pestle is a long-necked flask; the hollow, whistle-cut end of its handle proves a hollow vessel.
- **Impossibility.** A little "church" at Amiens stands on open arches, as if on four feet, which the book finds hard to accept of a church; with other reasons, this leads it to read a furnace.
- **Caution.** Where an attribute is lost, the book does not guess it: of an object held by one Paris figure it says it does not know what it is, and reads the figure from the ram that remains.

### Step 6: Name it with the old writers' words

Match the carving with the old writers and their pictures, and read colours, numbers and words in stone as signs.

- **Books and pictures.** The relief of a man at a fountain on the Paris porch has, the book says, the same subject as a figure in an old book of pictures: the one man, among many seeking the fountain, who weighs it in his hand. Stone and writer explain each other: a ram shown by one Paris figure helps to understand an old author's words.
- **Colours.** The book holds, with an author it cites, that a language of colours tied to religion has always existed and reappears in the stained glass of the Gothic cathedrals. The three main colours of the work always come in the same order: black, white, red.
- **Numbers.** Numbers can mark operations. The ladder of nine rungs held by the figure of alchemy stands for the patience needed through nine successive operations; at Bourges, three repetitions of one process are marked by the figure 3 followed by the letter R, by two riddling words each cut three times, and by three flaming pomegranates.
- **Stories.** The book lines a carving up with myths and legends it holds to carry the same sign: the sword that opens the rock, the rod of Moses that makes water spring from the stone, the sceptre of the goddess Rhea and the javelin of Atalanta are, it says, one and the same sign.
- **Words in stone.** Mottoes and inscriptions are read in two layers. The four letters on the crossbar of the Hendaye cross, which openly stand for "Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews", are read in their hidden sense as "by fire nature is renewed whole".

### Step 7: Place it as a stage of the work

Turn a scene into an operation; sometimes the figure shows a thing instead, such as a vessel or a furnace.

- **How it is done.** On one Paris medallion a rider thrown from a fiery horse clings to it: the horse stands for the light, spirit-like substance, the rider for the heavy metal body; at each repetition the horse throws its rider, who at once takes his seat again, until the worn-out horse carries him for good. Another medallion shows a mirror, a horn of plenty and a tree of life: the mirror marks the beginning of the work, the tree its end, the horn its result.
- **Caution.** A small Amiens relief that one scholar called a copy of one at Paris is, for the book, unrelated to it: the Paris one shows the start of the work, the Amiens one its end.

### Step 8: Test the reading

Where it can, the book checks a reading against the stone, the colours and older witnesses. This testing runs through the reading and sometimes comes first, when a received explanation is cleared away.

- **Breaks of pattern.** On the Virgin's door at Paris six of seven circles repeat two by two around a centre decorated in its own way, which never happens in purely decorative Gothic work; in the line joining them one space is left empty, which, the book says, finally proves that the symbolic sense is intended. The book grants that a carved zodiac may simply show the stars, but there the Lion takes the place of the Crab, and it reads this shift as made on purpose. At Hendaye an inscription whose word for "hope" has lost its first letter and become the word for "foot" is judged, in Fulcanelli's view, an error made on purpose to rouse curiosity, since the other carvings are carefully placed and the letters are clean.
- **Colour.** Colour, where it can still be found, clears up doubtful points. The Paris paint is lost, but Guillaume de Paris, whose work the book holds the figures to be, had the medallions copied in the glass of the central round window: glass thus completes stone, and shows, for instance, that the thrown rider is a prince crowned with gold.
- **Older witnesses.** Whether a king's head carved on the block of the Paris south-door pillar wears a crown or a helmet cannot be told for certain today, but a text written in 1640 says it wears a triple crown.

### Step 9: Stop where the book stops

Leave a figure unread where the stone, the paint or the oath allows no more.

- **Lost stone.** Two Paris medallions are too damaged for their sense to be restored.
- **Lost paint.** Before the bare stone of a lion the book will not settle for one explanation: the lion may stand for gold, or for a material that the old texts call by the same name and that serves in making the dissolving agent. A trace of cinnabar, malachite or metal would decide, but nothing remains: the stone lion keeps its secret.
- **Restored stone.** On the new pillar of the Paris south door the restorers changed the base: the symbol is cut short, the key lost. The reader is sent to the original in the Cluny museum.
- **Questions left standing.** The book asks, without answering, whether a small room at Bourges was a chapel, a study or an oratory.
- **The oath.** Where he could not go further without breaking his oath, Fulcanelli says, he preferred silence to misleading hints.
- **His own reading.** Of his reading of the Paris pillar he says that others, more learned, will perhaps give a better one, for he imposes it on no one.

## What it leads to

- **The material and the stages of the work.** The base of the Paris façade is where, the book says, one finds the name in stone of the material the old masters started from, and follows their work step by step.
- **The cathedral as a whole.** The whole cathedral, the book says, is a silent yet pictured glorification of the old science, and the book's reading of the Paris porch closes on the paradox of the torch of alchemical thought lighting the temple of Christian thought.
- **Who wrote the stone book.** The cathedrals were built, the book says, by the medieval freemasons to pass on the symbols and teaching of the science; in Gothic work, it says, the workmanship stays subject to the idea, while in Renaissance work it dominates and effaces it. Later, the master of one Bourges house probably chose, like the great masters of the Middle Ages, to entrust his science to stone rather than to parchment.
- **A warning at Hendaye.** Read in the same secret tongue, the Hendaye cross warns that it is written that life takes refuge in a single space, a land death will not reach at the time of a double catastrophe; where it lies is left for us to find.
- **What the book hopes to show.** That it is not impossible to recover the meaning hidden under the stone.

## The rules behind the whole reading

- **Sound, not spelling.** A law of sound links words that sound alike, in every language, whatever the spelling.
- **Nothing is chance.** Here below there is no chance and no coincidence; all is foreseen and ordered.
- **The carvers were exact.** The old carvers were careful translators, who would not have left out a needed part.
- **Irregularity shows intent.** The book takes a break in a decorative pattern, or an apparent mistake carved with care, as a sign placed on purpose.
- **Wear keeps the sense; restoration loses it.** Time blurs the stone but leaves the meaning; the restorer can destroy it.
- **One sign, many stories.** Myths, saints' legends and romances can carry the same sign as a carving; the book holds the romances of the Round Table to be traditional legends of the science, renewed from Greek fables.
- **Reason over enthusiasm.** The book holds its science to be as exact as optics or geometry, and asks that enthusiasm and faith in its research be kept under logic and tested by experience.

## Mistakes and dangers

- **Repeating a received reading without looking.** In the scholars' reading of the Amiens figure by the wheels as a sleeping prophet, the book finds two errors: an incomplete study of the carvers' rules and, worse, faulty observation.
- **Believing the guide.** At Bourges the guide calls a dark basement room the kitchen and a carved figure a cook's boy with a pestle; the book finds the hearth too small for cooking but large enough to hold the tower of a furnace, and the pestle a flask.
- **Offering a reading without its reason.** Scholars read a figure at Amiens as Apostasy, a cleric throwing off his clothes as he leaves the church; why such men should undress, the book says, is exactly the reason they should have given.
- **Stretching a meaning without proof.** Cambriel was broadly right about the Paris pillar, the book says, but wrong to extend its meaning, without proof, to the statue.
- **Trusting a copy.** Cambriel's engraving showed the saint with a finger on his lips for silence; the original shows him blessing, and the gesture of silence, the book says, is not there and never was.
- **Taking a symbol literally.** The old writers took a soft green growth that swells with night dew as a picture of their material; ignorant practitioners took it literally, which, the book says, did much to discredit alchemy and make its followers ridiculous.
- **Missing the aim of the whole.** A carved riddle at Bourges stayed unread, probably, the book says, because no one had understood what the whole decoration aimed at.

## What Canseliet adds

- **What the book achieved.** In the first preface Canseliet writes that, thanks to Fulcanelli, the Gothic cathedral yields its secret, and that the marvels of the Middle Ages hold the same positive truth as the pyramids of Egypt and the temples of Greece, among others. Students of the science worthy of the name, he says, will find that from the confrontation of the Book and the Building the spirit comes free and the letter dies; Fulcanelli made the first effort, the last is theirs.
- **A key in a picture.** He writes that he knows, because the author assured him of it, that the key to the greatest secret is given, without any fiction, by one of the book's pictures, and that this key is simply a colour, shown to the worker from the very first labour. He does not say which picture or which colour.
- **The hearing of words restored.** In the second preface he says the book's merit would be proved by this alone: it brought back into full light the reading of words by sound, whose principles and use had been wholly forgotten. This mother tongue, easily understood though never spoken, can no longer, he says, be confused with the transpositions and calculations, as abstruse as they are arbitrary, of the Jewish Kabbalah. Fulcanelli, he says, found again the lost key of the secret tongue.
- **The pillar at Paris.** In the third preface he defends at length Fulcanelli's correction of Cambriel and says that the original is now kept in the cathedral's north tower. In the saint's legend, where the dragon is led round in a circle and sent to the desert or the sea, he sees the picture of the two ways of the work, the dry and the wet; this reading is his, not the book's.
- **On the way of working.** He names an old saying, "dissolve and coagulate", as the best guide to the way of working, a simple technique that asks for sincerity, resolve and patience and calls on imagination.

## What the book leaves open

- **What are the rules of hearing words by sound?** The book never states them; it says other authors gave them, sketches the way at Hendaye, and otherwise shows only cases.
- **How do some readings work?** A motto on a Bourges house, read with "hearts" in the old spelling, gives three things at once, the book says; it does not show how. The last link that makes Gothic art the art of light rests on a syllable and two Greek letters, with no further explanation.
- **Which reading of the Paris lion is right?** Gold, or a material that the old texts call by the same name and that serves in making the dissolving agent; with the paint gone, Fulcanelli says he cannot decide.
- **Chapel, study or oratory?** The book asks this of the small room at Bourges and does not answer.
- **What do the biblical scenes at Paris hide?** Fulcanelli grants that they have a hidden sense but chooses not to read them.
- **What is the common name of the starting material?** The old writers, the book says, pointed to it by a near-sound that answers to the oak; Fulcanelli says he cannot speak more plainly there without overstepping certain bounds, and writes no name in that place.
- **What ties the cock to the oak?** At Amiens Fulcanelli says he will no doubt explain later the close tie between the cock and the oak, like that of son and father. At Bourges he links oak and cock again through three related Latin words, but does not say that this is the promised explanation.
- **Where is the refuge?** The Hendaye cross says that it is written that life takes refuge in a single place; where that land lies, the book leaves us to find.
