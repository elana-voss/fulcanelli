# Reading the Monuments in Plain Terms

This page states, for a modern reader, the method by which *Le Mystère des Cathédrales* reads the Gothic cathedrals and a few later buildings. It is organised by principle. Every claim is marked **stated** (the text says it) or **interpreted** (our inference, with the passages it rests on). Quotations are verbatim French with our English beside them. Canseliet's prefaces are kept to the final section.

---

## 1. The claim: the cathedral is a book, and its text is alchemy

**Stated.** The first move of the book is to treat the cathedrals literally as books. They are «ces beaux livres d’images dressés sur nos parvis, et qui développent jusqu’au ciel leurs feuillets de pierre sculptés» (*these beautiful picture-books raised on our forecourts, which unfold their sculpted stone leaves up to the sky*) [INTRO.1 ¶2]. Their letters are carvings: «Si ces livres lapidaires ont leurs lettres sculptées, — phrases en bas-reliefs et pensées en ogives, — ils n’en parlent pas moins par l’esprit, impérissable, qui s’exhale de leurs pages» (*If these lapidary books have their letters carved — sentences in bas-reliefs and thoughts in ogives — they speak nonetheless by the imperishable spirit that breathes from their pages*) [INTRO.1 ¶4].

**Stated.** The stone book is held to be clearer than written ones, because it carries a single, absolute meaning, «un sens purgé des finesses, des allusions, des équivoques littéraires» (*a meaning purged of the subtleties, allusions and equivocations of literature*) [INTRO.1 ¶4]. This is the ground of the whole method: a carving is taken to mean one thing, not many (interpreted from the same passage).

**Stated.** The cathedral is not only Christian. It is «une vaste concrétion d’idées, de tendances, de foi populaires, un tout parfait auquel on peut se référer sans crainte dès qu’il s’agit de pénétrer la pensée des ancêtres» (*a vast concretion of ideas, tendencies and popular faith, a perfect whole to which one may refer without fear whenever one wants to penetrate the thought of the ancestors*) [INTRO.1 ¶6]. Its structure and ornament breathe «un esprit plus laïque et, disons le mot, presque païen» (*a more secular, and let us say it, almost pagan spirit*) [INTRO.1 ¶8]. It is «une encyclopédie très complète et très variée, tantôt naïve, tantôt noble, toujours vivante, de toutes les connaissances médiévales» (*a very complete and varied encyclopaedia, now naive, now noble, always living, of all medieval knowledge*), and its carvings are «des éducateurs, des initiateurs au premier chef» (*educators, initiators of the first rank*) [INTRO.2 ¶3].

**Stated.** Among all this, the part that matters to the book is «le symbolisme des vieux alchimistes, habilement traduit par les modestes statuaires médiévaux» (*the symbolism of the old alchemists, skilfully translated by the modest medieval statuaries*) [INTRO.2 ¶4]. Notre-Dame de Paris is called «église philosophale» (*philosophal church*), and Victor Hugo is quoted (QUOTED: Victor Hugo, endorsed) calling it «l’abrégé le plus satisfaisant de la science hermétique, dont l’église de Saint-Jacques-la Boucherie était un hiéroglyphe si complet» (*the most satisfying abridgement of hermetic science, of which the church of Saint-Jacques-la-Boucherie was so complete a hieroglyph*) [INTRO.2 ¶4].

**Stated.** The reading has precedents: the alchemists of the fourteenth century met weekly at Notre-Dame, «au jour de Saturne, soit au grand porche, soit au portail Saint-Marcel, ou encore à la petite Porte-Rouge» (*on Saturn's day, either at the great porch, or at the Saint-Marcel portal, or again at the little Red Door*) [INTRO.2 ¶5]; there «on y étudiait sur place l’allégorie du beau livre» (*one studied on the spot the allegory of the beautiful book*), and the liveliest part of the meetings was «l’exégèse abstruse des mystérieux symboles» (*the abstruse exegesis of the mysterious symbols*) [INTRO.2 ¶6]. Fulcanelli places himself after Gobineau de Montluisant and Cambriel and sets out to «parler aux pierres et les interroger» (*speak to the stones and question them*) [INTRO.2 ¶7], to «retrouver le sens de l’arcane dissimulé sous l’écorce pétrifiée du prodigieux grimoire» (*recover the meaning of the arcanum hidden under the petrified bark of the prodigious grimoire*) [INTRO.2 ¶8].

**Stated.** The book's own name for the cathedral is a silent book: the trumeau figure of alchemy is «le titre du chapitre de ce mutus Liber qu’est le temple gothique ; le frontispice de cette Bible occulte aux massifs feuillets de pierre» (*the title of the chapter of this mutus Liber that is the Gothic temple; the frontispiece of this occult Bible with massive leaves of stone*), «le sceau du Grand Oeuvre laïque au front du Grand Oeuvre chrétien» (*the seal of the secular Great Work on the brow of the Christian Great Work*) [PARIS.1 ¶4]. «la cathédrale tout entière n’est qu’une glorification muette, mais imagée, de l’antique science d’Hermès» (*the whole cathedral is nothing but a mute but pictured glorification of the ancient science of Hermes*) [PARIS.1 ¶12]. The paradox is named: «le flambeau de l’alchimique pensée illuminant le temple de la pensée chrétienne !» (*the torch of alchemical thought lighting the temple of Christian thought!*) [PARIS.5 ¶20].

**Stated.** The two levels are themselves carved: the figure of alchemy holds «deux livres, l’un fermé (ésotérisme), l’autre ouvert (exotérisme)» (*two books, one closed (esotericism), the other open (exotericism)*) [PARIS.1 ¶3]. The open level is secular and popular: «Rien de plus laïque que l’exotérisme de cet enseignement !» (*Nothing more secular than the exotericism of this teaching!*) [INTRO.2 ¶4]. The method aims at the closed book (interpreted from PARIS.1 ¶3 with PARIS.7 ¶35, where the uninstructed visitor finds only «l’exotérisme légendaire de saint Marcel», *the legendary exotericism of saint Marcel* [PARIS.7 ¶35]).

## 2. The key: *art gothique* is *argot*, a spoken cabala

**Stated.** The key to the stone book is a reading of its own name. The word *gothique* is not to be explained by Goths or by "barbarous": «l’explication doit en être recherchée dans l’origine cabalistique du mot plutôt que dans sa racine littérale» (*the explanation must be sought in the cabalistic origin of the word rather than in its literal root*) [INTRO.3 ¶3]. The people kept the right name against the Academy's *Art ogival*: «La vérité, qui sort de la bouche du peuple, a pourtant maintenu et conservé l’expression d’Art gothique» (*Truth, which comes from the mouth of the people, has nevertheless maintained and preserved the expression Gothic Art*) [INTRO.3 ¶3]. Some authors noted «similitude qui existe entre gothique et goétique» (*the similarity between gothic and goetic*) [INTRO.3 ¶4]; Fulcanelli's own reading is different.

**Stated.** «art gothique n’est qu’une déformation orthographique du mot argotique, dont l’homophonie est parfaite» (*gothic art is only an orthographic deformation of the word argotique, with which it is perfectly homophonous*), by «la loi phonétique qui régit, dans toutes les langues et sans tenir compte de l’orthographe, la cabale traditionnelle» (*the phonetic law which governs, in all languages and without regard to spelling, the traditional cabala*) [INTRO.3 ¶5]. Hence «La cathédrale est une œuvre d’art goth ou d’argot» (*The cathedral is a work of goth art or of argot*) [INTRO.3 ¶5]. The dictionaries (QUOTED: les dictionnaires, adopted) define argot as «un langage particulier à tous les individus qui ont intérêt à se communiquer leurs pensées sans être compris de ceux qui les entourent» (*a language particular to all individuals who have an interest in communicating their thoughts without being understood by those around them*); so «C’est donc bien une cabale parlée» (*It is therefore indeed a spoken cabala*) [INTRO.3 ¶5].

**Stated.** The speakers of argot are given a lineage by sound: the *argotiers* are «descendants hermétiques des argo-nautes, lesquels montaient le navire Argo, parlaient la langue argotique, — notre langue verte» (*hermetic descendants of the argo-nauts, who manned the ship Argo, spoke the argotic tongue — our green tongue*) on their way to the Golden Fleece [INTRO.3 ¶5]. «Tous les Initiés s’exprimaient en argot» (*All Initiates expressed themselves in argot*): the truands of the Cour des Miracles with Villon, and the «Frimasons, ou francs-maçons du moyen âge, “logeurs du bon Dieu”» (*Frimasons, or freemasons of the Middle Ages, “lodgers of the good God”*), who built the works [INTRO.3 ¶5]. Today's outlaws who speak it are called «voyous, c'est-à-dire de voyants» (*voyous, that is to say seers*), and the closing reading is «L’art gothique est, en effet, l’art got ou cot (Χο), l’art de la Lumière ou de l’Esprit» (*Gothic art is, in fact, the art got or cot (Χο), the art of Light or of the Spirit*) [INTRO.3 ¶6]. The text gives no further derivation of *cot* than the Greek letters in parentheses.

**Stated.** Fulcanelli concedes the obvious objection and answers it with a doctrine of language: «Ce sont là, pensera-t-on, de simples jeux de mots. Nous en convenons volontiers.» (*These are, one will think, mere plays on words. We willingly agree.*) «L’essentiel est qu’ils guident notre foi vers une certitude, vers la vérité positive et scientifique, clef du mystère religieux» (*The essential thing is that they guide our faith towards a certainty, towards positive and scientific truth, key of the religious mystery*) [INTRO.3 ¶7]. The justification is that nothing in language is accidental: «Il n’y a, ici-bas, ni hasard, ni coïncidence, ni rapport fortuit ; tout est prévu, ordonné, réglé» (*There is, here below, neither chance, nor coincidence, nor fortuitous relation; all is foreseen, ordered, ruled*) [INTRO.3 ¶7]. Words have a shell and a spirit: «si nous savions disséquer les mots, en briser l’écorce et libérer l’esprit, divine lumière qu’ils renferment» (*if we knew how to dissect words, break their bark and free the spirit, divine light, that they enclose*) [INTRO.3 ¶7]. Parables and puns are of the same kind: «Jésus ne s’exprimait qu’en paraboles» (*Jesus expressed himself only in parables*), and the witty are «cabalistes sans le savoir» (*cabalists without knowing it*) [INTRO.3 ¶7].

**Stated.** Argot is one derived form of an older tongue: «l’argot est une des formes dérivées de la Langue des Oiseaux, mère et doyenne de toutes les autres, la langue des philosophes et des diplomates» (*argot is one of the derived forms of the Language of the Birds, mother and eldest of all the others, the language of philosophers and of diplomats*) [INTRO.3 ¶8]. The ancient Incas called it «Langue de cour, parce qu’elle était familière aux diplomates» (*Court language, because it was familiar to the diplomats*), to whom it gave «la clef d’une double science : la science sacrée et la science profane» (*the key of a double science: the sacred science and the profane science*); the Middle Ages called it «Gaie science ou Gay sçavoir, Langue des dieux, Dive-Bouteille» (*Gay science or Gay knowing, Language of the gods, Divine Bottle*); «C’est elle dont Jésus révèle la connaissance à ses apôtres, en leur envoyant son esprit, l’Esprit-Saint» (*It is this whose knowledge Jesus reveals to his apostles by sending them his spirit, the Holy Spirit*); men spoke it before Babel, and its traces survive in argot, Picard, Provençal and the dialect of the gypsies [INTRO.3 ¶8]. Rabelais's *Gargantua et Pantagruel* is «une œuvre ésotérique, un roman d’argot» (*an esoteric work, a novel in argot*) [INTRO.3 n1]. Tirésias, said to have known this language, shared it with Thalès, Melampus and Apollonios de Tyane, «personnages fictifs dont les noms parlent éloquemment» (*fictitious figures whose names speak eloquently*) [INTRO.3 ¶9]; only Apollonios is analysed, his name seeming «une image mytho-hermétique du compost, ou rebis philosophal» (*a mytho-hermetic image of the compost, or philosophic rebis*) [INTRO.3 n4].

**Stated.** The author's own practice is covered by the same word: he cites Artephius (QUOTED: Artephius, adopted), «Notre Art, disait jadis Artephius, est entièrement cabalistique» (*Our Art, Artephius once said, is entirely cabalistic*), and says the cabala let him, «sans truquer la vérité, sans dénaturer l’expression, sans falsifier la Science ni nous parjurer, de dire plusieurs choses qu’on chercherait vainement dans les livres de nos prédécesseurs» (*without faking the truth, without distorting expression, without falsifying the Science or perjuring ourselves, say several things one would seek in vain in the books of our predecessors*) [AMIENS ¶21]. The Philosophers' books are «fermés, comme celui de l’Apocalypse, par des sceaux cabalistiques. Il vous faut les briser un à un.» (*closed, like that of the Apocalypse, by cabalistic seals. You must break them one by one.*) [PARIS.3 ¶34].

**Stated.** This cabala is set against another: the reading of the oak «ne saurait parler mieux sans outrepasser certaines bornes» (*could not be put better without overstepping certain bounds*), and «Seuls, les initiés au langage des dieux comprendront sans aucune peine, parce qu’ils possèdent les clefs qui ouvrent toutes les portes» (*Only the initiates in the language of the gods will understand without any trouble, because they hold the keys that open all doors*); the «docteurs en kabbale» (*doctors in kabbalah*), Jewish or Christian, are dismissed, their «combinaisons illusoires ne conduisent à rien de solide, de positif ni de scientifique» (*illusory combinations lead to nothing solid, positive or scientific*) [BOURGES.2 ¶21]. The text does not say here what distinguishes the two in method; Canseliet does (section 10).

**What is not said.** The "phonetic law" is never stated as a set of rules. The one place that names rules, the Hendaye inscription, refers the reader to Grasset d'Orcet's analysis of the *Songe de Polyphile* for the rules of the *Diplomatique* and says they were given clearly enough to spare him repeating them [HENDAYE ¶9]. The procedure there is described, not codified: read the Latin as written but in French, the «langue des diplomates»; apply the permuting vowels; take the assonance of the new words; restore spelling, word order and literary sense [HENDAYE ¶9]. What follows is therefore a collection of worked cases, not a grammar (interpreted).

## 3. Worked readings by sound and root

Each is recorded exactly as far as the text takes it. Nothing is extended.

**Names of the building.** *Notre-Dame* and the Sicilian *Matrices* make the cathedrals «des temples dédiés à la Mère (lat. mater, matris), à la Matrone dans le sens primitif, mot qui, par corruption, est devenu la Madone» (*temples dedicated to the Mother (lat. mater, matris), to the Matron in the primitive sense, a word that by corruption became the Madonna*) [PARIS.1 ¶1]. Matter itself is «la Matière élémentaire (lat. materea, racine mater, mère» (*elementary Matter (lat. materea, root mater, mother*) [PARIS.1 ¶5]. The crypt is «la crypte (du grec Κρυπτος, caché)» (*the crypt (from the Greek Κρυπτος, hidden)*) [INTRO.8 ¶1].

**Plan and cross.** «la croix est l’hiéroglyphe alchimique du creuset, que l’on nommait jadis cruzol, crucible et croiset» (*the cross is the alchemical hieroglyph of the crucible, which was once called cruzol, crucible and croiset*), with the note «(dans la basse latinité, crucibulum, creuset, a pour racine crux, crucis, croix, d’après Ducange)» (*in low Latin crucibulum, crucible, has for root crux, crucis, cross, according to Ducange*) [INTRO.4 ¶1]. Popular speech confirms it: «Porter sa croix, gravir son calvaire, passer au creuset de l’existence sont autant de locutions courantes où nous retrouvons le même sens sous un même symbolisme» (*To bear one's cross, climb one's calvary, pass through the crucible of existence are so many current phrases in which we find the same meaning under the same symbolism*) [INTRO.4 ¶2]. The apse added to the choir makes the plan «la croix ansée, qui se lit ank, et désigne la Vie universelle cachée dans les choses» (*the ansate cross, which reads ank, and designates the universal Life hidden in things*); its hermetic equivalent is «l’emblème de Vénus ou Cypris (en grec Κυπρις, l’impure), le cuivre vulgaire» (*the emblem of Venus or Cypris (in Greek Κυπρις, the impure), common copper*), and «Κυπρος est le même mot que Σουϕρος, soufre, lequel a la signification d’engrais, fiente, fumier, ordure» (*Κυπρος is the same word as Σουϕρος, sulphur, which has the meaning of manure, droppings, dung, filth*) [INTRO.4 ¶4]. Thus «le plan de l’édifice chrétien nous révèle les qualités de la matière première, et sa préparation, par le signe de la Croix» (*the plan of the Christian building reveals to us the qualities of the first matter, and its preparation, by the sign of the Cross*) [INTRO.4 ¶5].

**Ariane.** The longest chain in the book, given to explain the labyrinth's thread «en nous appuyant sur la cabale» (*leaning on the cabala*) [INTRO.5 ¶4]: «Ariane est une forme d’airagne (araignée), par métathèse de l’i» (*Ariane is a form of airagne (spider), by metathesis of the i*); «Le verbe αιρω signifie prendre, saisir, entraîner, attirer ; d’où αιρην, ce qui prend, saisit, attire. Donc, αιρην est l’aimant» (*The verb αιρω means to take, seize, draw, attract; whence αιρην, that which takes, seizes, attracts. So αιρην is the magnet*); «En provençal, le fer est appelé aran et iran, suivant les différents dialectes. C’est l’Hiram maçonnique, le divin Bélier, l’architecte du Temple de Salomon» (*In Provençal iron is called aran and iran, according to dialect. It is the masonic Hiram, the divine Ram, the architect of Solomon's Temple*); «Le verbe αρυω exprime le lever d’un astre qui sort de la mer» (*The verb αρυω expresses the rising of a star that comes out of the sea*), so «αρυαν, ou ariane est donc l’Orient, par permutation de voyelles» (*αρυαν, or ariane, is therefore the Orient, by permutation of vowels*); the chain closes on «notre aran, iran, airan provençal, l’αρυαν grecque, le soleil levant» (*our Provençal aran, iran, airan, the Greek αρυαν, the rising sun*) [INTRO.5 ¶5]. The same spider returns in the Theseus reading: «Thésée est θεσ-ειος, la lumière organisée, manifestée, qui se sépare d’Ariane, l’araignée qui est au centre de sa toile» (*Theseus is θεσ-ειος, organised, manifested light, which separates from Ariane, the spider at the centre of her web*) [PARIS.4 ¶3]. The Cretan labyrinth's name is matched by nearness only: «Absolum. Or, nous ferons remarquer que ce terme est voisin d’Absolu, qui est le nom par lequel les alchimistes anciens désignaient la pierre philosophale» (*Absolum. Now, we shall point out that this term is close to Absolu, the name by which the old alchemists designated the philosopher's stone*) [INTRO.5 ¶7].

**Saints and statues.** «la cabale phonétique découvre un autre sens, adéquat et conforme à la doctrine hermétique. Christophe est mis pour Chrysophe : qui porte l’or» (*phonetic cabala discovers another meaning, adequate and in accordance with hermetic doctrine. Christophe stands for Chrysophe: he who carries gold*) [INTRO.9 ¶15]. The Parvis statue that the people called *Monsieur Legris* is explained through Amédée de Ponthieu (QUOTED: Amédée de Ponthieu, adopted as the best explanation because «l’auteur, qui n’était point hermétiste, juge sans parti pris», *the author, who was no hermetist, judges without bias* [INTRO.9 ¶10]): «il se nommait aussi messire Legris, alors que gris signifiait feu, et particulièrement feu grisou, feu follet» (*he was also named messire Legris, when gris meant fire, and particularly firedamp, will-o'-the-wisp*) [INTRO.9 ¶11]; the note glosses *Phœbigène* as «Engendré du soleil ou de l’or» (*Begotten of the sun or of gold*) [INTRO.9 n2]. The devil-figure the people called *Maistre Pierre du Coignet*, «ce qui ne laissait pas d’embarrasser les archéologues» (*which did not fail to embarrass the archaeologists*), is read as «le symbole de notre pierre angulaire, la pierre du coin, la maîtresse pierre du coignet» (*the symbol of our corner stone, the stone of the corner, the master stone of the coignet*), the first matter shown as «Lucifer (qui porte la lumière, — l’étoile du matin)» (*Lucifer (who bears the light — the morning star)*) [INTRO.4 ¶6]. The Chartres labyrinth «vulgairement appelé la lieue (pour le lieu)» (*commonly called la lieue (for le lieu)*) [INTRO.5 ¶1]. Jesse: «le mot hébreu Jes signifie le feu, le soleil, la divinité. Etre de la tige de Jessé, c’est donc être de la race du soleil, du feu» (*the Hebrew word Jes means fire, sun, divinity. To be of the stem of Jesse is therefore to be of the race of the sun, of fire*) [PARIS.1 ¶10]. Elie is glossed «Elie (Ηλι ος, soleil)» (*Elie (Ηλι ος, sun)*) [PARIS.2 ¶9].

**Festivals and small words.** The Feast of the Ass's "land of Saba" becomes «ces mystificateurs du pays de Saba, ou Caba, les cabalistes en personne !» (*those mystifiers of the land of Saba, or Caba, the cabalists in person!*) [INTRO.1 ¶14]. The choirboys' whipping-tops: «En cabale, sabot équivaut à cabot ou chabot, le chat botté des Contes de ma Mère l’Oie» (*In cabala, sabot equals cabot or chabot, Puss in Boots of Mother Goose's Tales*) [INTRO.1 n4]. Aeneas's *Laurente*: «Cabalistiquement, l’or enté, greffé.» (*Cabalistically, grafted gold.*) [INTRO.7 n1]. Pegasus: «Le coursier de Notre-Dame est le même que le Pégase ailé de la fable (racine πηγη, source)» (*The steed of Notre-Dame is the same as the winged Pegasus of the fable (root πηγη, spring)*) [PARIS.5 ¶2]. May dew: «la Rosée de Mai (Maïa était mère d’Hermès), — humidité vivifiante du mois de Marie, la Vierge mère» (*the Dew of May (Maia was mother of Hermes) — vivifying moisture of the month of Mary, the Virgin mother*) [PARIS.6 ¶25]; Thomas Corneille (QUOTED: Thomas Corneille) is reported as saying the Rose-Croix masters were called «Frères de la Rosée-Cuite, signification qu’ils donnaient eux-mêmes aux initiales de leur ordre : F. R. C.» (*Brothers of the Cooked Dew, the meaning they themselves gave to the initials of their order: F. R. C.*) [PARIS.6 ¶24]. Saturn: «le Saturne des Sages, dénomination anagrammatique de natures» (*the Saturn of the Sages, anagrammatic name of natures*) [PARIS.7 ¶22]. The nostoc: «Ce mot vient du grec νυξ, νυχτος, correspondant au latin nox, noctis, nuit. C’est donc une chose qui naît la nuit» (*This word comes from the Greek νυξ, νυχτος, corresponding to the Latin nox, noctis, night. It is therefore a thing born at night*) [AMIENS ¶36]. The Hebrew chain of *hur, heur, heurim* leading to *heureux* is Pierre Dujols's (QUOTED: Pierre Dujols): «Cette transcription de l’hébreu plus ou moins variable (hur, heur, hurim, heurim) nous conduit au mot heureux» (*This more or less variable transcription of the Hebrew (hur, heur, hurim, heurim) leads us to the word heureux*) [PARIS.3 ¶28].

**Bourges: oak, gall, cock, kermes.** The subject's vulgar name is given only by an approximation «dont l’équivalent répond au chêne» (*whose equivalent answers to the oak*) [BOURGES.2 ¶21]; then: «si nous rapprochons trois mots de la même famille latine : galla, Gallia, gallus, nous obtenons galle, Gaule, coq» (*if we bring together three words of the same Latin family: galla, Gallia, gallus, we obtain gall, Gaul, cock*); the oak also gives «le Kermès, qui a, dans la Gaye Science, la même signification que Hermès, les consonnes initiales étant permutantes» (*Kermes, which has in the Gay Science the same meaning as Hermes, the initial consonants being permutable*); «le kermès (en arabe girmiz, qui teint en écarlate) caractérise la substance préparée» (*kermes (in Arabic girmiz, which dyes scarlet) characterises the prepared substance*) [BOURGES.2 ¶22]. «Gala, en grec, signifie lait, et le mercure est encore appelé Lait de Vierge (lac virginis)» (*Gala, in Greek, means milk, and mercury is also called Virgin's Milk (lac virginis)*) [BOURGES.2 ¶23]. Tristan's title: «l’hiéroglyphe de fabrication du Lion vert, — d’où le nom de Léonois ou Léonnais porté par Tristan» (*the hieroglyph of the making of the Green Lion — whence the name Léonois or Léonnais borne by Tristan*) [BOURGES.1 ¶9]. The pilgrim's shell «sert, dans la symbolique secrète, à désigner le principe Mercure, appelé encore Voyageur ou Pèlerin» (*serves, in the secret symbolism, to designate the principle Mercury, also called Traveller or Pilgrim*), worn by those who «cherchent à obtenir l’étoile (compos stella)» (*seek to obtain the star (compos stella)*) [BOURGES.1 ¶5]; the note adds that Mercury is «l’eau benoîte des Philosophes» (*the blessed water of the Philosophers*) and that large shells once held holy water [BOURGES.1 n1]. Sign and salt: «Sceau d’Hermes, Sel des Sages (Sel mis pour Scel)» (*Seal of Hermes, Salt of the Sages (Sel put for Scel)*) [BOURGES.2 ¶14]; a porcelain fish in a Twelfth-Night cake: «ce poisson était une sole (lat. Sol, solis, le soleil)» (*this fish was a sole (lat. Sol, solis, the sun)*); and the Cosmopolite's fish «Echinéis, est l’oursin (echinus), l’ourson, la petite ourse, constellation dans laquelle se trouve l’étoile polaire» (*Echineis, is the sea-urchin (echinus), the bear-cub, the Little Bear, the constellation in which the pole star lies*) [BOURGES.2 ¶14].

**Jacques Cœur's motto.** The device «A vaillans cuers riens impossible» (*To valiant hearts nothing impossible*) read «selon les règles cabalistiques» (*according to the cabalistic rules*) yields, in the text's words, «lisons cuer avec l’orthographe de l’époque, et nous obtiendrons à la fois : 1° l’énoncé de l’Esprit universel (rayon de lumière) ; 2° le nom vulgaire de la matière basique ouvrée (le fer) ; 3° les trois réitérations» (*let us read cuer with the spelling of the time, and we obtain at once: 1. the statement of the universal Spirit (ray of light); 2. the vulgar name of the basic matter worked (iron); 3. the three reiterations*) [BOURGES.1 ¶4]. The text does not show how *cuer* gives these three; it only asserts them.

**Letters as matter: RERE and RER.** On the Lallemant credence, «RE, ablatif latin de res, signifie la chose, envisagée dans sa matière» (*RE, Latin ablative of res, means the thing, considered in its matter*), RE + RE is two things in one, so «RERE équivaudra ainsi à RE BIS» (*RERE will thus be equivalent to RE BIS*), the Philosophers' compost [BOURGES.2 ¶33]. For the second word: «R, qui est la moitié de RE, signifiera une moitié de chose, de matière. RER équivaut donc à une matière augmentée de la moitié d’une autre ou de la sienne propre» (*R, which is half of RE, will mean a half of a thing, of matter. RER is therefore equivalent to a matter increased by half of another or of its own*) [BOURGES.2 ¶35]; the example given is realgar and its sulphur or arsenic, offered as a supposition [BOURGES.2 ¶35]. The three-fold repetition of both words on the niche is itself read as a number (section 6).

**Hendaye (own words, by the rights rule).** The carved OCRUXAVES / PESUNICA is first restored to the known *O crux ave spes unica*, then the misplaced S is argued to be deliberate: the carver no doubt sketched the letters in chalk or charcoal before cutting, the other motifs are carefully placed, so the apparent fault is a sign set on purpose [HENDAYE ¶7]. The S, serpent-shaped, is given the value of the Greek *khi* and tells the reader to translate into the language of the birds; the result is «Il est écrit que la vie se réfugie en un seul espace» (*It is written that life takes refuge in a single space*) [HENDAYE ¶9].

INRI is read, beyond its usual expansion, as «Igne Natura Renovatur Integra» (*By fire Nature is renewed whole*) [HENDAYE ¶10]. The evangelist Matthew's name is derived from the Greek word for science, making his the Gospel according to Science [HENDAYE ¶15].

## 4. How a carved motif is read: what is looked at, in what order

**Stated: the order.** On the Paris façade the reading follows the building's own order: «nous observerons toujours l’ordre de succession des figures, en allant de l’extérieur vers les ventaux du porche, comme le ferait un fidèle pénétrant dans le sanctuaire» (*we shall always observe the order of succession of the figures, going from the outside towards the leaves of the porch door, as a worshipper entering the sanctuary would*) [PARIS.2 ¶3]. The reading unit is the lowest zone: «Le stylobate de la façade, qui se développe et s’étend sous les trois porches, est tout entier consacré à notre science» (*The stylobate of the façade, which runs under the three porches, is entirely devoted to our science*) [PARIS.2 ¶1], and the sequence is taken as a sequence of operations: «nous suivrons pas à pas le travail de l’Elixir, depuis sa calcination première jusqu’à son ultime coction» (*we shall follow step by step the work of the Elixir, from its first calcination to its final coction*) [PARIS.2 ¶2]. The figures are in rows: twelve medallions below, twelve seated figures above, «Tous présentent des disques garnis d’emblèmes variés ayant trait au labeur alchimique» (*All hold discs furnished with varied emblems relating to the alchemical labour*) [PARIS.3 ¶1].

**Stated: the attribute decides.** What is read first is the object held or shown, matched to a known emblem. The first relief shows «l’image du corbeau, symbole de la couleur noire. La femme qui le tient sur ses genoux symbolise la Putréfaction» (*the image of the raven, symbol of the black colour. The woman holding it on her knees symbolises Putrefaction*) [PARIS.3 ¶2]. The ninth figure raises a stave «ayant quelque apparence d’une douve de tonneau, dont l’essence nous est révélée par la branche de chêne que porte l’écusson» (*looking somewhat like a barrel-stave, whose wood is revealed to us by the oak branch on the shield*) [PARIS.4 ¶13]. Where the attribute is lost, the reading stops short: of the fourth figure's object, «Est-ce un minéral, un fragment d’attribut, un ustensile ou encore quelque morceau d’étoffe ? Nous ne savons.» (*Is it a mineral, a fragment of an attribute, a utensil or some piece of cloth? We do not know.*), and only the Ram, which remains, is read [PARIS.3 ¶23]. The knight's target is «une forme imprécise (quelque rayon ? une gerbe de flammes ?), qu’il est malheureusement impossible d’identifier, tant le relief en est mutilé» (*an imprecise form (a ray? a sheaf of flames?) which it is unfortunately impossible to identify, so mutilated is the relief*) [PARIS.2 ¶25].

**Stated: the texts are the dictionary.** Each emblem is tied to the vocabulary of the Philosophers, and the author marshals citations for that purpose: «Voici quelques citations qui pourront éclairer l’investigateur et lui permettre de reconnaître sa route dans ce ténébreux labyrinthe» (*Here are some citations which may enlighten the investigator and let him recognise his road in this dark labyrinth*) [PARIS.3 ¶7], stopping when they add nothing: «Arrêtons ici ces citations, que nous pourrions multiplier sans autre profit pour l’étudiant, et revenons aux figures hermétiques de Notre-Dame» (*Let us stop these citations here, which we could multiply without further profit to the student, and return to the hermetic figures of Notre-Dame*) [PARIS.3 ¶16]. The stone and the text explain each other both ways: the Ram «nous aide à comprendre les paroles de Pernety» (*helps us understand Pernety's words*) about the steel drawn from the belly of Aries [PARIS.3 ¶23]. The alchemist at the fountain on the left buttress is identified with one figure of Abraham le Juif's book: «C’est ce dernier personnage qui forme le sujet du motif sculpté de Notre-Dame de Paris.» (*It is this last figure that forms the subject of the carved motif of Notre-Dame de Paris.*) [PARIS.2 ¶18]. Of the serpent on the golden rod: «nous rencontrons ce symbole durant toute la période médiévale» (*we meet this symbol throughout the medieval period*) [PARIS.3 ¶17].

**Stated: the meaning is a step of the Work, and only sometimes a thing.** The reading translates a scene into an operation and its agents: the unhorsed rider is «la rectification de l’esprit obtenu et la cohobation de cet esprit sur la matière grave» (*the rectification of the spirit obtained and the cohobation of that spirit upon the heavy matter*) [PARIS.5 ¶2]; the mirror, tree and horn: «Le miroir symbolise le début de l’ouvrage, l’arbre de vie en marque la fin, et la corne d’abondance le résultat» (*The mirror symbolises the beginning of the work, the tree of life marks its end, and the horn of plenty the result*) [PARIS.5 ¶3]; the brawling children are the clearest writing of «l’action de l’eau pontique sur la matière grave» (*the action of the pontic water on the heavy matter*) [PARIS.5 ¶13]. The set of medallions is read as a grouping «des points variables dans la pratique de la Solution» (*of the variable points in the practice of Solution*), and the solution alone «suffit, en effet, à identifier la voie suivie» (*suffices, indeed, to identify the way followed*) [PARIS.5 ¶14].

**Stated: one hieroglyph, many stories.** A motif is confirmed by lining it up with myths and legends that are held to carry the same hieroglyph. At Rocamadour, «L’épée qui ouvre le rocher, la verge de Moïse qui fait jaillir l’eau de la pierre d’Horeb, le sceptre de la déesse Rhée, dont elle frappe le mont Dyndime, le javelot d’Atalante sont un seul et même hiéroglyphe» (*The sword that opens the rock, the rod of Moses that makes water spring from the stone of Horeb, the sceptre of the goddess Rhea with which she strikes Mount Dindymus, the javelin of Atalanta are one and the same hieroglyph*) [INTRO.9 ¶16]. The combat that yields the solvent «a fourni le sujet de quantité de fables profanes et d’allégories sacrées» (*has furnished the subject of a quantity of profane fables and sacred allegories*): Cadmos, Apollon, Jason, Horus, Hercule, Persée, saint Michel, saint Georges, saint Marcel, the cock and the fox, Cyliani's dragon, Cyrano's remora and salamander [BOURGES.1 ¶9]. The Round Table romances are «légendes hermétiques traditionnelles renouvelées des fables grecques» (*traditional hermetic legends renewed from Greek fables*) [BOURGES.1 ¶7]. The direction can be reversed to mock historians: «Et voici comment, à l’aide de fables hermétiques, de célèbres auteurs ont écrit l’Histoire !» (*And this is how, with hermetic fables, famous authors wrote History!*) [PARIS.5 ¶9].

**Stated: buildings read against buildings.** Amiens is used as a second witness to Paris: «le porche central de Notre-Dame d’Amiens, — porche du Sauveur, — est la reproduction à peu près fidèle, non seulement des motifs qui ornent le portail de Paris, mais encore de la succession qu’ils y affectent» (*the central porch of Notre-Dame d'Amiens — porch of the Saviour — is the nearly faithful reproduction not only of the motifs that adorn the Paris portal but of the order they take there*) [AMIENS ¶1]. Printed books of figures serve the same way: the Azoth woodcut, Trismosin, the Douze Clefs, Aurach's manuscript, Piccolpassi's plates [PARIS.2 ¶8; PARIS.2 ¶20; PARIS.4 ¶14; BOURGES.2 ¶5; BOURGES.2 ¶36].

**Interpreted: the implied procedure.** Taken together, the Paris and Amiens readings follow a fixed course: find the zone the building reserves to "our science"; take the figures in the building's own order; identify each attribute; name it with the Philosophers' word; place it as an operation in the sequence; confirm by colour, by parallel figures elsewhere, by legend and by earlier witnesses. This summary is ours; it rests on PARIS.2 ¶1–3, PARIS.3 ¶1–2, PARIS.4 ¶13, PARIS.5 ¶14, PARIS.5 ¶16–18 and AMIENS ¶1.

## 5. What counts as evidence

**Stated: irregularity proves intention.** The strongest argument the book uses is the breach of decorative symmetry. On the Virgin portal the seven circles of the sarcophagus repeat two by two around a different centre, «ce qui n’a jamais lieu dans les motifs purement décoratifs de l’art ogival» (*which never happens in the purely decorative motifs of ogival art*) [PARIS.6 ¶7]; and the line of four crosses and three crooks, with a gap, is decisive: «c’est ce qui achève de prouver que le sens symbolique est voulu, c’est qu’un espace, celui de gauche, demeure libre» (*what finally proves that the symbolic meaning is intended is that one space, the left one, remains empty*) [PARIS.6 ¶9]. On the zodiac the objection is stated and met: «On nous objectera sans doute que le zodiaque peut ne pas avoir une portée occulte et représenter tout uniment la zone des constellations. C’est chose possible.» (*It will doubtless be objected that the zodiac may have no occult import and simply represent the zone of the constellations. That is possible.*); but «Or, à Gemini succède Leo, lequel usurpe la place de Cancer, rejeté sur le pilier opposé» (*Now, Gemini is followed by Leo, which usurps the place of Cancer, thrown back on the opposite pillar*), so «L’imaigier a donc voulu indiquer, par cette habile transposition, la conjonction du ferment philosophique» (*The image-maker therefore meant to indicate, by this skilful transposition, the conjunction of the philosophic ferment*) [PARIS.6 ¶21]. At Hendaye the same argument is made from a misspelt inscription [HENDAYE ¶7].

**Stated: the conventions of the image-makers.** The medieval carvers are credited with exact rules, and a reading that ignores them is faulted. Against the "vision of Ezekiel" explanation of the Amiens quatrefoil: «La première témoigne d’une étude incomplète de la technique traditionnelle, des formules que respectaient les latomi dans l’exécution de leurs symboles. La seconde, plus lourde, relève d’une observation défectueuse.» (*The first [error] shows an incomplete study of the traditional technique, of the formulas the latomi respected in executing their symbols. The second, graver, comes from faulty observation.*) [AMIENS ¶5]. The rules named are: «nos imagiers avaient coutume d’isoler, ou tout du moins de souligner leurs attributs surnaturels à l’aide d’un cordon de nuées» (*our image-makers were accustomed to isolate, or at least to underline, their supernatural attributes with a band of clouds*); and «dans toutes les scènes gothiques figurant des apparitions, l’illuminé est toujours représenté face au phénomène» (*in all Gothic scenes showing apparitions, the one illumined is always shown facing the phenomenon*); here there are no clouds and «notre personnage a les yeux ouverts ; il n’est donc point endormi» (*our figure has his eyes open; so he is not asleep*) [AMIENS ¶6]. Exactness is assumed: the cock-and-fox lacks the dogs of the fable, as G. Durand himself noted (QUOTED: G. Durand) — «On ne voit pas, ajoute G. Durand, le ou les chiens qui sont le complément de la fable» (*One does not see, adds G. Durand, the dog or dogs that complete the fable*) — and «nos aïeux, traducteurs exacts et méticuleux, n’eussent pas négligé de figurer ces acteurs s’il se fût agi d’une scène connue de fabliau» (*our forebears, exact and meticulous translators, would not have omitted these actors had it been a known fabliau scene*) [AMIENS ¶14]. At Bourges the guide's "pestle" is rejected on the same ground: «Nous ne pouvons croire que l’artiste eût négligé de figurer également le mortier, sa contre-partie indispensable» (*We cannot believe the artist would have omitted to figure the mortar as well, its indispensable counterpart*), and the hollow, bevelled end shows a long-necked matras [BOURGES.2 ¶4]. Impossibility in the image is a clue: «il est difficile d’admettre qu’une église puisse être bâtie sur voûtes apparentes et semble ainsi reposer sur quatre pieds» (*it is hard to admit that a church could be built on open vaults and so seem to rest on four feet*), so the "church" is an athanor [AMIENS ¶12]. Furnaces are carved as buildings on purpose: «Il va de soi qu’on ne pouvait, ésotériquement, le reproduire tel qu’il existait au laboratoire. On se bornera donc à lui donner une forme architectonique, sans toutefois en abolir les caractéristiques» (*It goes without saying that one could not, esoterically, reproduce it as it existed in the laboratory. One would therefore give it an architectural form, without abolishing its characteristics*), the parts «cendrier, tour et dôme» (*ash-pit, tower and dome*) remaining [AMIENS ¶13].

**Stated: colour, when it survives, settles the case.** Everything was once painted: «Dans les cathédrales, tout était doré et peint de couleurs vives» (*In the cathedrals everything was gilded and painted in bright colours*) [INTRO.2 n2]. Where paint is gone, readings stay open: for the lion, «nous ne pouvons, devant la nudité actuelle de la pierre, nous contenter d’une seule explication» (*we cannot, before the present nakedness of the stone, be content with a single explanation*) [PARIS.4 ¶16]; «Quelque trace de cinabre, de malachite ou de métal viendrait aussitôt nous tirer d’embarras» (*Some trace of cinnabar, malachite or metal would at once get us out of difficulty*), but «Le lion de pierre conserve son secret !» (*The stone lion keeps its secret!*) [PARIS.4 ¶19]. The remedy named is the rose window: «Notre-Dame conserve elle-même le coloris original des figures de son grand porche» (*Notre-Dame itself preserves the original colouring of the figures of its great porch*) [PARIS.5 ¶16]; Guillaume de Paris «fit reproduire minutieusement les motifs des médaillons sur les vitraux de la rose centrale. Le verre vient ainsi compléter la pierre» (*had the motifs of the medallions minutely reproduced on the glass of the central rose. Glass thus completes stone*) [PARIS.5 ¶17]; «On découvrira là l’intelligence des points douteux de la statuaire» (*There one will find the understanding of the doubtful points of the statuary*): the rider is «un prince couronné d’or, à veste blanche et bas rouges» (*a prince crowned with gold, with white jacket and red hose*), one child green, the other violet-grey, the queen white-crowned in green and purple [PARIS.5 ¶18]. Remembered colour counts too: the old man at the Palace «était jadis peint en vert, tandis que l’intérieur visible du Palais offrait une coloration pourpre» (*was once painted green, while the visible interior of the Palace was coloured purple*), which makes him the Green Lion and the Palace the red [PARIS.5 ¶11].

**Stated: earlier witnesses and the original.** Where the stone is doubtful or restored, documents decide. The king's head on the Saint-Marcel die might be a helmet, «Mais nous possédons, heureusement, le texte d’Esprit Gobineau de Montluisant» (*But we fortunately possess the text of Esprit Gobineau de Montluisant*), written in 1640, which «nous apprend positivement que le roi porte une triple couronne» (*tells us positively that the king wears a triple crown*) [PARIS.7 ¶19]. Against Cambriel's bearded, finger-to-lips bishop: «Le contrôle est aisé, puisque nous possédons l’oeuvre originale, et la supercherie éclate au premier coup d’oeil» (*The check is easy, since we possess the original work, and the trickery is obvious at first glance*) [PARIS.7 ¶32]. A legend recorded by Jacques de Voragine is used as a reading key, the author underlining «les passages et les noms qui se rapportent directement au travail, aux conditions et aux matériaux» (*the passages and names relating directly to the work, the conditions and the materials*) [BOURGES.2 ¶9], and the one detail not in the legend — the belt — «donne à saint Christophe son véritable caractère ésotérique» (*gives saint Christopher his true esoteric character*), credited to the inspiration «du savant hermétiste qui lui avait commandé l’œuvre» (*of the learned hermetist who had commissioned the work from him*) [BOURGES.2 ¶13]; a note says Jean Lallemant belonged to the alchemical fraternity of the Chevaliers de la Table ronde [BOURGES.2 n4].

**Stated: a hidden detail can fix a whole scene.** On the Lallemant ceiling the child with the open book and dead serpent is undecided until «Un détail situé dans la pénombre des moulures, détermine le sens du petit bas-relief» (*A detail set in the half-light of the mouldings determines the sense of the little bas-relief*): the starred seal on the top ingot [BOURGES.2 ¶28]. The credence's riddle remained unread because «personne n’a compris vers quel but était orienté le symbolisme de toute la décoration, ni quelle science se dissimulait sous ses multiples hiéroglyphes» (*no one understood towards what end the symbolism of the whole decoration was directed, nor what science hid under its many hieroglyphs*) [BOURGES.2 ¶30]: the whole decides the part (interpreted from this passage).

**Stated: the people's word and the scholars' embarrassment.** Popular names are treated as preserved tradition: «le peuple, gardien fidèle des traditions orales» (*the people, faithful guardian of oral traditions*) [INTRO.4 ¶2]; a statue the archaeologists could not identify was named by the people «tantôt Monsieur Legris, tantôt Vendeur de gris, Grand Jeûneur de Notre-Dame» (*now Monsieur Legris, now Seller of grey, Great Faster of Notre-Dame*) [INTRO.9 ¶9]. Scholarly puzzlement is itself a pointer (interpreted from INTRO.4 ¶6 and INTRO.9 ¶10).

**Stated: the standard claimed.** The reading is presented as a science with physical checks: «La science que nous étudions est aussi positive, aussi réelle, aussi exacte que l’optique, la géométrie ou la mécanique» (*The science we study is as positive, as real, as exact as optics, geometry or mechanics*), and «Pas un mot qui ne soit pesé, pas une pensée qui n’ait été passée au crible du jugement et de la réflexion» (*Not a word that is not weighed, not a thought that has not been passed through the sieve of judgement and reflection*) [PARIS.7 ¶29]. The Conclusion asks of the student «beaucoup de justesse, d’exactitude, de perspicacité dans l’observation des faits, un esprit sain, logique et pondéré» (*much accuracy, exactness, perspicacity in observing facts, a sound, logical and balanced mind*) [CONCL ¶7].

## 6. Numbers, colours, heraldry, inscriptions, zodiac, orientation

**Numbers (stated).** Counted elements are read as counted operations or principles. The ladder «aux neuf degrés, scala philosophorum, hiéroglyphe de la patience que doivent posséder ses fidèles, au cours des neufs opérations successives du labeur hermétique» (*of nine rungs, scala philosophorum, hieroglyph of the patience its faithful must have through the nine successive operations of the hermetic labour*) [PARIS.1 ¶3]. «vous y verrez sept cercles : ce sont les symboles des sept métaux planétaires» (*you will see seven circles there: they are the symbols of the seven planetary metals*) [PARIS.6 ¶2], arranged from centre outward as the Cosmopolite orders the planets [PARIS.6 ¶7–8]. «Les quatre croix, de même qu’en la notation spagyrique, représentent les métaux imparfaits ; les crosses à double spirale, les deux parfaits, et la crosse simple, le mercure, demi-métal ou semi-parfait» (*The four crosses, as in spagyric notation, represent the imperfect metals; the double-spiral crooks the two perfect ones, and the single crook mercury, half-metal or semi-perfect*) [PARIS.6 ¶10]. Philalèthe (QUOTED: Philalèthe) on the eagles, «il ne faut pas moins de sept aigles, et l’on devrait même en employer jusqu’à neuf» (*no fewer than seven eagles are needed, and one should even use up to nine*) [PARIS.4 ¶4], is matched with Callimachus's swans circling seven times and «la procession que Josué fit faire sept fois autour de Jéricho» (*the procession Joshua had made seven times round Jericho*) [PARIS.4 ¶5–9; PARIS.4 ¶10]. The six-pointed star: «C’est une figure radiée, à six pointes (digamma), dite Etoile des Mages» (*It is a radiating figure with six points (digamma), called Star of the Magi*) [INTRO.6 ¶13]; at Bourges «C’est l’union des deux triangles du feu et de l’eau, ou du soufre et du mercure assemblés en un seul corps, qui génère l’astre à six pointes» (*It is the union of the two triangles of fire and water, or of sulphur and mercury joined in one body, that generates the six-pointed star*) [BOURGES.2 ¶28]. Three recurs as reiteration: «le chiffre 3 suivi de la lettre R, qui indiquent à l’artiste la nécessité des trois réitérations du même procédé» (*the figure 3 followed by the letter R, which tell the artist the necessity of three reiterations of the same process*) [BOURGES.2 ¶28]; RERE and RER each cut three times, and «les trois grenades ignées du fronton confirment cette triple action d’un unique procédé» (*the three flaming pomegranates of the pediment confirm this triple action of a single process*) [BOURGES.2 ¶32]; the cock will have crowed three times [AMIENS ¶15]. Four is the elements: the cross on the Philosophy disc «l’expression du quaternaire des éléments» (*the expression of the quaternary of the elements*) [PARIS.3 ¶39]; on the Saint-Marcel die a four-petalled flower for the four first elements, four flutings for the four second elements, and «les cinq unités de la quintessence, comprenant les trois principes et les deux natures, puis leur totalisation sous le nombre dix» (*the five units of the quintessence, comprising the three principles and the two natures, then their total under the number ten*) [PARIS.7 ¶22]. Two wheels at Amiens stand for the two turns of one wheel, humid then dry [AMIENS ¶7]. A closed book «scellé par sept bandes ; ce sont les marques des sept opérations successives qui permettent de l’ouvrir» (*sealed with seven bands; these are the marks of the seven successive operations that allow it to be opened*) [BOURGES.2 ¶16]. At Hendaye the scythe is tied to the number seven, the number of transformation and destruction [HENDAYE ¶15].

**Colours (stated).** The three roses of a cathedral, never lit, lit at noon, lit at sunset, display «les couleurs de l’Œuvre, selon un processus circulaire allant des ténèbres» (*the colours of the Work, in a circular process going from darkness*) to the red, through the white [INTRO.6 ¶2]. The rule of reading colour: «Ces couleurs, au nombre de trois, se développent selon l’ordre invariable qui va du noir au rouge en passant par le blanc» (*These colours, three in number, develop in the invariable order that goes from black to red by way of white*); intermediate tints exist but «L’artiste en tient peu de cas parce qu’elles sont superficielles et passagères» (*The artist takes little account of them because they are superficial and passing*) [PARIS.3 ¶25]. The colours are a language: «il exista, de tous temps, une langue des couleurs, intimement unie à la religion, ainsi que le dit Portal, et qui reparaît, au moyen-âge, dans les vitraux des cathédrales gothiques» (*there has always existed a language of colours, intimately united with religion, as Portal says, which reappears in the Middle Ages in the windows of the Gothic cathedrals*) [PARIS.3 ¶26]. Fixed equations: black to Saturn, lead, «c’est le sable de la science héraldique et l’emblème de l’élément terre, de la nuit et de la mort» (*it is the sable of heraldry and the emblem of the element earth, of night and of death*) [PARIS.3 ¶27]; white to purity and the Initiate [PARIS.3 ¶28]; red to fire and sovereignty [PARIS.3 ¶29]; «la Vierge est toujours représentée drapée de bleu (correspondant au noir, ainsi que nous le dirons par la suite), Dieu de blanc et le Christ de rouge» (*the Virgin is always shown draped in blue (corresponding to black, as we shall say later), God in white and Christ in red*) [PARIS.3 ¶31]; blue is the moon's: «toujours vêtue de bleu, couleur symbolique de l’astre des nuits» (*always clothed in blue, symbolic colour of the star of the nights*) [AMIENS ¶39]. Grey: «D’après Aristote, le Mercure a pour couleur emblématique le gris ou le violet, ce qui suffit à expliquer pourquoi les statues de saint Christophe étaient revêtues d’un enduit du même ton» (*According to Aristotle, Mercury has grey or violet as its emblematic colour, which suffices to explain why the statues of saint Christopher were coated in the same tone*) [INTRO.9 ¶15]. Black Virgins: «Il apparaît donc régulier que l’hiéroglyphe humanisé de ce minéral en possède la couleur spécifique et qu’on lui réserve pour habitat les lieux souterrains des temples» (*It therefore appears regular that the humanised hieroglyph of this mineral should have its specific colour and be housed in the underground parts of temples*) [INTRO.8 ¶4]; for their processions «on ne brûlait que des cierges de couleur verte» (*only green candles were burnt*) [INTRO.8 ¶12]. A caution against reading colour literally: the Green Lion «est appelé Lion vert, non pas tant parce qu’il possède une coloration verte, que parce qu’il n’a point acquis les caractères minéraux qui distinguent chimiquement l’état adulte de l’état naissant» (*is called Green Lion not so much because it is green in colour as because it has not acquired the mineral characters that chemically distinguish the adult from the nascent state*) [PARIS.4 ¶17]; and the painted motto Etteilla preserved (QUOTED: Etteilla's painting), «Ne vous en rapportez point trop à la couleur» (*Do not rely too much on colour*), leads to the teaching that the *régime* matters more than the colour [PARIS.3 ¶33; PARIS.3 ¶35].

**Heraldry (stated).** The raven is «Principale figure du blason hermétique» (*Principal figure of the hermetic blazon*) [PARIS.6 ¶18]. The shield of saint Thomas d'Aquin is blazoned in heraldic terms, field by field [PARIS.8 ¶6], and valued because «il dévoile les secrets relatifs à l’extraction du mercure et à sa conjonction avec le soufre, points obscurs de la pratique sur lesquels tous les auteurs ont préféré garder un silence religieux» (*it unveils the secrets of the extraction of mercury and its conjunction with sulphur, obscure points of practice on which all authors preferred to keep a religious silence*) [PARIS.8 ¶7]. Arms and mottoes are read as rebuses: Jacques Cœur's three hearts and his device [BOURGES.1 ¶4]; the coupled shell and heart as «un rébus imposé sur le nom du propriétaire, ou sa signature stéganographique» (*a rebus imposed on the owner's name, or his steganographic signature*) [BOURGES.1 ¶5]; Nicolas Rollin's tiled device, read as a rebus whose star is «l’unique, la seule étoile» (*the unique, the only star*) [AMIENS ¶40].

**Inscriptions (stated).** Carved words are read in the same two layers as images. *Virgini parituræ* on the black Virgins is glossed «à la Vierge qui doit enfanter» (*to the Virgin who is to give birth*) and tied to Isis statues bearing the same words [INTRO.8 ¶4]. The Parvis fountain's distich is given with its translation [INTRO.9 ¶5–8]; the Limoux well's line is translated and its water is tied to the *eau pontique* to which the author promises to return [PARIS.2 ¶22–24]. A house at Tousson carries «PIERRE BELLE» and the note explains that alchemy «portait encore le nom d’Agriculture céleste, et ses Adeptes celui de Laboureurs» (*also bore the name of celestial Agriculture, and its Adepts that of Ploughmen*) [PARIS.4 n2]. The Saint-Marcel die is read with Hermes's «Rex ab igne veniet, ac conjugio gaudebit et occulta patebunt» (*The king will come from the fire, will rejoice in the marriage, and hidden things will be revealed*) [PARIS.7 ¶19]. Letters can be matter (RERE, RER) [BOURGES.2 ¶31–35] or an engraved error can be a sign (Hendaye) [HENDAYE ¶7].

**Zodiac (stated).** On the Virgin portal the signs run upward Ariès, Taurus, Gemini: «Ce sont les mois printaniers indiquant le début du travail et le temps propice aux opérations» (*These are the spring months indicating the start of the work and the time favourable to the operations*) [PARIS.6 ¶20]. The bull is doubly read: «Envisagé comme signe zodiacal, c’est le second mois des opérations préparatoires dans le premier œuvre» (*Considered as a zodiac sign, it is the second month of the preparatory operations in the first work*); as a practical figure it is Sulphur, the male principle [PARIS.4 ¶21].

**Plan, orientation, and the parts of the building (stated).** The cross plan is the crucible (section 3). Orientation: the faithful enter from the west and walk east, «Ils quittent les ténèbres et vont vers la lumière.» (*They leave darkness and go towards the light.*) [INTRO.6 ¶1]. The central rose: «Au moyen âge, la rose centrale des porches se nommait Rota, la roue. Or, la roue est l’hiéroglyphe alchimique du temps nécessaire à la coction de la matière philosophale» (*In the Middle Ages the central rose of the porches was called Rota, the wheel. Now the wheel is the alchemical hieroglyph of the time necessary for the coction of the philosophal matter*) [INTRO.6 ¶3]; the flame-like tracery explains the name of the style: «la prépondérance du symbole igné, qui caractérise nettement la dernière période de l’art médiéval, a fait donner au style de cette époque le nom de Gothique flamboyant» (*the preponderance of the fiery symbol, which clearly characterises the last period of medieval art, gave the style of that period the name Flamboyant Gothic*) [INTRO.6 ¶12]. The floor labyrinth is «emblématique du travail entier de l’Œuvre» (*emblematic of the entire work of the Work*) with its two difficulties, the way in and the way out [INTRO.5 ¶3]. The crypt houses the black Virgins, read as «la terre primitive» (*the primitive earth*) that the artist must choose [INTRO.8 ¶4]. Fountains and wells inside churches are the *Fontaine de Vie* made real [PARIS.2 ¶21]. The pagan content of the labyrinth at Chartres is treated as proof: «C’est encore là une preuve de l’infiltration des sujets païens dans l’iconographie chrétienne et, conséquemment, celle d’un sens mytho-hermétique évident» (*This is again a proof of the infiltration of pagan subjects into Christian iconography and, consequently, of an evident mytho-hermetic meaning*) [INTRO.5 ¶1]; the same inference "pagan, therefore hermetic" underlies the reading of the feasts (interpreted from INTRO.1 ¶11–15).

## 7. Who wrote the stone book, and why it is veiled

**Stated.** The builders: cathedrals were «Edifiées par les Frimasons médiévaux pour assurer la transmission des symboles et de la doctrine hermétique» (*Built by the medieval Frimasons to ensure the transmission of the symbols and of the hermetic doctrine*) [PARIS.8 ¶1]; they were «Artisans anonymes de purs chefs-d’œuvre, ils édifièrent pour la Vérité, pour l’affirmation de leur idéal, pour la propagation et la noblesse de leur science» (*Anonymous artisans of pure masterpieces, they built for Truth, for the affirmation of their ideal, for the propagation and nobility of their science*) [INTRO.9 ¶21]. Art and science left the monasteries for the building [INTRO.2 ¶4]. Later Adepts did the same on their own houses: Flamel «se plaisait à revêtir d’emblèmes et d’hiéroglyphes les constructions qu’il élevait de tous côtés» (*delighted in clothing with emblems and hieroglyphs the buildings he raised on every side*) [PARIS.8 ¶2]; Jean Lallemant «préféra-t-il confier à la pierre, plutôt qu’au vélin, le témoignage irrécusable d’une science immense» (*preferred to entrust to stone rather than to vellum the irrefutable witness of an immense science*) [BOURGES.2 ¶37]; a Paris building he saw built in his student days carries the eagle and lion, «l’emblème encombrant, imposé par une volonté intransigeante et supérieure» (*the cumbersome emblem imposed by an uncompromising and superior will*) [PARIS.4 ¶12]. The Renaissance broke the chain, its artists «tout à fait ignorants de la symbolique médiévale» (*wholly ignorant of medieval symbolism*) [INTRO.9 ¶19]; in Gothic work «la facture demeure soumise à l’Idée» (*the workmanship remains subject to the Idea*) [INTRO.9 ¶21].

**Stated.** The clergy are shown as outside the secret. The Feast of Fools was «kermesse hermétique processionnelle» (*hermetic processional fair*), a «Satire hilarante d’un clergé ignorant, soumis à l’autorité de la Science déguisée» (*Hilarious satire of an ignorant clergy, subject to the authority of disguised Science*) [INTRO.1 ¶11]; the Feast of the Ass a «Parodie grotesque que le prêtre, incapable de comprendre, acceptait en silence» (*Grotesque parody that the priest, unable to understand, accepted in silence*) [INTRO.1 ¶14]; the odd customs are ones «où transparaît un sens hermétique souvent très pur» (*in which a hermetic sense, often very pure, shows through*) [INTRO.1 ¶15].

**Stated.** Why veiled: the mysteries of Isis and Ceres are the model, where «La révélation aux profanes du secret de ces pratiques était punie de mort» (*The revelation to the profane of the secret of these practices was punished by death*) [INTRO.8 ¶14]. The Philosophers' writing rule is given twice over: «les Philosophes ne parlent clairement que lorsqu’ils veulent écarter les profanes de leur Table ronde» (*the Philosophers speak clearly only when they want to keep the profane away from their Round Table*), so that clear descriptions of régimes are to be judged «fausses et chimériques» (*false and chimerical*) [PARIS.3 ¶34]; the raven's recurrence «permet aux auteurs de jeter la confusion dans l’ordre des opérations» (*allows the authors to throw confusion into the order of the operations*) [PARIS.3 ¶4], and «Nos vieux maîtres ont donc eu beau jeu pour couvrir l’arcane d’un voile épais» (*Our old masters thus had an easy game covering the arcanum with a thick veil*) [PARIS.3 ¶6]. An anonymous author is cited for the motive: men would no longer esteem the matter if they knew it was something they use [AMIENS ¶32]. Reading advice follows: a text is useful «à condition toutefois qu’on sache le lire avec prudence et le comprendre avec sagesse» (*provided however one knows how to read it with prudence and understand it with wisdom*), for «l’esprit vivifie, mais la lettre tue» (*the spirit gives life, but the letter kills*) [PARIS.4 ¶15]; the Philosophers are to be read «avec un grain de sel» (*with a grain of salt*) on Philalèthe's advice [PARIS.5 ¶7].

**Stated.** Fulcanelli's own veil is described as a different one. The old way gave «une vérité, exprimée paraboliquement, accompagnée d’une ou de plusieurs allégations spécieuses ou frelatées» (*a truth, expressed parabolically, accompanied by one or more specious or adulterated allegations*); he instead scattered the mystery «en le morcelant au hasard des emblèmes» (*by breaking it up at random among the emblems*), so that only the perspicacious can reassemble it; «nous ne pensons pas qu’on nous reprochera jamais d’avoir écrit un seul mensonge» (*we do not think we shall ever be reproached with having written a single lie*); and where he could go no further without breaking his oath, «nous avons préféré le silence aux allusions décevantes, le mutisme à l’abus de confiance» (*we preferred silence to deceptive allusions, muteness to abuse of trust*) [AMIENS ¶21]. The oath binds the highest points: «Nous touchons au plus haut secret de l’Œuvre et désirons tenir notre serment» (*We touch the highest secret of the Work and wish to keep our oath*) [PARIS.6 ¶25]; «pour ne point attirer sur notre tête la malédiction des Philosophes, en révélant ce qu’ils ont cru devoir laisser dans l’ombre» (*so as not to draw on our head the curse of the Philosophers by revealing what they thought right to leave in shadow*) [PARIS.3 ¶35]. The book ends on Zoroaster's «Scire. Potere. Audere. Tacere.» (*To know. To be able. To dare. To keep silent.*) [CONCL ¶1], and «La Nature n’ouvre pas à tous, indistinctement, la porte du sanctuaire.» (*Nature does not open the door of the sanctuary to all indiscriminately.*) [CONCL ¶3]; the successful Sage «restera fidèle à son vœu de silence» (*will remain faithful to his vow of silence*) [CONCL ¶14].

## 8. Where Fulcanelli declines to decode, and warns against arbitrary reading

**Stated: he reads only what is plainly profane.** Of the biblical scenes above the buttress reliefs: «comme l’ésotérisme se cache ici sous des dehors sacrés et des scènes bibliques, nous éviterons d’en parler, afin de ne point encourir le reproche d’une interprétation arbitraire» (*since esotericism here hides under sacred appearances and biblical scenes, we shall avoid speaking of it, so as not to incur the reproach of an arbitrary interpretation*); though the old masters read Scripture alchemically, «nous préférons cependant nous en tenir exclusivement aux motifs dont le caractère profane est indiscutable» (*we prefer nonetheless to keep strictly to the motifs whose profane character is indisputable*) [PARIS.2 ¶26]. Of the saint Marcel statue itself: it is «dépourvue, selon nous, de toute signification secrète» (*devoid, in our view, of any secret meaning*); «Dé et socle ont, seuls, une réelle valeur hermétique» (*Die and plinth alone have real hermetic value*) [PARIS.7 ¶11]; and Cambriel is faulted precisely for having «voulu étendre, sans preuve, le symbolisme du dé à la statue elle-même» (*wanted to extend, without proof, the symbolism of the die to the statue itself*) [PARIS.7 ¶28].

**Stated: he leaves his own thesis open.** «D’autres, plus érudits ou plus savants, en donneront peut-être une interprétation meilleure, car nous ne prétendons imposer à personne la thèse développée ici» (*Others, more erudite or learned, will perhaps give a better interpretation, for we do not claim to impose on anyone the thesis developed here*) [PARIS.7 ¶28]. The Amiens citadel is left between three readings — the symbol of the esotericism unfolded in the other hundred and fifteen quatrefoils, the source of Khunrath's fortress, the Palace of the king — with «Quoiqu’il en soit, citadelle ou demeure royale» (*Be that as it may, citadel or royal dwelling*) [AMIENS ¶17]; the mass under the dew may be a fleece or the Magnesia, «Sans infirmer cette opinion, il est tout aussi vraisemblable d’y soupçonner un corps différent» (*Without invalidating this opinion, it is just as plausible to suspect a different body there*) [AMIENS ¶34]; the Jacques-Cœur group may be Tristan and Yseult, «Nous n’y contredirons pas, le sujet ne changeant rien, d’ailleurs, à l’expression symbolique qu’il dégage» (*We shall not contradict it, the subject changing nothing, moreover, in the symbolic expression it gives off*) [BOURGES.1 ¶7]; the Lallemant room: «Chapelle, studio ou oratoire ? Nous posons la question sans la résoudre.» (*Chapel, studio or oratory? We pose the question without resolving it.*) [BOURGES.2 ¶18]. He leaves the reader to sort the star legends: «Nous laisserons au lecteur le soin d’établir tous rapprochements utiles, de coordonner les versions, d’isoler la vérité positive combinée à l’allégorie légendaire» (*We shall leave to the reader the care of making all useful comparisons, coordinating the versions, isolating the positive truth combined with legendary allegory*) [INTRO.6 ¶15].

**Stated: he stops where the stone is lost.** Two medallions «ont, en effet, souffert de mutilations trop profondes pour qu’il soit possible d’en rétablir le sens» (*have indeed suffered mutilations too deep for it to be possible to restore their meaning*) [PARIS.5 ¶1]; the lion and the fourth figure's object are left (sections 4–5). Restoration is treated as destruction of meaning: on the new Saint-Marcel pillar «le symbole en est tronqué, la science mutilée, la clef perdue, l’ésotérisme éteint» (*the symbol is truncated, the science mutilated, the key lost, the esotericism extinguished*), whereas weathering is harmless: «Le temps corrode, use, désagrège, effrite le calcaire ; la netteté en souffre, mais le sens demeure» (*Time corrodes, wears, disaggregates, crumbles the limestone; sharpness suffers, but the meaning remains*) [PARIS.7 ¶14]. The reader is sent to the original in the museum [PARIS.7 ¶16]. (A footnote says the pillar has since returned to Notre-Dame and that most of the dragon is lost; it refers to "Alchimie, op. cit."; whose voice it is cannot be told from the text [PARIS.7 n9].)

**Stated: he stops where his oath binds.** On the secret fire: «Nous touchons ici au plus haut secret de l’Œuvre» (*Here we touch the highest secret of the Work*), he confesses «nous fûmes arrêté nous-même par cette difficulté pendant plus de vingt ans» (*we were ourselves stopped by this difficulty for more than twenty years*), and «nous ne pouvons faire plus que signaler l’écueil» (*we can do no more than point out the reef*), referring to Artephius, Pontanus and the *Epistola de Igne Philosophorum* [PARIS.3 ¶20]. On the May dew, the extraction from «un corps particulier, abject et méprisé» (*a particular body, abject and despised*) is withheld, «s’il n’était des bornes infranchissables» (*were there not impassable bounds*) [PARIS.6 ¶25]. On RER: «il ne nous est pas permis de déchirer le voile de mystère qu’il recouvre» (*we are not permitted to tear the veil of mystery it covers*); «Comment donner de suffisantes indications sans devenir parjure ?» (*How give sufficient indications without becoming perjured?*) [BOURGES.2 ¶34]. On the matter's name: «La phrase dont nous nous servons peut sembler équivoque ; nous le regrettons, mais on ne saurait parler mieux sans outrepasser certaines bornes» (*The phrase we use may seem equivocal; we regret it, but one could not speak better without overstepping certain bounds*) [BOURGES.2 ¶21]. On night and generation: «Nous pourrions dire beaucoup plus, mais nous estimons avoir assez parlé.» (*We could say much more, but we judge we have said enough.*) [AMIENS ¶39]. On saint Christopher, no description of the lost colossi survives, and he regrets not being able to say more [INTRO.9 ¶17]. On the Sainte-Chapelle glass, a leaf-by-leaf reading would fill volumes; one panel is given [PARIS.8 ¶8].

**Stated: he warns against readings that lack their reason.** Of the Amiens "apostate": «C’est cette raison, précisément, qu’il eût fallu nous donner, afin de soutenir et d’étayer la thèse proposée par les auteurs cités» (*It is precisely that reason which should have been given us, to support and prop the thesis proposed by the authors cited*) [AMIENS ¶12]. Of the kabbalists' arbitrary combinations [BOURGES.2 ¶21]. Of the difficulty itself: «mais la grosse difficulté gît dans l’interprétation du symbolisme. Comment établir une concordance satisfaisante entre tant d’images diverses, de textes contradictoires ?» (*but the great difficulty lies in interpreting the symbolism. How establish a satisfactory concordance among so many diverse images, contradictory texts?*) [BOURGES.2 ¶20].

## 9. What the text does not say

- It never names the matter of the Work in plain words; the nearest it comes is the oak "approximation" and the hints of the cake, the cat and the Ram [BOURGES.2 ¶21–23]. It says this silence is deliberate [AMIENS ¶21].
- It never sets out the phonetic cabala as rules; it refers the rules to Grasset d'Orcet [HENDAYE ¶9] and shows cases.
- It does not explain the mechanism of several readings it asserts: *cuer* yielding three things [BOURGES.1 ¶4], *cot* (Χο) as light [INTRO.3 ¶6], Léonois as Green Lion [BOURGES.1 ¶9].
- It gives no complete original colour scheme for the Paris façade; it names the rose window as the source and reports a few colours from it [PARIS.5 ¶16–18].
- It does not read the biblical reliefs, the saint Marcel statue, the Sainte-Chapelle beyond one panel, two Paris medallions, or the lion's colour (section 8).
- The promised later explanation of the cock and the oak «Nous expliquerons sans doute plus tard l’étroite relation qui existe entre le coq et le chêne» (*We shall doubtless explain later the close relation between the cock and the oak*) [AMIENS ¶15] is only partly met, by the gall–kermes reading at Bourges [BOURGES.2 ¶22] (interpreted).

## 10. What Canseliet adds

Canseliet's three prefaces are a later layer in his own voice. On the method they add the following.

- **The book's achievement, in his words.** Thanks to Fulcanelli the Gothic cathedral yields its secret, and the medieval marvels hold the same positive truth as the pyramids, Greek temples, Roman catacombs and Byzantine basilicas [PREF1 ¶6–7]. For hermetists the gain is elsewhere: «c’est de la confrontation du Livre et de l’Edifice que l’Esprit se dégage et que la lettre meurt» (*it is from the confrontation of the Book and the Building that the Spirit emerges and the letter dies*) [PREF1 ¶9]. Fulcanelli made the first effort; the last is left to them [PREF1 ¶9].
- **A key placed in a picture.** Canseliet reports, on Fulcanelli's assurance (QUOTED: Fulcanelli, through Canseliet), that the key of the major arcanum is given by one of the book's figures, and «Et cette clef consiste tout uniment en une couleur, manifestée à l’artisan dès le premier travail.» (*And this key consists simply in a colour, manifested to the artisan from the first work.*) [PREF1 ¶11]. He does not say which figure or which colour.
- **Cabale against kabbale.** The book's merit, he says, would be justified by this alone: «ce livre a remis en pleine lumière la cabale phonétique dont les principes et leur application étaient tombés dans le plus total oubli» (*this book brought back into full light the phonetic cabala whose principles and their application had fallen into total oblivion*) [PREF2 ¶45]. He separates the two words: *cabale* from a Greek word he gives and from the Latin *caballus*, horse; *kabbale* from the Hebrew *Kabbalah*, tradition; and he opposes the *langue matrice*, understood though never spoken, called by Cyrano Bergerac the instinct or voice of Nature, to the transpositions and calculations of the Jewish kabbalah; Fulcanelli, he says, found again the lost key of the Gay Science, the Language of the Gods or of the Birds, which Jonathan Swift knew and practised [PREF2 ¶45]. This is the explicit distinction that the main text only gestures at [BOURGES.2 ¶21].
- **An epigraph attributed to the master.** A sentence on dissecting words, breaking their bark and freeing the light in them is printed as Fulcanelli's [PREF2 ¶51–52]; it is the sentence of the Introduction [INTRO.3 ¶7].
- **The saint Marcel controversy.** The third preface defends the correction of Cambriel at length: it reproduces Fulcanelli's own verdict on the trickery (QUOTED: Fulcanelli) [PREF3 ¶26], rejects Grillot de Givry's invented sixteenth-century statue and its photograph [PREF3 ¶28–39, ¶47–49], and cites Gobineau de Montluisant's eyewitness of 1640 as proof that the original stood on the pillar then [PREF3 ¶45–46]. Canseliet also states that the original is now kept in the cathedral's north tower [PREF3 ¶44], and, reading the saint's legend, sees in the dragon led round and sent to the desert or the sea the allegory of the dry and humid ways [PREF3 ¶43]. Only this last point is a reading the main text does not make; it is Canseliet's.
- **On method generally.** He names the whole technique as the ancient *Solve et coagula*, simple and linear, demanding sincerity, resolution, patience and imagination [PREF3 ¶12]; and he notes that the third edition is illustrated with photographs of the sculptures Champagne had drawn, the camera's infallibility confirming the artist's fidelity [PREF3 ¶10].
