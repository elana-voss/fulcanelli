Le Mystère des Cathédrales — An English Reading

The Great Work in Plain Language

This page states, from the French text of Fulcanelli’s book on the cathedrals alone, what a practitioner needs before starting the Great Work it describes, and the Work itself, step by step. The passages are given in The Great Work in Plain Terms. The book never sets the Work out as one list, though Fulcanelli says one cathedral front lets the reader follow it step by step, from its first burning down to its last cooking, and he holds part of it back under oath; so the order here follows the book’s main line and says where the book is firm. What the book leaves open, says in two ways or withholds is said where it arises and listed again at the end. Canseliet’s additions in his prefaces to the first, second and third editions have their own section.

What the work is

What is worked on. A mineral as it comes out of the mine, deep under the rock: by the old texts he cites, a black, heavy, brittle, crumbly substance that looks like a stone and can be crushed into small pieces like one. It is common and despised. An old text he calls clear says it contains all the metals and is not itself a metal. Its names, he says, refer either to its magnet-like attraction for the colouring principle or to the ease with which it melts.

What does the work. A hidden agent that works as a fire but looks more like a water than a flame: the living spark the Creator gave to inert matter, the spirit shut inside things. Roused by the steady outer heat the worker keeps up, it drives the cooking and brings about what he sees in his vessel. The worker creates nothing, since only God creates; he brings about a generation in his materials, with the help of nature.

What it is for. To bring the raw matter, by cooking, to its finished state. Raw and finished are two things alike in kind and origin, the finished derived from the raw, and they differ only in purity, stability in fire and ripeness. Finished, it is a red crystal or powder fit to turn common metals into gold; a white form of it turns base metals into silver. Fulcanelli says it gives its owner knowledge, fortune and health.

What you need before you begin

Understanding

  1. You must know the raw matter. You choose it at the very start, with the right season. Fulcanelli never names it plainly, but says that if you attend to what he wrote of the cake shared at Epiphany, and know why the Egyptians made the cat a god, you will be in no doubt which matter to choose.

  2. You must know the vessel, and how to make it. Without perfect knowledge of the vessel and of how it is made, Fulcanelli affirms, not the least success is possible. Make it yourself, undertake nothing until you understand it, and seek it first; the compound, he says, will then be easy to know. The potter’s craft would teach you more about it than glassmaking.

  3. You must know the old texts, and how to read them. Study the classic texts until you can make out the obscure points of their working instructions; one old text, Fulcanelli says, must be read with prudence, by its meaning rather than its letter. The old masters speak clearly only to keep outsiders away, so their limpid descriptions of the stages and their colours are to be judged false.

  4. You must learn from nature. Set aside theories accepted on the strength of books or of their authors’ fame, think with your own head, and ask nature for the truth of the science’s principles, the knowledge of its teaching and the practice of its work.

  5. If you work the old way, you must know the weights. The prepared water comes from a set quantity of water taking in a certain part of the colouring principle, so in the old way the exact proportions must be known, though even the sincerest authors veil them. If ordinary gold replaces that colouring part, the excess water can be distilled off, and weighing comes down to judging consistency.

Character

  1. You need patience. The book pictures it as a ladder of nine rungs, for the nine successive operations of the labour.

  2. You need a sound mind and a pure heart. The work demands exact, sharp observation of facts, a sound, logical and balanced mind, a lively imagination without excitement, and an ardent, pure heart. Enthusiasm and faith help, but must be ruled by reasoning and tested by experience.

  3. You must bring your whole life into tune with the search. Without that, no one can hope to possess the great secret. Study and perseverance are not enough without a solid principle, or if enthusiasm blinds reason, pride rules judgement or greed takes hold.

  4. You must be prudent and silent. The work is hidden, and prudence and care for silence must be carried far in it.

  5. You may not be let in. Nature does not open the door of its sanctuary to everyone without distinction, and the hidden fire, Fulcanelli says, is a mystery whose revelation depends on the Father of Lights.

Practice

  1. You must work in darkness. Since the work is a generation, its first condition is the total absence of sunlight, even diffuse or filtered: the sun destroys substances too young to resist it. You may work by day if you wish, Fulcanelli says, but do not blame him if your efforts end only in failure.

  2. You must use the night and the spring. The moisture the work needs condenses only at night, under a clear sky and in calm air, most abundantly in spring, and the spring months mark the beginning of the work. The name of a night-growing plant the old masters took as the image of their matter shows, he says, a thing born at night, which needs the night to grow and can be worked only at night.

  3. You need the right equipment. A furnace with an ash-pit, a tower and a dome, openings to watch the phases, and fittings inside to hold the flask. A flask of clear glass without bubbles, round or egg-shaped, with walls thick enough to resist the pressure inside and a neck that can be sealed shut at a lamp.

The Work, step by step

The book pictures the whole labour as a maze with two great difficulties: the way in to the centre, where two opposed natures fight, and the way out. It is firm on a few points of order: the raw matter is first prepared with hard labour, after which the labour comes down to keeping the fire; the cooking runs in a wet turn and then a dry one, its colours going from black through white to red, always in that order. It says that one process is repeated three times and that the product is multiplied by further cookings, but not where these fall. It divides the Work into a first part and a second, which begins with the cooking of the double compound. The remaining steps follow its main line; it counts the operations in several ways it does not reconcile.

Step 1: Open the raw matter

The first labour is to break the raw mineral down and draw out its pure part. Once these hard labours are done, Fulcanelli says, the rest comes down to keeping the fire, which a woman spinning at her distaff could easily do.

  • How it is done: Break the matter down and try to isolate its pure part.
  • What happens: You obtain a water with the power to colour, which, the book says, carries a hidden gold within it. It gains this power only through these first preparations.
  • A first rotting: A valuable old manuscript he quotes says the matter’s elements cannot be drawn out unless it is rotted and killed, and gives the signs: some sourness, a smell of the grave, small bubbles like fish eyes, a froth. The fermentation, it says, is long, and done by the hidden fire, the only agent that can open the matter, raise it and make it rot.
  • Cautions: This first rotting must not be confused with the later ones: the black appears several times, which lets the old authors confuse the order of the operations. A quoted author puts the first of four rottings in the first separation; Fulcanelli does not say at which step it falls.

Step 2: Make the dissolving water

The Work needs a water that dissolves all metals: a hidden fire enclosed in a water. Fulcanelli calls the hidden fire, and the drawing out of the night moisture, the highest secret of the Work; the fire, he says, stopped him for more than twenty years.

  • How it is done: A spirit spread over the earth, the fire of nature without which nothing grows, must be caught as it takes body, in an attracting earth able to take it in and give it a body, which must be taken at the moment of its birth. The spirit condenses into a moisture at night, easily drawn from a particular body, low and despised; how, Fulcanelli does not say, to keep his oath.
  • What the moisture is: Without going into the working method, the book says this spirit, given body in minerals as their colouring principle, is the agent of all metallic colourings, and can be had only by breaking down what nature put together. It does not place this in the order of the Work.
  • The first agent: The first agent used to prepare the water is the matter in its young state, before it reaches the adult mineral state: a sour green fruit beside the red ripe one, as arsenic and lead are to silver and gold. The book pictures its making as a combat between two chemical bodies whose combination yields the hidden dissolver and the vessel of the compound.
  • What it is: A third water, from a first water coming from the raw matter joined with a second, different in nature but of the same kind: mineral and metallic, solid, brittle, looking like a stone and melting very easily, a water set into a stony mass, dry, not wetting the hands, with none of the look of ordinary quicksilver. It can enter all metals, gold above all, and with the body it dissolves it carries out the whole work.
  • Signs: Rightly prepared, the water shows on its surface a pattern of crossing lines, clearer once gold is dissolved in it; the whole work of the craft, Fulcanelli repeats, is to work the water until it bears this sign. Elsewhere the book says a radiating six-pointed figure on the surface of the compound shows the worker he has followed the right road and prepared the mixture rightly; it does not say at which step.
  • Cautions: The hidden fire is not ordinary fire. Fulcanelli may not reveal it, he says, since its revelation depends on the Father of Lights; he points out the danger and names three old works to read.

Step 3: Dissolve the body and bring it back to its first state

Fulcanelli calls the first step taken in practice the finding of the agent able to bring a stable body back to a form like its first substance, to make it raw again. That agent is the dissolving water.

  • Why: The first matters of the Work are metals that fire has killed, the old masters say, and melting has left inert. The masters advise making them raw again by giving them, with a fluid form, the agent they lost in smelting.
  • How it is done: The water softens natural gold and returns it to its first state, in the form of a salt, crumbly and very easily melted. The volatile draws out the stable, pure parts: pour the water on the earth, the spirit on the body, again and again, until the volatile, which throws the stable part off each time, carries it for good. Only so is the hidden stable part of the ripe matter drawn out by the volatile part of the young one.
  • What happens: For a time the gold loses its colour. Once the dissolving is done, the water carries the gold as a mother carries a child, and the gold is reborn within it.
  • The two ways: This dissolving alone shows which of two ways is followed: the Work’s own gold dissolved by the stony water marks the first, ordinary gold dissolved by the masters’ own water the second, which brings the water to life. In the way shown first, while the water is being brought to life, the gold dissolves slowly, without violence; in the second the water seems to attack the metal with a vigour like chemical fizzing. The book does not link this pair of ways to its short dry and long wet ways, nor to its old way.
  • Cautions: Ordinary gold seems to be excluded from the old way unless it has been through two operations that change its properties and fit it for the work; the book names them but does not describe them.

Step 4: Unite the two, little by little

The stable body and the volatile water, of contrary natures, must be joined into one: the first union.

  • How it is done: The union is made little by little, by a painful, tedious series of operations in which the volatile is made to rise again and again, pictured as bringing a light out of a tomb to the surface. By their repetition the heightened water sheds its coarse, earthy parts and excess moisture and seizes a portion of the stable body, which it dissolves and absorbs; an old author he quotes asks for no fewer than seven of these risings, and up to nine.
  • What happens: A violent fight comes first: the two substances, of equal power, one earthy and stable, the other airy and volatile, tear at each other until they make one body of middle quality and even substance, the water brought to life. The water greedily absorbs the colouring principle and holds it so fast that the bond cannot be broken. The result, a compound of the first order, needs only graded cooking to become first a red product, then a second product, then, in a third period, the last.
  • Also required: In the bringing to life of the water, a black powder must be separated from the compound.
  • When: The book places a union of what it calls the leaven with the compound of the water toward the end of the fourth month of the first part; it does not say there what the leaven is. Fulcanelli reads this from the deliberately altered order of zodiac signs on a portal, though he grants a zodiac could have no hidden meaning.

Step 5: The second dissolving: the colouring part

After the hard preparation comes a second dissolving: the colouring part, red or white, is dissolved by the water.

  • How it is done: Fulcanelli tells the worker that it then remains only to follow an old parable, in which ten parts of this water are taken and the fruit of the tree the water came from is put into it at once; he does not say in plain terms what the fruit is.
  • Order: The book shows this second dissolving last among its carvings on dissolving, and does not say where it falls against the cooking.

Step 6: Seal the double compound and cook it

The second part of the Work begins when the double compound, shut in its flask at the centre of the furnace, is cooked. It is one matter both dry and wet, a dry and a wet matter joined with a double hidden property exactly balanced, holding white and red in a proportion like that of a bird’s egg.

  • How it is done: Keep a steady, constant, even outer heat night and day; the hidden fire, roused by it, drives the cooking. The book reports heats from body heat to a heat that keeps lead or tin molten, or more, without choosing.
  • What happens: At the start the compound suffers the breaking apart of its parts and tends to die, an active but gentle beginning pictured as cold and winter; black is the first sign of the decay that follows the perfect mixing. The cooking turns twice: in the wet turn the compound stays melted until a thin skin forms and thickens; the dry turn follows, and ends when the contents appear roasted dry, grainy or powdery, like crystals, sand or ash.
  • The colours: Three, in an order that never changes: black, white, red. The passing shades between them are superficial; the essential colours run through the whole mass and mark a change of state. At the white, the old masters say, the matter is free of all impurity, and it appears as solid grains with a diamond-like gleam.
  • Feeding: The cooking holds several stages, repetitions of one way of working, each a way of making the life the matter received at birth grow, not necessarily shown by colour. Its food must be graded and changed at a certain point, which the book pictures as milk, then meat; after each digestion separate the wastes, or they may infect it.
  • Signs: According to the old masters, the black is the sure mark of future success and of an exactly prepared compound; old authors he quotes add a foul smell, sensed not by the nose but only by the understanding.
  • Cautions: Do not rely too much on colour: learn how one stage differs from the next, not one colour from another. Fulcanelli will not reveal what the old masters left in shadow about the stages.

Step 7: Repeat the process three times

One and the same technique is repeated three times.

  • How it is done: Make a vessel, then your compound. Seal it with care so that no spirit can escape, and heat the whole, as the craft requires, until it is completely burnt down. Put the pure part of the powder obtained back into your compound, seal it in the same vessel, and repeat for a third time.
  • What happens: Following an old doctrine he adopts, the first operation gives the colouring part, a product of the first order; the second, exactly like it, a product of the second order, differing in quality but not in nature; the third, done the same way, the finished product, holding all their powers multiplied in strength and extent.
  • Another account: Elsewhere the book gives a threefold cycle on one matter. The dry water, if a means is found to hold it long in the fire, becomes stable: water turned earth. Since, by an old saying, a dry colouring is useless while dry, this earth is dissolved again in the water that gave it birth, and a second like operation sets it again, now resisting the fire while molten. So is born the first stone-like body, very penetrating and very easily melted, whose qualities a third repetition raises.
  • Order: The book does not say whether this cycle and the three operations above are the same.

Step 8: Multiply it by further cookings

The book speaks of increasing the first or second product this way, and does not say where this falls against the other steps.

  • How it is done: The product can be multiplied only with the help of the water, which plays the passive part in the Work, and by successive cookings or settings.
  • What happens: Fulcanelli reads a carving as showing that the water can change metals in quantity, but gains this power only through a series of later cookings with the colouring part. He offers this reading as likely, not certain.

The short way

Fulcanelli holds, though few admit it, that there are two ways, a short, easy one worked dry and a longer, more thankless one worked wet; chemically, he says, nothing prevents a wet method from being replaced by a dry one. He reads the south portal of Notre-Dame de Paris as showing the shortest.

  • How it is done: The cooking needs only a simple melting pot. On that portal, the subject and its dissolver, the two metallic matters with which the Work begins, are changed by preliminary operations until their roots are opened; united in one compound, they undergo a last purification by fire, from which the pure, unburnable principles rise as a salt pictured as a king born in the fire, and the residue then gives a volatile, easily melted salt.
  • Heat: At the fourth degree of fire, working dry, a heat near 1200 degrees must be kept.
  • Time: The book reports others’ figures, from four days in a melting pot over an open fire to a week, and eight days against nearly eighteen months for the long way, without saying which it accepts.

What it leads to

  • A red crystal or powder fit to turn into gold the common metals, which the book calls sick and imperfect because they rust; and a white form that turns base metals into silver.
  • For its owner, knowledge, fortune and health.
  • A worker who, once successful, turns from worldly vanities to the humble and the suffering, an unnamed and silent follower of nature.

The rules behind the whole work

  • Spirit alone acts. Bodies have no action on one another.
  • The water alone is enough. Before starting the practice, know that the water contains in itself everything the work needs; it is the only matter needed.
  • Death comes before new life. In the melting pot the matter suffers and dies, then rises again purified and already transformed.
  • Dissolve and set. The Work turns the stable into the volatile and the volatile into the stable.
  • Keep to natural simplicity. Use analogy, follow nature as faithfully as you can, and never stray from its simplicity.
  • The science is exact. Fulcanelli holds it as real and exact as optics, geometry or mechanics, and its results as tangible as those of chemistry.

Mistakes and dangers

  • Neglecting the vessel. One old master confessed that, before he knew the vessel, he began the same work over more than two hundred times without success, though with the right matters and method. Do not take literally an old author’s claim that whoever has the matter will find a pot to cook it in.
  • Dissolving ordinary quicksilver. Fulcanelli finds it hazardous, if not a sham, at best a variant of the old way, since the agent stays the same, and sees no gain in dissolving quicksilver with the masters’ dissolver, itself the chief, secret agent. Citing experience, he disagrees with an author who holds that ordinary quicksilver, dissolved without loss of weight, quickly becomes the prepared water.
  • Seeking the result in something stable. On an old author’s advice he calls excellent, the work is to be sought in something volatile, which needs a sealed vessel to keep it from escaping.
  • An uneven fire. Old verses he quotes ask for an equal heat throughout and warn that climbing too high burns.

What Canseliet adds

Canseliet’s prefaces to the first, second and third editions are a later layer, in his voice unless said otherwise.

  • The key is a colour. On Fulcanelli’s assurance, he says, one of the book’s figures gives the key of the great secret, and that key is simply a colour, shown to the worker from the first labour. He names neither figure nor colour.
  • The book came before the gift. When it was written, in 1922, Fulcanelli had not yet received what Canseliet calls the gift of God, though he was so close to the highest enlightenment that he judged it right to wait.
  • The end of the fire. The writer of a letter Fulcanelli kept, its signature scratched out, believed he understood that the fire goes out only when the work is done and the whole colouring mass has soaked into the glass, which shines like the sun.
  • The sign is double. From notes of Fulcanelli’s talks, Canseliet gives his saying that the star-shaped sign is single yet double. Canseliet explains that the sign first seen on the prepared water is a reflection of the other, which shines far more discreetly.
  • The sign is not special to the Work. It appears in many lesser operations, always marking a change in the body it settles on. Of a reported star formed on lead under an acid, he warns that essential facts are missing: ordinary lead needs several preparations before it will swell and change.
  • Light must be separated from darkness. With the help of the hidden fire and the spirit spread through all things, the worker must separate the clear, shining, pure parts of his matter from the dense, dark, coarse ones.
  • A recipe he quotes. Four parts of one matter to nine of another, mixed together over a burning fire into the form of a mineral water, the froth that floats up set aside, the crust thrown away and the kernel purged three times by fire and salt, which is easy, the old author says, under a condition given only in images.
  • Rotting is necessary. Nothing, he says, gives the way of working better than the old saying: dissolve and set. Rotting is a law for every matter, a dark, secret phase that opens astonishing possibilities to the practical work; old masters say its foul smell gives way to a very sweet one.

What the book leaves open

  • What is the raw matter called? Fulcanelli says he cannot speak more plainly without overstepping set bounds. The old masters point to its common name by a near-pun on the word for oak; he adds the hints of the cake and the cat, and never names it plainly.
  • Is the matter iron? Reading a word of Jacques Cœur’s motto in its old spelling, by the rules of his word-play, Fulcanelli obtains iron as the common name of the basic matter that is worked, and concludes that Cœur practised the art, or at least saw the white product made from iron turned into an essence and cooked three times; elsewhere he ties iron to the magnet by word origins. The book does not say whether this basic matter is the raw matter from the mine, and gives arsenic and lead as examples of the matter’s unripe state.
  • What is the hidden fire, where is the night moisture drawn from, and what are the stages of the cooking? All three are withheld, the second on oath; for the first Fulcanelli names three old works and stops.
  • What is the vessel? By its shape the vessel is a glass flask that can be sealed, or in the short way a melting pot; of its nature, the book gives only a few unclear descriptions: it is commonly given the same name as the young agent that first prepares the water, and an old author whom Fulcanelli finds nearest the truth calls it the one unclean body that enters the work, the means of joining the colourings. A riddle it may not fully unveil stands for the vessel: a matter increased by half of another matter or of itself, a chemical combination, not a proportion; its one example, a natural red mineral of arsenic increased by one of its components, is only a supposition. It does not say how these accounts fit together.
  • How many operations? The book speaks of nine successive operations, of seven that open the matter, of seven to nine risings, of three works, of two final products, and of one single operation. It does not reconcile these, nor say whether its two threefold cycles are the same.
  • Which way dissolves gently? The book has the gold dissolve gently while the water is being brought to life, yet says the water is brought to life by the second way, in which it attacks the metal with violence.
  • What heat, and for how long? The book reports heats from body heat to beyond molten lead, and times from four days to eighteen months, without choosing; its own timings, in months, are not tied to either way.
  • What weights? It reports old authors’ ratios, one to two, plural water to singular earth, a body whose weight equals spirit and soul by almost its half, and chooses none of them. It also tells the worker to follow a parable in which ten parts of water are taken, without giving a measure for what is put into them. Its own rule is only that in the old way the proportions must be known exactly.
  • How is the water drawn out and joined to the colouring part? A painted coat of arms, he says, reveals these points, on which all authors kept silent; he describes it without reading it.
  • Why does he reject ordinary quicksilver? As printed, his reason reads as if ordinary quicksilver, lacking its own agent, could become a water useful to the Work, which seems at odds with his disagreement; a negative may be missing, and the book does not settle it.
