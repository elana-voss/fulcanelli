Le Mystère des Cathédrales — An English Reading

Paris

PARIS.1 — The Virgin-Mother, the pillar of Alchemy and the stone alchemist

Fulcanelli opens with the dedication: Notre-Dame of Paris, like most metropolitan basilicas, is placed under the Virgin Mary or Virgin-Mother [PARIS.1 ¶1]. In Sicily such churches are called Matrices, so they are temples of the Mère (Latin mater), the Matrone, a word which by corruption became Madone, “ma Dame”, and so Notre-Dame [PARIS.1 ¶1]. The study of the façade begins with the great central porch, called the porch of the Judgment [PARIS.1 ¶2].

On the trumeau pillar that divides the doorway, among allegories of the medieval sciences, alchemy holds the place of honour: a woman whose brow touches the clouds, enthroned, a sceptre in her left hand, two books in her right, one closed (esotericism), one open (exotericism) [PARIS.1 ¶3]. Against her breast stands the ladder of nine rungs, scala philosophorum, «hiéroglyphe de la patience que doivent posséder ses fidèles, au cours des neufs opérations successives du labeur hermétique» (hieroglyph of the patience her faithful must have, through the nine successive operations of the hermetic labour) (pl. II) [PARIS.1 ¶3]. Valois is quoted in support: «La patience est l’eschelle des Philosophes» (Patience is the ladder of the Philosophers) [PARIS.1 ¶3]. Fulcanelli calls this figure the chapter title of the gothic temple read as a mutus Liber, «le sceau du Grand Oeuvre laïque au front du Grand Oeuvre chrétien» (the seal of the lay Great Work on the brow of the Christian Great Work) [PARIS.1 ¶4].

The cathedral is thus founded on alchemy, which studies the transformations of the original substance, elementary Matière (Latin materea, root mater, mother) [PARIS.1 ¶5]. Stripped of her symbolic veil, the Virgin-Mother «n’est autre chose que la personnification de la substance primitive dont se servit pour réaliser ses desseins, le Principe créateur de tout ce qui est» (is nothing other than the personification of the primitive substance that the creating Principle of all that is used to carry out its designs) [PARIS.1 ¶5]. He reads in this sense the epistle of the mass of the Immaculate Conception, where she speaks as present “before he formed any creature” and “with him” at the founding of the world, and says it plainly concerns «l’essence même des choses» (the very essence of things) [PARIS.1 ¶5; PARIS.1 ¶6; PARIS.1 ¶7]. The Litanies call the Virgin the vase containing the Spirit of things, Vas spirituale; Eteilla is cited on the Magi’s table bearing the book of Thoth and a vase of celestial-astral liquor: «Le secret, le mystère étoit dans le vase» (The secret, the mystery was in the vase) [PARIS.1 ¶7].

Her epithets, he says, betray her positive origin: Palm of Patience, Lily among thorns, Samson’s symbolic Honey, Gideon’s Fleece, Mystic Rose, Gate of Heaven, House of Gold [PARIS.1 ¶8]. Lilium inter spinas is also, a note adds, the title of famous alchemical manuscripts of Agricola and Ticinensis [PARIS.1 n3]. Her title Seat of Wisdom means «le Sujet de la Science hermétique» (the Subject of the hermetic Science) [PARIS.1 ¶8]. Among the planetary metals she is the Moon, which receives the Sun’s rays and keeps them secretly; she dispenses the passive substance which the solar spirit animates [PARIS.1 ¶8]. Mary, Virgin and Mother, stands for form; Elie, the Sun, God the Father, for the vital spirit; their union yields living matter, which is Jesus, «l’esprit incarné, le feu corporifié dans les choses» (the incarnate spirit, the fire made bodily in things) [PARIS.1 ¶8; PARIS.1 ¶9]. Mary is of the stem of Jesse; the Hebrew Jes means fire, sun, divinity, so to be of that stem is to be of the race of the sun, and the name Jesus shows its origin: fire, sun, God [PARIS.1 ¶10]. In the Ave Regina she is called Root (Salve Radix), the principle and beginning of the All [PARIS.1 ¶11].

So hermetic philosophy welcomes the visitor, and the whole cathedral is «une glorification muette, mais imagée, de l’antique science d’Hermès» (a mute but pictured glorification of the ancient science of Hermes); it even keeps one of its artisans, its alchemist [PARIS.1 ¶12]. On the second gallery, at the inner angle of the north tower, among the chimeras, stands a great stone old man: the alchemist of Notre-Dame (pl. III) [PARIS.1 ¶13]. He wears the Phrygian cap, «attribut de l’Adeptat» (attribute of Adeptship), and the light laboratory cape; one hand on the balustrade, the other in his beard, he does not meditate but observes, his posture showing the strongest surprise [PARIS.1 ¶14]. Fulcanelli sees in him the old master who watches the evolution of mineral life and at last beholds the prodigy his faith alone let him glimpse [PARIS.1 ¶15]. A note gives the cap’s sense: it was the distinctive sign of the Initiates, and Pierre Dujols (quoted) writes that at the grade of Epopte in the Mysteries of Eleusis a red cap was put on the candidate who felt the strength to set his hand to the Great Work, a cap called libéria in the Mithraic rites and the supreme mark of Initiation [PARIS.1 n4].

PARIS.2 — The mysterious Fountain and the athanor

The base of the façade under the three porches is wholly given to the science: there one finds the stone name of the sujet des Sages (subject of the Sages) and follows the work of the Elixir from first calcination to final coction [PARIS.2 ¶1; PARIS.2 ¶2]. The figures are read from outside toward the doors, as a worshipper would enter [PARIS.2 ¶3].

On the left buttress of the great portal a small relief shows the alchemist discovering the Fontaine mystérieuse described by the Trévisan in the closing Parable of his book [PARIS.2 ¶4]. The stream of living water bubbles from the hollow old oak; having laid aside the bow and arrows with which, like Cadmus, he pierced the dragon, the Adept watches the spring, whose dissolving power and volatile essence are attested by a bird on the tree (pl. IV) [PARIS.2 ¶5]. Flamel’s Livre des Figures hiéroglyphiques says simply «Note ce chêne» (Note this oak) [PARIS.2 n2]. The question is what this hidden Fountain is, this «puissant dissolvant capable de pénétrer tous les métaux» (powerful solvent able to penetrate all metals), gold above all, which with the dissolved body accomplishes the whole work; most seekers have broken their heads against this wall [PARIS.2 ¶6].

Mythology names it Libéthra, a fountain of Magnesia beside another spring called the Rock; both flowed from a rock shaped like a woman’s breast, so the water seemed to run from two breasts like milk [PARIS.2 ¶7]. Fulcanelli points out that the old authors call the matter of the Work notre Magnésie (our Magnesia) and the liquor drawn from it Lait de la Vierge (Virgin’s Milk) [PARIS.2 ¶7]. The mixing of this primitive water from the Sages’ Chaos with a second water of different nature but the same kind yields a third water «qui ne mouille pas les mains» (which does not wet the hands), called now Mercury, now Sulphur, according as its quality or its physical aspect is meant [PARIS.2 ¶7]. A woodcut in the Azoth, attributed to Basile Valentin but rather by Senior Zadith, shows a crowned siren on the sea jetting two streams of milk from her breasts into the waves [PARIS.2 ¶8]. Arab authors call the fountain Holmat, whose waters gave immortality to the prophet Elie (Ηλι ος, sun), and place it in the Modhallan, whose root means «Mer obscure et ténébreuse» (dark and gloomy Sea), marking the elemental confusion of the Sages’ Chaos or first matter [PARIS.2 ¶9]. A painting at Brixen, described by Misson and noted by Witkowski, gives the religious version: Christ’s blood and the Virgin’s milk fall into one basin whose overflow refreshes the souls of Purgatory, and its Latin inscription (the book’s French gloss) says that blood and milk mingle and become the Fountain of Life [PARIS.2 ¶10; PARIS.2 ¶11; PARIS.2 ¶15; PARIS.2 n6].

Two of the Figures of Abraham the Jew, whose book is said to have belonged to Flamel, bear on the Fountain [PARIS.2 ¶16]. The third shows an enclosed garden with an old hollow oak, a rose-bush with golden leaves and white and red roses climbing it, and at its foot a fountain clear as silver; four blind men dig for it and four search without digging, though it is before them, «excepté un qui la pèse en sa main» (except one who weighs it in his hand) [PARIS.2 ¶17]. That last figure is the subject of the Notre-Dame relief [PARIS.2 ¶18]. The preparation of the solvent is in the fourth: a crowned king dressed in red, soldiers killing the children of two mothers and pouring the blood into a vat where sun and moon come down to bathe, seven innocents dead, one mother in blue and one in red, both weeping [PARIS.2 ¶18; PARIS.2 ¶19]. A figure in Trismosin, almost the same as Abraham’s third, shows the oak crowned with gold at its foot, white birds and one sleeping raven which a poorly dressed man on a ladder is about to seize, while two richly dressed sophists argue without seeing the oak or the Fountain at their feet [PARIS.2 ¶20]. The tradition of the Fountain of Life or Youth was made material in the sacred wells of medieval churches, with healing water: Saint-Germain-des-Prés (after Abbon), Saint-Marcel (after Grégoire de Tours), Notre-Dame de l’Epine, and Notre-Dame de Limoux, whose inscription promises health to whoever drinks with faith [PARIS.2 ¶21; PARIS.2 ¶22; PARIS.2 ¶23]. He will return to this eau pontique (pontic water) [PARIS.2 ¶24].

On the opposite buttress is the cooking of the philosophical compost: an armed knight on a crenellated terrace threatens with a javelin a shape too mutilated to identify (a ray? flames?) [PARIS.2 ¶25]. Behind him a small crenellated building on four pillars, domed, with a flame-shaped mass under its lower vault, is the instrument of the Great Work, the Athanor, «l’occulte four aux deux flammes» (the occult furnace of two flames), potential and virtual (pl. V) [PARIS.2 ¶25]. The two subjects above these are biblical; Fulcanelli declines to read them, to avoid any charge of arbitrary interpretation, and keeps to motifs whose profane character is beyond doubt [PARIS.2 ¶26].

PARIS.3 — Upper row, left: Raven, Mercury, Salamander, Ram, the colours, Philosophy

The hermetic subjects of the base run in two rows on each side of the porch: twelve medallions below, twelve seated figures above, each holding a disc with an emblem of the work [PARIS.3 ¶1]. The first upper figure, on the left, is a woman holding the raven, symbol of the black colour; she stands for Putrefaction (pl. VI) [PARIS.3 ¶2]. In the coction of the philosophical Rebis, the raven expresses the black colour, first sign of the decomposition that follows the perfect mixing of the matters in the Egg, and the philosophers take it as the sure mark of future success [PARIS.3 ¶3]. «Le Corbeau est, en quelque sorte, le sceau canonique de l’Œuvre, comme l’étoile est la signature du sujet initial» (The Raven is, as it were, the canonical seal of the Work, as the star is the signature of the initial subject) [PARIS.3 ¶3].

But the blackness appears several times, which lets authors confuse the order of operations [PARIS.3 ¶4]. Le Breton counts «quatre putréfactions dans l’Œuvre philosophique» (four putrefactions in the philosophical Work): in the first separation, the first conjunction, the second conjunction (of the heavy water with its salt), and the fixation of the sulphur, each bringing blackness [PARIS.3 ¶5]. The old masters mixed the qualities of these four, so that they are hard to tell apart [PARIS.3 ¶6]. The Chevalier Inconnu says that in the second operation the putrefaction called the Tête de Corbeau (Raven’s Head) is very long and is followed by a third multiplication; Fulcanelli notes that this marks two operations divided by the black, which is not the case in the coction [PARIS.3 ¶8; PARIS.3 ¶9]. An anonymous eighteenth-century manuscript gives the signs of the first putrefaction: Morien’s acidity and «odeur de sépulcre» (smell of the grave), Philalèthe’s «yeux de poisson, c’est-à-dire des petites bouteilles sur la superficie» (fish eyes, that is, small bubbles on the surface), a long fermentation made by «notre feu secret, qui est le seul agent qui puisse ouvrir, sublimer et putréfier» (our secret fire, which is the only agent that can open, sublime and putrefy) [PARIS.3 ¶10; PARIS.3 ¶11].

The descriptions of the raven of the coction are the most numerous, since they include the features of the others [PARIS.3 ¶12]. Bernard Trévisan says the compost, soaked with the permanent water, turns like melted pitch, black as charcoal, and is then called «la poix noire, le sel bruslé, le plomb fondu, le laton non net, la Magnésie et le Merle de Jean» (the black pitch, the burnt salt, the molten lead, the unclean latten, the Magnesia and John’s Blackbird); a black cloud flies in the vessel, and this is Raymond’s eclipse of the sun [PARIS.3 ¶14; PARIS.3 n5]. The blackened mass is «la clef, le commencement et le signe de la parfaicte invention de la manière d’oeuvrer du second régime de nostre pierre précieuse» (the key, the beginning and the sign of the perfect discovery of the way of working the second regime of our precious stone), and Hermes says that seeing the blackness one is on the right road [PARIS.3 ¶14]. A note defines Clef as any radical (that is, irreducible) alchemical dissolution, sometimes extended to the solvents that effect it [PARIS.3 n6]. Batsdorff, presumed author of a classic work that others attribute to Gaston de Claves, lists the names of this putrefaction (Occident, Darkness, Eclipse, Leprosy, Raven’s Head, Death, Mortification of Mercury), says it separates pure from impure, and gives as signs a very deep black and a stinking odour which «n’est pas sensible à l’odorat, mais seulement à l’entendement» (is not sensible to the nose, but only to the understanding) [PARIS.3 ¶15].

The second relief shows the philosophic Mercury: a serpent coiled on a golden rod, the symbol Abraham the Jew, also called Eléazar, used in the book that came to Flamel (pl. VII) [PARIS.3 ¶17]. The serpent shows the incisive, dissolving nature of Mercury, which avidly absorbs the metallic sulphur and holds it inseparably; the reptile is «Mercure dans son premier état» (Mercury in its first state) and the golden rod the bodily sulphur added to it [PARIS.3 ¶18]. The resulting homogeneous body keeps the name philosophic Mercury and the image of the caduceus: it is «l’oeuf vitriolé» (the vitriolated egg), which needs only graduated cooking to become first red sulphur, then Elixir, then Universal Medicine; as the Philosophers say, «le Mercure seul suffit» (Mercury alone suffices) [PARIS.3 ¶18].

The third figure, a woman with flame-like hair, is Calcination, pressing to her breast the disc of the Salamander, which lives in fire (pl. VIII) [PARIS.3 ¶19]. The Salamander means the «sel central, incombustible et fixe» (central salt, incombustible and fixed) that keeps its nature even in the ashes of calcined metals, which the Ancients called Semence métallique (metallic Seed) [PARIS.3 ¶19]. That is only the spagyric likeness; the Initiate’s calcination uses no common fire but an occult agent, a secret fire which «ressemble plus à une eau qu’à une flamme» (looks more like a water than a flame) [PARIS.3 ¶20]. This fire or burning water is «l’étincelle vitale communiquée par le Créateur à la matière inerte» (the vital spark communicated by the Creator to inert matter), the spirit shut in things [PARIS.3 ¶20]. Fulcanelli calls it the highest secret of the Work, says it stopped him for more than twenty years, will not reveal it, and refers the reader to Artephius, Pontanus and the Epistola de Igne Philosophorum on this feu aqueux or eau ignée (watery fire, fiery water) [PARIS.3 ¶20]. The Préceptes du Père Abraham say this primitive heavenly water must be drawn from the body where it is, a body expressed «par sept lettres» (by seven letters); it is the universal solvent in which sun and moon bathe and resolve into water [PARIS.3 ¶21]. Limojon de Saint-Didier says the secret fire «n’est pas actuellement chaud, mais il est un esprit igné introduit dans un sujet d’une même nature que la Pierre» (is not actually hot, but is a fiery spirit introduced into a subject of the same nature as the Stone), which, moderately excited by the outer fire, calcines, dissolves and sublimes the Stone and resolves it into dry water [PARIS.3 ¶22].

The fourth figure shows the matter from which this solvent is prepared: a man presents the image of the Ram and holds an object now impossible to identify (pl. IX) [PARIS.3 ¶23]. The man is the hieroglyph of the male metallic principle, which explains Pernety: «Les Adeptes disent qu’ils tirent leur acier du ventre d’Ariès, et ils appellent aussi cet acier leur aimant» (The Adepts say they draw their steel from the belly of Aries, and they also call this steel their magnet) [PARIS.3 ¶23].

The fifth, Evolution, holds the oriflamme of three pennons, the three Colours of the Work (pl. X) [PARIS.3 ¶24]. They follow the fixed order black, white, red; passing hues between them are superficial, while the essential colours are «des colorations dans la masse» (colourings in the mass) marking a change of state in the matter [PARIS.3 ¶25]. These phases became a symbolic language of colours, tied to religion (after Portal) and found again in the stained glass of gothic cathedrals [PARIS.3 ¶26]. Black went to Saturn, lead in spagyric; in Egypt the candidate was told «Souviens-toi qu’Osiris est un dieu noir !» (Remember that Osiris is a black god!); it is the colour of primal Chaos, of heraldic sable, of earth, night and death [PARIS.3 ¶27]. White follows as light follows darkness: the matter is then washed and purified, in brilliant grains of adamantine whiteness, and white is the colour of the Initiates [PARIS.3 ¶28]. Pierre Dujols, quoted at length, derives heureux from the Hebrew hur, heur (to be white) and hurim (the nobles, the whites, the pure), makes bienheureux a synonym of Initiate, links the Cathares to Καθαρος, the Pure, and reads Hebrew schher, a transitional black, as «le profane cherchant l’initiation» (the profane seeking initiation) [PARIS.3 ¶28]. Red, symbol of fire, marks the dominance of spirit over matter; the stone obtained as a red, volatile, fusible crystal or powder becomes fit «à guérir les lépreux, c’est-à-dire à transmuer en or les métaux vulgaires» (to heal the lepers, that is, to transmute common metals into gold) [PARIS.3 ¶29]. Paracelsus gives four spagyric colours, black, white, yellow, red, and says «le noir est la racine et l’origine des autres couleurs» (black is the root and origin of the other colours) [PARIS.3 ¶30]. The Virgin is always in blue (which corresponds to black), God in white, Christ in red, the colours also of the French flag and of the three-storeyed Chaldean ziggurats [PARIS.3 ¶31].

Then Fulcanelli turns from theory to practice [PARIS.3 ¶32]. A legend on a hermetic painting preserved by Etteilla advises: «Ne vous en rapportez point trop à la couleur» (Do not trust too much to the colour) [PARIS.3 ¶33]. The Philosophers speak clearly only to keep the profane away, so their limpid descriptions of coloured régimes must be taken as false [PARIS.3 ¶34]. What matters is how one régime differs from the next, a regime being simply «la manière de faire végéter, d’entretenir et d’accroître la vie que votre pierre a reçue dès sa naissance» (the way of making grow, maintaining and increasing the life your stone received at its birth), which need not show as a sequence of colours [PARIS.3 ¶35]. The Regime of the stone, its coction, contains several others, that is, several repetitions of one way of operating [PARIS.3 ¶35]. By analogy with a man who eats, rests and excretes, the stone needs food, graduated and changed in time, first milk, then meat, with the excrements separated after each digestion [PARIS.3 ¶35; PARIS.3 ¶36]. Tollius is quoted mocking those who look for the black raven, white dove, yellow eagle and red pheasant in their glass vessels, and those who seek the stone in a fixed thing [PARIS.3 ¶36]. He adds that among the Philosophers «l’odeur puante, chez les Philosophes, dénote la fixation ; au contraire, l’odeur agréable marque la volatilité» (the stinking odour, among the Philosophers, denotes fixation; on the contrary, the pleasant odour marks volatility) [PARIS.3 ¶37; PARIS.3 ¶38].

The sixth figure, Philosophy, bears a cross on her disc: the quaternary of the elements and the two metallic principles, sun and moon (the moon now hammered off), or sulphur and mercury, parents of the stone according to Hermes (pl. XI) [PARIS.3 ¶39].

PARIS.4 — Upper row, right: athanor, griffin, oak stave, Lion, cock-fox, Bull

The right-side reliefs are worn by seven centuries of west winds [PARIS.4 ¶1]. The seventh shows a section of the athanor with the inner fittings that hold the philosophic egg; the figure holds a stone (pl. XII) [PARIS.4 ¶2].

The eighth is a griffin, eagle in head and breast, lion in body, teaching the contrary qualities that must be joined in the matter (pl. XIII) [PARIS.4 ¶3]. It is the hieroglyph of the première conjonction (first conjunction), made little by little through the tedious labour the Philosophers call their aigles (eagles), also called Sublimation; by reiterated Eagles the exalted mercury sheds its gross parts and takes up a portion of the fixed body [PARIS.4 ¶3]. «Faire voler l’aigle, selon l’expression hermétique, c’est faire sortir la lumière du tombeau et la porter à la surface» (To make the eagle fly, in the hermetic phrase, is to bring the light out of the tomb and carry it to the surface) [PARIS.4 ¶3]. He reads the fable of Theseus and Ariadne so: Theseus is θεσ-ειος, organised, manifested light, which leaves Ariane, the araignée (spider) at the centre of her web, the empty shell, the cocoon, the butterfly’s husk (Psyché) [PARIS.4 ¶3]. Philalèthe says the preparation of the flying Eagles is the first degree of perfection, that no fewer than seven eagles are needed and up to nine, and that the philosophic Mercury is the bird of Hermes, also called Goose, Swan or Pheasant [PARIS.4 ¶3; PARIS.4 ¶4]. Callimachus describes these sublimations when his swans circle Delos seven times before Apollo is born, a variant of the seven processions round Jericho [PARIS.4 ¶5; PARIS.4 ¶9; PARIS.4 ¶10]. The Sages show the two natures as Eagle and Lion, the lion terrestrial and fixed, the eagle aerial and volatile, who fight until the eagle has lost its wings and the lion its head and they make one body, the Mercure animé (animated Mercury) [PARIS.4 ¶11]. Fulcanelli recalls a modern Paris building whose corner relief shows this combat [PARIS.4 ¶12]. Its note adds a house at Tousson inscribed as built by a Laboureur and named PIERRE BELLE, and explains that alchemy was called Agriculture céleste (celestial Agriculture) and its Adepts Laboureurs (ploughmen) [PARIS.4 n2].

The ninth figure lets us go further into the making of the universal Dissolvent: a woman raises a board like a barrel stave, its wood named by the oak branch on her shield; these are the materials of the hermetic vessel (pl. XIV) [PARIS.4 ¶13]. Her gesture shows the spiritual nature of the substance, «ce feu de nature sans lequel rien ne peut croître ni végéter ici-bas» (this fire of nature without which nothing can grow or vegetate here below), a spirit spread over the globe which the artist must catch as it materialises, in a particular body, an attractive earth, that receives and makes it bodily [PARIS.4 ¶13]. This is the magnet shut in the belly of Aries, to be taken at its birth [PARIS.4 ¶13]. The anonymous Clef du Cabinet Hermétique is quoted: the water is the general Dissolvent of Nature, the bath of King and Queen, the Fountain of the Trévisan; the Cosmopolite calls it steel and magnet, found in Aries at the beginning of spring [PARIS.4 ¶14]. In Flamel’s figure the old hollow oak «marque le tonneau qui est fait du bois de chesne, dans lequel il faut corrompre l’eau» (marks the barrel made of oak wood, in which the water must be corrupted), and the vessel must be of old oak turned round inside like a half-globe; the smoke rising from it in the Douze Clefs marks the fermentation of this water, and the water itself is notre vinaigre mercuriel (our mercurial vinegar) that comes down from heaven to earth and rises from earth to heaven [PARIS.4 ¶14]. Fulcanelli warns that this text must be read with prudence: the spirit gives life, the letter kills [PARIS.4 ¶15].

The tenth figure, the Lion, admits more than one reading [PARIS.4 ¶16]. In the griffin the lion was the fixed base that loses its form, its head, to the volatile [PARIS.4 ¶16]. In general «le Lion est le signe de l’or, tant alchimique que naturel» (the Lion is the sign of gold, both alchemical and natural), but the texts give the same name to the matter that receives the universal Spirit, the secret fire, in the preparation of the solvent; in both cases the sense is power, incorruptibility, perfection, as the knight in mail with raised sword, who represents the king of the alchemical bestiary, shows (pl. XV) [PARIS.4 ¶16]. The first magnetic agent used to prepare the solvent (the solvent some have called Alkaest) is the Lion vert (Green Lion), green not in colour but because it has not reached the adult mineral state: «C’est l’arsenic et le plomb à l’égard de l’argent et de l’or» (It is arsenic and lead in relation to silver and gold) [PARIS.4 ¶17]. Basile Valentin called it green Vitriol; others Emerald of the Philosophers, May Dew, Saturnian Herb, Vegetable Stone; Arnaud de Villeneuve says the water takes the names of all green things to deceive fools [PARIS.4 ¶17]. The Lion rouge (Red Lion) is the same matter brought to the quality of hermetic gold, whence Basile’s advice: «Dissous et nourris le vray Lion du sang du Lion vert» (Dissolve and feed the true Lion with the blood of the Green Lion) [PARIS.4 ¶18]. Which reading is right Fulcanelli cannot say, since the paint is gone: «Le lion de pierre conserve son secret !» (The stone lion keeps its secret!) [PARIS.4 ¶19].

The eleventh, a monster part cock part fox, shows the extraction of the red, incombustible Sulphur, the symbol of Basile Valentin’s third Key, where the cock eats the fox and is in turn devoured by it (pl. XVI) [PARIS.4 ¶19]. The twelfth is the Bull (pl. XVII) [PARIS.4 ¶20]. As a zodiac sign it is the second month of the preparatory operations of the first work and the first regime of the elementary fire in the second; as the animal of the sun (the cow being the moon’s) it figures Sulphur, the male principle, so that bull and cow, sun and moon, sulphur and mercury are the contrary natures avant leur conjonction (before their conjunction) [PARIS.4 ¶21].

PARIS.5 — Lower medallions: the Solution, the two ways, and the rose window

Of the twelve lower medallions, the fifth on the left and the eleventh on the right are too mutilated to read [PARIS.5 ¶1]. The first shows an unhorsed rider clinging to a fiery horse (pl. XVIII) [PARIS.5 ¶2]. It concerns the extraction of the fixed, pure parts by the volatile in the philosophical Dissolution: the rectification of the spirit and its cohobation (repeated pouring back) on the heavy matter [PARIS.5 ¶2]. The horse is the spiritual substance, the rider the heavy metallic body; at each cohobation the horse throws him, until, exhausted, it carries the burden for good, and only so is the occult salt of the Red Lion extracted with the spirit of the Green Lion [PARIS.5 ¶2]. This horse is Pegasus (root πηγη, source), who throws his riders and with a kick makes the Hippocrene spring flow [PARIS.5 ¶2].

The second shows the Initiator with a mirror, the horn of Amalthea and the Tree of Life (pl. XIX): the mirror marks the start, the tree the end, the horn of plenty the result [PARIS.5 ¶3]. The first matter is called Miroir de l’Art (Mirror of the Art), as Moras de Respour, the Cosmopolite and Basile Valentin are quoted to show; this vile, despised subject becomes the Tree of Life, Elixir or philosophical Stone, giving knowledge, fortune and health [PARIS.5 ¶4].

The third, the alchemist unveiling a balance, is the allegory of the poids de nature (weight of nature) (pl. XX) [PARIS.5 ¶5]. The authors say little: Basile bids «bailler un cygne blanc à l’homme double igné» (give a white swan to the double fiery man), Huginus à Barma shows a ratio of one to two, the Cosmopolite says the water’s weight must be plural and the earth’s singular; the Aphorismes Basiliens begin the work with the three principles in a proportion where the body’s weight equals the spirit and soul «presque de sa moitié» (almost by half of it) [PARIS.5 ¶6]. Fulcanelli argues that since philosophic mercury results from a determined quantity of mercury absorbing a certain part of sulphur, the proportions must be known in the ancient way; but common gold may replace the metallic sulphur, and then the excess solvent can be distilled off and weight becomes a matter of consistency [PARIS.5 ¶6]. The balance therefore points to the ancient way, from which common gold (gold not exalted or transfused) seems excluded [PARIS.5 ¶6].

The fourth, a queen kicking over a servant who offers her a cup (pl. XXI), shows a rarely used dissolution of common quicksilver to make the philosophers’ common mercury, which Fulcanelli finds hazardous if not sophistic, at best a variant of the ancient way since the agent stays the same [PARIS.5 ¶7]. He rejects Sabine Stuart de Chevalier’s claim that common mercury is easily dissolved into philosophic water: the servus fugitivus needed is «une eau minérale et métallique, solide, cassante, ayant l’aspect d’une pierre et de liquéfaction très aisée» (a mineral and metallic water, solid, brittle, looking like a stone and very easily melted), and this coagulated water is the Alkaest and universal Dissolvent [PARIS.5 ¶7].

Next, an old man numb with cold, leaning on a stone, his hand in a muff (pl. XXII), is the first phase of the second work: the Rebis in the athanor suffers the dislocation of its parts, the gentle start of the feu de roue (wheel fire), shown as cold and winter, the reign of Saturn, emblem of radical dissolution and of black [PARIS.5 ¶8; PARIS.5 ¶9]. Basile makes Saturn say «Je suis vieil, débile et malade» (I am old, weak and sick); and Fulcanelli mocks Plutarch’s traveller Démétrius, who placed a sleeping, imprisoned Saturn on an island off England, as history written from hermetic fables [PARIS.5 ¶9]. The sixth repeats the second: the Adept prays before Nature, a woman’s bust reflected in a mirror, the sujet des Sages in which «on voit toute la nature à découvert» (one sees all nature laid bare) (pl. XXIII) [PARIS.5 ¶10].

The seventh shows an old man tearing away the veil of the mysterious Palace (pl. XXIV): the first step of practice, the finding of the agent that can réincruder the fixed body, bring it back to a form like its first substance; the alchemists call this réanimer les corporifications, that is, making the dead metals live, and it is Philalèthe’s Entrée au Palais fermé du Roy [PARIS.5 ¶11]. The old man is our Mercury, identified in the texts with Saturn who devoured his children and once painted green; the Palace, once purple inside, is living or philosophical gold, «or vil» (vile gold) hidden in rags; the scene is a variant of the Green and Red Lions, solvent and body to be dissolved [PARIS.5 ¶11]. The hieroglyph of Saturn as solvent is very old: a Louvre sarcophagus shows the god Sèr (Saturn) lying with green flesh [PARIS.5 ¶11]. The eighth shows the meeting of the old man and the crowned king, solvent and body, as in the Trévisan’s parable of the old priest and the king whom the fountain attracts and engulfs [PARIS.5 ¶12]. In this way the gold is dissolved slowly and gently; in the second way the hermetic mercury attacks the metal violently, the “storms” of the Conjunction, figured as eagle and lion (Flamel), cock and fox (Basile), and best of all, Fulcanelli says, in Cyrano Bergerac’s duel of the Rémore and the Salamandre [PARIS.5 ¶12]. Another medallion shows two children fighting, one dropping a pot, the other a stone (pl. XXV): the action of the pontic water on the heavy matter [PARIS.5 ¶13].

This series groups the variable points of the Solution, which alone identifies the way followed: dissolving alchemical gold with the Alkaest marks the first way, dissolving common gold with notre mercure the second, which produces animated mercury [PARIS.5 ¶14]. The twelfth relief shows a second solution, of the Sulphur by the philosophical water: a warrior drops his sword before a tree with a ram at its foot, three ball-shaped fruits and a bird [PARIS.5 ¶15]. The tree is the Cosmopolite’s arbre solaire (solar tree) from which the water is drawn; the warrior is the artist after his labour of Hercules, the ram shows he chose the right season and substance, the bird the volatile nature of the compound; it is also Jason with the golden-fleeced ram and the tree of the Hesperides [PARIS.5 ¶15].

The original paint is lost, but Guillaume de Paris had the medallions copied in the glass of the central rose, which restores their colours: the unhorsed rider is a prince crowned with gold, in white jacket and red stockings; one fighting child green, the other violet-grey; and the window keeps images lost from the façade [PARIS.5 ¶16; PARIS.5 ¶17; PARIS.5 ¶18]. Fulcanelli ends on the paradox of «le flambeau de l’alchimique pensée illuminant le temple de la pensée chrétienne» (the torch of alchemical thought lighting the temple of Christian thought) [PARIS.5 ¶19; PARIS.5 ¶20].

PARIS.6 — North portal: seven metals, the base figures, the zodiac, the Innocents, May Dew

On the tympanum of the Virgin’s portal, the sarcophagus bears seven circles, the seven planetary metals (pl. XXVI) [PARIS.6 ¶1; PARIS.6 ¶2]. The central circle is distinct and the six others repeat two by two outward, a symmetry that matches the Cosmopolite: the virtues of the planets descend, Mars turns into Venus and Jupiter into Mercury, Saturn corresponds to the Moon with the Sun in the middle [PARIS.6 ¶7]. So the centre is the Sun, the ends Saturn and Moon, then Jupiter and Mercury, and Mars and Venus beside the Sun [PARIS.6 ¶8]. The line linking them is four crosses and three croziers, one with a single spiral, two with double volutes, with a gap left on the left, which proves a symbolic intent [PARIS.6 ¶9]. As in spagyric notation, the crosses are the imperfect metals, the double croziers the two perfect ones, the simple crozier mercury, the half-perfect [PARIS.6 ¶10].

On the left base are small figures [PARIS.6 ¶11]. The dog and two doves (pl. XXVII) belong to the animation of exalted mercury: the chien de Corascène of Arthépius and Philalèthe, to be separated from the compost as a black powder, and the Colombes de Diane (Doves of Diana), which hide the spiritualisation and sublimation of the mercury [PARIS.6 ¶12]. The lamb is the sweetening of the arsenical principle of the Matter; the upside-down man (pl. XXVIII) renders solve et coagula, volatilising the fixed and fixing the volatile [PARIS.6 ¶12; PARIS.6 ¶13; PARIS.6 ¶16]. Here too was once the Raven, which legend made the pointer to where Guillaume de Paris hid the philosophers’ stone in a pillar of the nave [PARIS.6 ¶17; PARIS.6 ¶18; PARIS.6 ¶19].

On the jambs the zodiac runs Aries, Taurus, Gemini, the spring months when the work begins [PARIS.6 ¶20]. Then Leo takes the place of Cancer, which is moved to the opposite pillar; Fulcanelli reads this deliberate displacement as the conjunction of the philosophical ferment, the Lion, with the mercurial compound, toward the end of the fourth month of the first Work [PARIS.6 ¶21].

A small square relief shows a child falling from a crucible held by a haloed archangel who seems to strike it, against a starry night sky (pl. XXIX) [PARIS.6 ¶22]. It expresses the condensation of the universal Spirit into the Bain des astres (Bath of the stars) where the chemical sun and moon bathe and are renewed; it is Flamel’s Massacre of the Innocents, simplified [PARIS.6 ¶22]. The universal Spirit, made bodily in minerals as Soufre (Sulphur), is the agent of all metallic tinctures; this Spirit, the red sang (blood) of the children, is had only by decomposing what nature assembled, so the body must die to yield its «âme, vie métallique et Rosée céleste» (soul, metallic life and heavenly Dew), which put into a pure fixed body gives birth to a new creature [PARIS.6 ¶23]. «Les corps n’ont point d’action les uns sur les autres ; l’esprit, seul, est actif et agissant» (Bodies have no action on one another; spirit alone is active) [PARIS.6 ¶23]. Because this mineral blood condenses at night, under a clear sky and still air, most abundantly in spring, the Sages called it Rosée de Mai (May Dew); Thomas Corneille reports that the Rose-Cross masters were called Frères de la Rosée-Cuite (Brothers of the Cooked Dew), which they gave as the meaning of F. R. C. [PARIS.6 ¶24]. May Dew (Maïa was the mother of Hermes), the moisture of Mary’s month, is drawn easily from a particular body, abject and despised, already described; Fulcanelli stops there to keep his oath, calling it the Trévisan’s Verbum dimissum and the Parole perdue (Lost Word) of the medieval freemasons [PARIS.6 ¶25]. Light comes out of darkness, as at the Creation, when the Spirit moving on the waters could not at first be told from them [PARIS.6 ¶26; PARIS.6 ¶27].

PARIS.7 — South portal: the short way and the pillar of Saint Marcel

The south portal of Sainte-Anne has a single motif, which describes the shortest practice of the science [PARIS.7 ¶1]. Grillot de Givry is quoted: the bishop over the aludel teaches where the sacred fire comes from, and the door kept shut all year shows «la voie non vulgaire» (the uncommon way) [PARIS.7 ¶2]. Few alchemists admit deux voies (two ways), a short dry way and a longer wet way; Pernety denies it, Huginus à Barma affirms that each old master had his own process [PARIS.7 ¶3]. The eighteenth-century Encyclopédie says the dry way consists in cooking «le Sel céleste, qui est le mercure des Philosophes, avec un corps métallique terrestre, dans un creuset et à feu nu, pendant quatre jours» (the celestial Salt, which is the mercury of the Philosophers, with an earthly metallic body, in a crucible over a naked fire, for four days) [PARIS.7 ¶4]. The Azoth speaks of twelve hours to learn and eight days to finish; Philalèthe of one week, a way reserved for the poor; Lenglet-Dufresnoy of the double mercure, eight days against eighteen months [PARIS.7 ¶5; PARIS.7 ¶6]. The Sages called this short way the Régime de Saturne; it needs a crucible, not a glass vessel, which could explain the maxim «un seul vaisseau, une seule matière, un seul fourneau» (one vessel, one matter, one furnace) [PARIS.7 ¶7]. Cyliani says there are two matters of one origin, one volatile, one fixed, and two ways, the dry one being done «avec une matière unique» (with a single matter) [PARIS.7 ¶9]. Linthaut and Henckel are cited for the dry way; Henckel reports that the artist Elias showed the stone still stuck to the crucible shards after four days, and wonders whether the alchemists’ “great months” are days, and whether there is a method of simply keeping the matters long at their greatest fluidity by a violent fire fed by bellows [PARIS.7 ¶10].

The emblem occupies the trumeau: a statue of Saint Marcel which in Fulcanelli’s view has no secret meaning, standing on a die with a Byzantine dragon, above a base; only die and base have hermetic value (pl. XXX) [PARIS.7 ¶11]. The pillar has been remade and altered: the restorers set Romanesque scrolls on the base in place of the old besants and flowers, so that «le symbole en est tronqué, la science mutilée, la clef perdue, l’ésotérisme éteint» (the symbol is truncated, the science mutilated, the key lost, the esotericism extinguished) [PARIS.7 ¶12; PARIS.7 ¶13; PARIS.7 ¶14]. The original went to the Cluny museum [PARIS.7 ¶16]. A note says it has since returned to Notre-Dame, to a room of the north tower, but with most of the dragon cut away; it describes the little king, triply crowned, «le fils de ses oeuvres violentes sur la morte adultère» (the son of its violent works upon the dead adulteress), swaddled like the porcelain baby of the Epiphany cake [PARIS.7 n9]. François Cambriel solved this enigma, at least in part [PARIS.7 ¶17].

On the right of the base two solid round besants are the metallic natures, subject and solvent, with which the Work begins; on the front they become rosettes with fused petals, changed by the preliminary operations; on the left they are flowers with a visible calyx, showing that the metallic roots have been opened and are ready to show their seminal principle [PARIS.7 ¶18]. On the die, the united matters undergo sublimation, the last fiery purification, from which the pure principles rise: the sel des philosophes (salt of the philosophers), «le roi couronné de gloire, qui prend naissance dans le feu» (the king crowned with glory, who is born in the fire), as in Hermes’ Rex ab igne veniet [PARIS.7 ¶19]. Only the king’s head shows above the flames, and Esprit Gobineau de Montluisant’s text says he wears a triple crown [PARIS.7 ¶19]. The residue then yields the volatile, fusible sel mercuriel, which old authors called Dragon babylonien [PARIS.7 ¶20]. The dragon emerges from the flames, its tail wrapping the human head, and grips the athanor with its claws [PARIS.7 ¶21]. In the flutings Fulcanelli reads a four-petalled flower for the universal matter, the quaternary of the first elements; below it the two natures whose union gives the Sages’ Saturne, «dénomination anagrammatique de natures» (an anagrammatic name for natures); four flutings for the second elements; and five units of the quintessence (three principles, two natures) totalling ten [PARIS.7 ¶22].

Against Cambriel, who denies that the multiplication of the Sulphur is shown, Fulcanelli points to the bands of touching circles on the dragon’s spine, wings and tail [PARIS.7 ¶23; PARIS.7 ¶24; PARIS.7 ¶25; PARIS.7 ¶26]. Their circles have a centre, the solar sign of gold, so the dragon, image of mercury, covered with the gold sign, can transmute in quantity, but only through later cookings with the Sulphur or philosophic Gold, which are the multiplications [PARIS.7 ¶27]. He agrees with Cambriel in general but not in extending the symbolism to the statue [PARIS.7 ¶28]. The science, he insists, is as exact as optics or geometry and must be cleansed of the frauds of puffers and charlatans [PARIS.7 ¶29]. Cambriel claimed the bishop lays a finger on his lips for silence, and his engraved plate is faked: bearded saint, short crozier, the dragon gnawing his foot [PARIS.7 ¶30; PARIS.7 ¶31]. The real statue is beardless, its mitre plain, its crozier set on the dragon’s mouth, and the saint blesses with index and middle finger raised [PARIS.7 ¶32]. The trumeau shows only the legend of Saint Marcel killing the dragon with his crozier; that it symbolises the life of matter is Cambriel’s opinion, but the gesture of silence «n’est pas et ne fut jamais» (is not and never was) [PARIS.7 ¶35].

PARIS.8 — Flamel’s buildings, the escutcheon of Saint Thomas, the Sainte-Chapelle

The great cathedrals, built by the medieval Frimasons to hand on the hermetic symbols and doctrine, influenced many humbler buildings [PARIS.8 ¶1]. Flamel covered his buildings with emblems: the small portal of Saint-Jacques-la-Boucherie (1389), with an angel holding a black marble round inlaid with a cross of gold thread; two houses for the poor (1407, 1410) with many carved figures; the chapel of the Saint-Gervais hospital; and two arcades at the charnier des Innocents (1389, 1407) [PARIS.8 ¶2]. On the first arcade was an escutcheon which, after Albert Poisson, Flamel imitated from one attributed to saint Thomas d’Aquin, and which appears at the end of Lagneau’s Harmonie Chymique [PARIS.8 ¶2; PARIS.8 ¶3]. Fulcanelli concludes that Lagneau copied it from the arcade, but holds the original escutcheon to be clearer; it was on a window of the chapel of Saint-Thomas-d’Aquin at the Jacobins’ convent until the Revolution [PARIS.8 ¶4]. It was drawn and painted from the window in 1787 by a hermetist named Chaudet, and that drawing is his source (pl. XXXI) [PARIS.8 ¶5]. It shows an upturned gold matras ringed with a crown of thorns; a gold cross with three blue globes and a red heart bearing a green branch, on which silver tears from the matras gather and fix; and quarters with stars and rays, a black earth with golden ears of wheat, a violet cloud with arrows darting toward the abyss, and three silver serpents on green [PARIS.8 ¶6]. This emblem, he says, reveals the extraction of the mercury and its conjunction with the sulphur, points on which all authors kept silent [PARIS.8 ¶7].

The Sainte-Chapelle of Pierre de Montereau (1245–1248) also held a remarkable alchemical ensemble; its original portal with two angels, one with a hand in a vase, the other in a cloud, is gone, but its south windows survive intact [PARIS.8 ¶8]. From the fifth bay Fulcanelli gives one panel, the Massacre of the Innocents, whose meaning he gave above (pl. XXXII), and he urges students to study the windows of the upper chapel and the great rose [PARIS.8 ¶8].
